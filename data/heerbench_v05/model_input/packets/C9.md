# 01 — Strategic Context

**Operation RESOLUTE HERALD** **Product type:** Compiled strategic guidance (national and theater) **Compiled by:** JTF-RH J5 (Plans), from national strategy documents and USEUCOM theater guidance **As of:** 060600ZSEP2027 **References:** NSS (2026); NDS; NMS; Joint Strategic Campaign Plan (JSCP) Russia campaign; USEUCOM Theater Strategy (2026) and Theater Campaign Order (TCO) 27-1; Department of State Integrated Country Strategy (Moldova); Joint Concept for Operating in the Information Environment (JCOIE)

## 1. Purpose and how the guidance nests

This document compiles the strategic guidance bearing on RESOLUTE HERALD and shows how the operation nests under it. The chain is: NSS → NDS / NMS → JSCP Russia campaign → USEUCOM Theater Strategy and TCO 27-1 → this operation. The unifying strategic logic is that the contest for the Republic of Moldova is being fought below the threshold of armed conflict, in the information environment, and that the *manner* of US support — its visibility, attribution, and restraint — is itself a strategic variable because Moldova is a partner without a treaty guarantee.

That last point deserves emphasis at the outset, because it shapes every subordinate decision in this operation. In a treaty-ally contingency, the strategic question is usually how much force to apply and how quickly. Here there is no Article 5 obligation, no automatic coalition, and no domestic political mandate for visible US military action; the partner is a small neutral state that has *chosen* a Western path and can be made to pay for that choice. The strategic value the United States is defending is therefore not territory but the credibility of the proposition that a Western-aligned trajectory, freely chosen, can be sustained against coercion. The instrument best suited to defending that proposition is not force but information, exposure, resilience, and cost imposition — applied in a way that keeps the partner, not the United States, as the visible protagonist. The guidance below repeatedly returns to this idea.

## 2. National Security Strategy (2026) — relevant excerpts

> “Strategic competition with the Russian Federation will be conducted across all domains and below the threshold of armed conflict. We will deny our competitors the ability to achieve their objectives through coercion, subversion, and the manipulation of information directed at our allies, our partners, and our own public, and we will not cede the information space.”
>
> “We will reinforce the sovereignty and resilience of partners outside formal alliance structures who choose a Western-aligned path. The absence of a treaty guarantee makes the manner of our support — its visibility, its attribution, and its restraint — a strategic decision in itself. We will help partners defend the integrity of their own democratic processes; we will not substitute for them.”
>
> “We will manage escalation deliberately with nuclear-armed competitors, accepting that some competitions must be won without crossing thresholds that would convert a contest of will into a contest of arms.”

The NSS thus frames three propositions that bound RESOLUTE HERALD: the information space is contested terrain the United States will not concede; support to non-allied partners is calibrated by visibility and restraint rather than measured only by capability delivered; and competitions with a nuclear-armed Russia must sometimes be won without crossing the armed-conflict threshold. The operational consequence is that “winning” in Moldova means preserving the partner’s sovereign choice and the integrity of its election while remaining below that threshold — not defeating Russian forces, which are not the immediate object of the contest.

## 3. National Defense Strategy — relevant excerpts

> “The Department will treat the information environment as a space of decisive competition. Operations in the information environment — integrating military information support operations, cyberspace operations, electromagnetic spectrum operations, public affairs, and civil-military operations — are a core element of integrated deterrence, not an enabler subordinate to kinetic action.”
>
> “Campaigning below armed conflict requires the Department to compete continuously and to impose costs on a competitor’s decision-making while managing escalation. Commanders will be provided authorities to act at the speed of relevance, balanced against the national interest in avoiding unintended escalation.”
>
> “Where the Joint Force supports a non-allied partner, it will operate in support of that partner’s institutions and in concert with the interagency and multinational partners, ensuring the partner remains the visible principal wherever feasible.”

The NDS does two things that matter directly for this operation. First, it elevates the Information joint function from a supporting enabler to a decisive line of effort — which is why RESOLUTE HERALD is built with OIE as the main effort and the other functions in support, rather than the reverse. Second, it pairs the grant of authorities “at the speed of relevance” with an explicit escalation-management caveat, establishing the central command tension of the operation: the JTF must act fast enough to matter against a fixed election date while keeping decisions that bear on escalation — attribution, disclosure, offensive cyber, visible posture — at or coordinated with the national level.

## 4. National Military Strategy — relevant excerpts

> “The Joint Force will contest adversary efforts to shape the perceptions and decisions of partner populations and leaders, and will plan and resource the Information joint function as a co-equal element of joint operations.”
>
> “Globally integrated operations require theater commands to draw on USCYBERCOM, USSPACECOM, and the Intelligence Community as integral participants, not occasional supporters, in campaigns whose decisive terrain is informational.”

The NMS confirms that this is not a EUCOM-only undertaking. Because the decisive terrain is informational and the adversary’s reach is global (cyber infrastructure, space-enabled ISR, diaspora information spaces in multiple countries), USEUCOM is directed to integrate USCYBERCOM, USSPACECOM, and the IC as integral participants. This is reflected in the supporting-command relationships and in the requirement to coordinate attribution and disclosure nationally.

## 5. JSCP Russia campaign and the regional read

The JSCP Russia campaign directs persistent campaigning to deter Russian aggression and counter malign influence, with priority to frontline and Western-aligned states on the seams of the alliance. The campaign assesses that, with large-scale Russia–Ukraine hostilities frozen under the 18 March 2027 ceasefire, the Russian Federation has reallocated information-confrontation, cyber, and electromagnetic-warfare capacity to secondary lines of effort, of which Moldova is the most active.

The strategic Russian objective in Moldova is assessed to be demonstration: to Moldova, to other frontline states, and to Western publics, that the Western-aligned path is reversible through coercion short of war. This is an important framing for the JTF, because it means the adversary’s true target audience is larger than the Moldovan electorate. A Russian success in Moldova — a flipped or paralyzed coalition achieved cheaply and deniably — would be cited as a template against other small partners and would erode the credibility of Western support broadly. Conversely, a visible Russian failure raises the perceived cost of the method everywhere. The stakes are therefore disproportionate to Moldova’s size, which is precisely why a small, information-led commitment is judged worthwhile.

## 6. USEUCOM Theater Strategy and TCO 27-1 — relevant excerpts

> “USEUCOM campaigns to deter Russian aggression and malign influence across the European theater, with priority to frontline states and Western-aligned partners on the seams of the alliance. The command will protect the information environment of partners targeted by Russian influence operations and be prepared to expose, counter, and impose cost on those operations.”
>
> “The Republic of Moldova is a priority partner. USEUCOM will support the Government of Moldova’s resilience against hybrid coercion — informational, cyber, electromagnetic, and economic — in coordination with the European Union, the OSCE, and Romania, and consistent with Moldova’s constitutional neutrality and sovereign control of activity on its territory.”
>
> “Support to Moldova will be conducted below the threshold of armed conflict and calibrated to avoid providing the Russian Federation a pretext for escalation or a narrative of Western militarization of Moldovan politics. Visible US ground presence will be held in Romania.”

**TCO 27-1 lines of effort relevant to RESOLUTE HERALD:** (LOE 1) deter Russian aggression on the eastern flank; (LOE 3) counter malign influence and protect partner information environments; (LOE 4) build partner resilience and interoperability. RESOLUTE HERALD is the named operation executing LOE 3 with respect to Moldova, supported by LOE 1 (deterrence presence in Romania) and LOE 4 (partner capacity). The theater guidance is unusually explicit about restraint — it names “militarization of Moldovan politics” as a Russian narrative to be avoided and directs that visible US ground presence be held in Romania. This is not risk-aversion for its own sake; it reflects the strategic judgment in paragraph 1 that visible US action could discredit the partner government it is meant to support.

## 7. DIME integration

**Diplomatic.** The Department of State leads US public diplomacy and bilateral engagement through Embassy Chișinău (Ambassador Caroline D. Whitfield) under the Integrated Country Strategy for Moldova, and holds Chief-of-Mission authority over US activity in country. The United States coordinates disclosure and messaging with the EU (EEAS) and the OSCE. The standing tension is between the deterrent and inoculation value of public attribution of Russian activity and the diplomatic cost of being seen to intervene in a partner’s election. Diplomacy is also the instrument that holds the coalition together; divergence among the United States, the EU, the OSCE, and Romania on attribution timing and sanctions sequencing is managed first through diplomatic channels.

**Information.** The main effort. The United States supports the protection of Moldova’s information environment and exposes and counters the Russian influence campaign, integrating MISO, public affairs, cyberspace operations, electromagnetic spectrum operations, and civil-military operations. Wherever feasible the United States acts in support of Moldovan (SIS, the Centre for Strategic Communication and Countering Disinformation, the Central Electoral Commission) and EU (EEAS East StratCom, the EU Partnership Mission in Moldova) institutions so that they remain the visible principals. No information activity is directed at or designed to affect US or allied domestic audiences. The information instrument is where the campaign is won or lost; the other instruments are arranged to enable it.

**Military.** USEUCOM provides the JTF; a deterrence and reassurance presence in Romania (2d Cavalry Regiment); Black Sea maritime domain awareness (NAVEUR/Sixth Fleet); defensive cyberspace support at Government of Moldova invitation (a Cyber National Mission Force hunt-forward team); electromagnetic spectrum support; and space support (PNT resilience, assured commercial SATCOM, space-enabled ISR) via USSPACECOM. Military force is held below the armed-conflict threshold; a force-protection and noncombatant-evacuation contingency for US persons in Moldova is a planning sequel, not the baseline. The military instrument’s most visible component — presence in Romania — functions as much as a strategic-signaling and information capability as a war-fighting one.

**Economic.** The Department of the Treasury (OFAC) prepares targeted financial measures against entities and individuals conducting or financing the influence campaign under the malign-foreign-influence sanctions authority. The United States, the EU, and Romania coordinate an energy-resilience backstop against the engineered electricity and gas shortfalls. The sequencing of sanctions relative to energy support carries escalation signaling and is a national/interagency decision. Economically, the campaign is partly a contest over whether Moldovan citizens experience winter hardship as the cost of Western alignment or as the cost of Russian coercion — a framing contest the energy backstop and its messaging are meant to win.

## 8. Strategic end state and its nesting

The desired strategic end state: Moldova’s sovereign, lawful choice is preserved against coercion; Moldovan and European institutions visibly and credibly lead the defense of Moldova’s information environment; the Russian campaign is exposed and its deniability degraded; Russian decision-makers perceive the costs of escalation as exceeding the benefits; and US activity remained below the armed-conflict threshold throughout. The JTF operational end state (08, 12) nests directly beneath this, translating each strategic condition into an operational objective and line of effort.

## 9. Strategic assumptions

- The 18 March 2027 Russia–Ukraine ceasefire holds through the planning horizon, allowing the Russian Federation to concentrate informational, cyber, electromagnetic, and proxy resources on Moldova. A breakdown of the ceasefire would both divert Russian capacity and change the escalation calculus, and is a key assumption to monitor.
- The Government of Moldova continues to request and consent to US and EU support, but its tolerance for *visible* US/NATO activity on Moldovan soil is bounded by domestic political risk ahead of the 5 December 2027 election.
- The Russian Federation seeks its objectives below the threshold that would produce a unified Western military response and will calibrate activity to remain ambiguous and deniable.
- Escalation control is a national-level interest; decisions on public attribution, intelligence disclosure, offensive cyberspace effects, and visible military signaling are retained at or coordinated with the national level.

## 10. Strategic-level constraints and restraints

- **Constraint:** support is conducted at the request and with the consent of the Government of Moldova, under Chief-of-Mission authority for in-country activity.
- **Constraint:** public attribution and intelligence disclosure are national-level decisions.
- **Restraint:** no information activity directed at or affecting US or allied domestic audiences.
- **Restraint:** no offensive cyberspace effects absent national-level approval; baseline cyber posture is defensive and at partner invitation.
- **Restraint:** no visible US military activity inside Moldovan territory beyond the noncombatant-evacuation sequel; visible ground presence held in Romania.

## 11. Strategic risk

The principal strategic risks are: (a) that US action is perceived as foreign interference in a partner’s election, damaging the very government it is meant to support and validating the Russian narrative of Western militarization; (b) that the United States either over-attributes (and is wrong, or burns intelligence sources) or under-attributes (and cedes the information space) Russian activity; and (c) that a localized provocation in the Transnistrian Security Zone is misread and triggers escalation across the armed-conflict threshold that neither the United States nor the partner seeks.

These risks are interdependent, and recognizing the interdependence is itself a piece of strategic guidance. An aggressive attribution or visibility posture reduces risk (b) — the risk of ceding the information space — but raises risks (a) and (c) by making the United States more visibly the actor and by pressing closer to Russian red lines. A maximally cautious posture inverts the trade. There is no posture that minimizes all three simultaneously; the operation must therefore choose, deliberately and at the right level, where on that trade-space to sit at each phase, and adjust as the election approaches and the adversary’s course of action clarifies. That choice is the substance of the commander’s risk guidance (12) and the reason so many decisions in this operation are reserved at or coordinated with the national level.

# 02 — Regional Intelligence Summary

**Operation RESOLUTE HERALD** **Product type:** Higher-headquarters intelligence summary and JIPOE narrative background **Producer:** USEUCOM J2, with EUCOM Joint Intelligence Operations Center (JIOC-EUR) **As of:** 060400ZSEP2027 **Reporting period:** 01 JUN – 06 SEP 2027 **References:** ATP 7-100.1 (Russian Tactics); DIA Russia military-power assessments; EEAS East StratCom / EUvsDisinfo reporting; OSCE Mission to Moldova reporting; partner-nation (SIS, SRI) liaison

## 1. Bottom line up front

The Russian Federation has shifted its long-running influence effort against the Republic of Moldova from a sustained baseline into a decisive-phase campaign timed to the 5 December 2027 parliamentary election. The campaign — tracked in aggregate as **EMBER LATTICE** — integrates cyber-enabled hack-and-leak, cloned-media and social amplification, electromagnetic operations along the Dniester, energy coercion, and proxy-military signaling from Transnistria, synchronized at the national level to the election calendar. The assessed objective (HIGH confidence) is to reverse or paralyze Moldova’s Western-aligned trajectory below the threshold of armed conflict, achieved through the perceptions and decisions of Moldovan voters, the diaspora, and the governing coalition. Russian intent at the upper end of the escalation ladder (whether Moscow will add a kinetic provocation or an election-day disruption) and the true effect of the campaign on the electorate are the principal intelligence gaps (LOW confidence on both).

What distinguishes EMBER LATTICE from the baseline influence activity Moldova has absorbed for years is not the introduction of new capabilities but their integration and timing. Each line — cyber, information, electromagnetic, economic, proxy — has been observed against Moldova before in isolation. The decisive-phase development is that they are now sequenced together against a fixed date: the gas-debt claim and delivery cuts arrive to bite in winter; the cloned-media surge and bot amplification activate to carry the narrative; the hack-and-leak provides feedstock; the EW injects the message physically into phones; and the proxy “exercises” hold a provocation in reserve. The campaign should therefore be read as a single synchronized operation with a deadline, not as a rise in background noise — which is also why its synchronization is its exploitable weakness.

## 2. Strategic setting

With large-scale Russia–Ukraine hostilities frozen under the **18 March 2027 ceasefire**, the Russian Federation has reallocated information-confrontation, cyber, and electromagnetic-warfare capacity toward secondary lines of effort. Moldova — constitutionally neutral, non-NATO, EU-candidate, with a Russian military presence on its territory (the OGRF in Transnistria), a Russian-owned power plant supplying most of its electricity, a disputed ~US\$680 million Gazprom gas debt, and a labor diaspora of ~1.0–1.2 million split between the EU and Russia — presents a low-cost, high-leverage opportunity for Moscow to demonstrate that the Western-aligned path is reversible. JIOC-EUR assesses (MODERATE confidence) that Moldova is currently the Russian Federation’s most active influence line of effort in the European theater.

The reallocation of capacity matters for warning. Assets and contractor capacity previously committed to the Ukraine information and cyber fight are assessed to have shifted toward the Moldova line, which both raises the ceiling on what Moscow can mount against a small target and means a breakdown of the ceasefire would likely pull that capacity back — making the durability of the 18 March ceasefire a key assumption to monitor (see Strategic Context, 01 §9). Moldova’s value to Moscow is demonstrative rather than intrinsic: a cheap, deniable success here would be advertised as a template against other small partners, which is why the campaign is worth a national-level synchronization effort against so small a state.

## 3. Information-environment baseline

The electorate is reached through segmented channels: Russian-language broadcast and Russian-platform social media penetrate older, rural, Russian-speaking, Gagauz, and Transnistria-adjacent audiences; Romanian-language and EU-platform media dominate among younger, urban, and EU-resident-diaspora audiences. The diaspora has accounted for up to ~15% of the national vote and skews pro-EU among EU residents and pro-opposition among Russia residents. No single channel reaches the whole electorate — a fact the campaign exploits and the defense must accommodate. Baseline Russian-aligned reach is concentrated in a recurring set of broadcast, online, and Telegram properties; the decisive-phase campaign layers cloned-media and amplification on top of this baseline.

Understanding the baseline matters because it sets the realistic objective for both sides. Moscow does not need to convert the whole electorate; it needs to deepen its existing advantage among Russian-language and minority audiences, suppress pro-EU diaspora turnout, and inject enough doubt about the result to paralyze the outcome. The friendly counter does not need to dominate the whole environment either; it needs to hold the contested segments — the diaspora and the persuadable Russian-speaking audience — and protect the credibility of the institutions that will certify the result. The contest is fought at the margins of a closely divided electorate, not across the whole of it.

## 5. The campaign, by line of effort

**5.1 Cyber.** The **“Dosarele Chișinău”** hack-and-leak (02–04 Sep) surfaced a mix of authentic internal documents from the European Path coalition and convincing forgeries, laundered through cloned-media properties. Technical indicators are consistent with the GRU-associated cluster tracked as **BIRCH BLIZZARD** (the 26165 designation; spear-phishing and credential theft, cloud-token abuse) for collection, with the **TUNDRA BLIZZARD** cluster (the 74455 designation; disruptive tooling, OT/ICS interest) probing the CEC’s results-publication contractor and two telecom providers on 28 Aug. A long-dwell espionage presence associated with FSB Center 16 (**GRANITE VESPER**) is assessed in at least one government network. The mixing of authentic and forged material in the leak is itself a technique: it raises the cost of rebuttal, because denying the forgeries risks appearing to deny the authentic documents as well. The TUNDRA BLIZZARD probing of the results contractor is the single most consequential cyber indicator, because it is the precursor that distinguishes a COA 1 information campaign from a COA 3 attack on the result itself; its intent — espionage, disruption pre-positioning, or perception-attack staging — is unresolved and is a priority for the defensive hunt.

**5.2 Information / influence.** Cloned outlets impersonating legitimate Moldovan and European media — among them **“Curierul Național,” “Glasul Basarabean,” “Moldova-24 Live,”** and the cloned EU property **“EuroPost Brussels”** — push two reinforcing narratives, tracked as **“RĂZBOI” (war)** and **“CĂLDURĂ” (warmth)**: that the governing coalition is corrupt and is dragging Moldova into the Ukraine war, and that only accommodation with Moscow restores cheap energy and stability. Amplification is automated (the **MATREȘCA** bot cluster) and laundered through authentic-seeming local voices and Telegram channels (**@MoldovaPravda, @VocePopular_MD, @EnergiaAdevarul**). Production is assessed (MODERATE confidence) to be run by the contractor **Mediakontur** under direction associated with GRU Unit 54777 (72nd Special Service Center). The diaspora is specifically targeted with tailored, host-country-language messaging: turnout-suppression themes for EU-resident voters and voting-access/intimidation themes for Russia-resident voters. A third theme — that the West is interfering in and militarizing Moldovan politics — is pre-positioned to exploit any visible US activity, which is why friendly visibility is itself an intelligence-relevant variable: a visible US misstep would not merely be a diplomatic cost but would feed a narrative the adversary has already loaded.

**5.3 Electromagnetic.** **Leer-3 (RB-341V)** systems operating from the left bank, using Orlan-10 UAVs to emulate cellular base stations, push mass SMS messages into right-bank cellular networks along the Dniester and conduct localized GNSS interference. **R-330Zh Zhitel** (GNSS/GSM/SATCOM jamming) and **Borisoglebsk-2** (multi-band jamming and direction-finding) signatures have been detected intermittently in the corridor opposite Tiraspol, Bender, and Dubăsari. The electromagnetic line is the clearest example of the campaign’s cross-domain fusion: the same system that demonstrates a coercive military reach also delivers the political message directly to a citizen’s phone, bypassing every editorial gatekeeper. Activity is presently calibrated to harass, demonstrate reach, and inject messaging — not to deny communications outright; a shift to broad, sustained communications denial would be a significant escalation indicator and a likely precursor to COA 2.

**5.4 Economic.** The engineered electricity shortfall (MGRES deliveries cut from 03 Sep) and the revived ~US\$680M gas-debt claim are timed to produce winter hardship attributable, in the Russian narrative, to the governing coalition’s Western alignment. Cuciurgan/MGRES supplies ~70–80% of right-bank electricity; the Romanian (ENTSO-E) interconnection backstop is real but capacity-limited and costlier. The effect is designed to peak with heating demand in the final weeks before the vote. The economic line is the campaign’s foundation because it is the one that produces real, felt hardship rather than mediated perception — and hardship that the population experiences directly is far harder to counter than a disinformation narrative. Blunting it requires both keeping the lights on (the backstop) and winning the argument over who turned them off (attribution).

**5.5 Proxy military.** OGRF “readiness exercises” (announced 05 Sep) and PMR mobilization signaling in the Security Zone raise the visible cost of the standoff and create a provocation reserve Moscow can draw on to shift Western and Moldovan attention. No offensive posture has been observed; activity to date is assessed as signaling. Bender/Tighina and the Cobasna depot are the most sensitive potential incident locations. The proxy line’s value is optional and held in reserve: it costs Moscow little to signal, and it can be escalated into a COA 2 provocation if the information campaign appears to be failing or if a dramatic attention-shift is judged useful in the final weeks.

## 6. Integrated assessment

EMBER LATTICE is assessed (HIGH confidence) to be nationally directed and synchronized across lines — narrative themes are aligned with cyber releases, EW activity, and energy actions against the election calendar. The campaign’s coherence is its strength and its vulnerability: it depends on appearing authentic (cloned media, local voices), on remaining deniable (ambiguous attribution), and on synchronization (narrative aligned to action). Exposure of inauthenticity, attribution that strips deniability, and a credible partner-led counter-narrative that reaches the same segmented audiences first are the assessed counters. The analytic implication is that the friendly effort should attack the campaign’s requirements (authenticity, deniability, synchronization) rather than chase its outputs (individual narratives), because the requirements are few and shared across all lines while the outputs are many and continuously regenerated.

## 7. Adversary courses of action (three retained)

**COA 1 — Sustained perception campaign (most likely).** Continue and intensify the information/cyber/EW/economic campaign through election day without a kinetic provocation. *Confidence: MODERATE.*

**COA 2 — Provocation-augmented campaign.** Augment with a staged Security-Zone incident to dominate the narrative and shift attention in the final weeks. *Confidence: MODERATE.*

**COA 3 — Election-day disruption / perception attack (most dangerous).** Add a cyber-enabled disruption to CEC results infrastructure or a fabricated “results” perception attack. *Confidence: LOW — limited reporting, high consequence.*

The three COAs are not mutually exclusive and are best read as an escalation ladder Moscow can climb if the most-likely course appears to be failing. COA 1 is the default and the cheapest; COA 2 is an attention-shifting escalation available if the information campaign stalls or if a dramatic event is judged useful; COA 3 is the most dangerous because it attacks the legitimacy of the result directly and could manufacture a crisis regardless of how Moldovans actually vote. The decisive analytic task is therefore not to pick the “right” COA now but to identify, for each, the earliest distinguishing indicator, so the force can recognize a climb up the ladder in time to respond. Those distinguishing indicators are consolidated below.

## 8. Key intelligence gaps

- **Russian decision-making:** outcome thresholds (electoral margin, coalition composition) that would cause Moscow to escalate to COA 2/3, and conditions for de-escalation. (LOW)

- **Campaign effect:** the true effect of EMBER LATTICE on the electorate and diaspora, as distinct from its volume. (LOW)

- **Attribution confidence** for public disclosure on the cyber and cloned-media lines, weighed against intelligence gain/loss. (Varies by line.)

- **OGRF/PMR intent** behind the “readiness exercises.” (LOW)

- **CEC results-chain integrity.** (MODERATE, improving with hunt-forward)

These gaps share a common character that the staff should keep in view: the two that matter most — Russian decision-making and the true effect on the electorate — are the two least amenable to collection, because both concern intent and cognition rather than observable activity. The collection plan (13) does not promise to close them; it promises to surround them with the best available proxies and to be explicit about the residual uncertainty. An assessment that claimed high confidence on Moscow’s escalation thresholds or on the campaign’s electoral effect would be overstating what the available collection can support.

## 9. Warning — what we are watching next

The highest-priority warning indicators over the next 30 days are: any deepening of TUNDRA BLIZZARD access into the CEC results chain (COA 3 precursor); OGRF/PMR activity beyond exercise norms near Bender or Cobasna (COA 2 precursor); and the appearance of pre-staged “results” or “attack” content (precursor to either). These map directly to PIR 1 and PIR 4 and are the indicators most likely to give early warning of a climb from the most-likely course toward the more dangerous ones.

## 10. Coordination and confidence variances

This summary is coordinated with the Intelligence Community, the EU Intelligence and Situation Centre (INTCEN) and EEAS East StratCom, the OSCE Mission to Moldova, and Romanian intelligence (SRI). Attribution confidence varies among partners: EEAS East StratCom assesses the cloned-media network with higher confidence (drawn from open-source and platform reporting), while technical cyber attribution is held more tightly by national agencies. These differences shape what each partner is willing to say publicly and when — a planning consideration carried into the attribution/disclosure decision points (13, PIR 3). The variance is not a defect to be resolved before action; it is a structural feature of a coalition in which different members hold different evidence under different disclosure rules, and the operation must be able to act coherently despite it.

# 03 — Adversary Order of Battle

**Operation RESOLUTE HERALD** **Product type:** Adversary order of battle and capability assessment **Producer:** JTF-RH J2, with JIOC-EUR; corroborated by DIA and partner (SIS, SRI) liaison **As of:** 060500ZSEP2027 **References:** ATP 7-100.1 (Russian Tactics); DIA Russia Military Power; open-source EW/cyber reporting (EEAS, OSINT); OSCE Mission reporting

Because the adversary’s main effort is informational, the information-confrontation, cyber, and electromagnetic order of battle (paragraphs 5–7) is presented with the same weight as conventional and proxy ground forces (paragraphs 3–4), and the adversary decision and coordination architecture (paragraph 10) is treated as part of the order of battle.

## 1. Adversary overview

Each of these features is also a vector the Russian campaign exploits — poverty makes energy coercion bite, the ethnic mix supplies grievance, neutrality denies an Article 5 backstop, and the diaspora is both a decisive electoral bloc and a dispersed, hard-to-defend audience. The country study below treats each feature as both a fact and a vulnerability.

## 2. Political environment and the election

The **5 December 2027 parliamentary election** is the central event. Mechanics that matter for planning:

- **Electoral system:** proportional; 5% threshold for parties, 7% for blocs; 101 seats.
- **Governing coalition margin:** narrow (~54 seats). A swing of a few seats changes the government.
- **Diaspora vote:** historically decisive and skewed: EU-resident voters lean strongly pro-EU; Russia-resident voters lean pro-opposition. Diaspora turnout and the number/placement of overseas polling stations are recurring points of contention. The diaspora has accounted for up to ~15% of the total vote in recent national elections.
- **Minority regions:** Russian-speaking, Gagauz, and Transnistria-adjacent districts lean pro-opposition; turnout management there is contested.
- **Principal opposition:** the **Patria-Renaștere (Homeland-Revival) Bloc**, led by **Grigore Tabără**, advocating “balanced” relations with Moscow; assessed to receive organizational and financial support through networks tied to fugitive financier **Vadim Ostrovschi**.
- **Integrity concerns:** vote-buying, illicit campaign financing, and organized “electoral tourism” (bussing voters across districts/borders) are persistent issues flagged by the Central Electoral Commission and by OSCE/ODIHR observers.

The political math is what makes the campaign worth Moscow’s investment. Because the governing coalition’s majority is narrow and the electorate is closely divided, the Russian campaign does not need to persuade a majority — it needs only to move a few percentage points, suppress pro-EU diaspora turnout, energize the pro-opposition base, and add enough doubt about the result’s legitimacy to paralyze whatever coalition emerges. The diaspora is the single most leveraged variable: it is large, decisive, geographically dispersed across many legal jurisdictions, and reachable through entirely different information channels depending on whether voters reside in the EU or in Russia. A counter-effort that addresses only the in-country electorate would miss the part of the electorate the adversary is working hardest.

**Key Moldovan institutions (the supported principals):**

- **Information and Security Service (SIS)** — Director **Dorin Ababii**; lead for counterintelligence and the cyber/hybrid defense partnership.
- **Centre for Strategic Communication and Countering Disinformation (StratCom Center)** — head **Natalia Cebotari**; the lead institution for narrative defense and the natural front for exposure activity.
- **Central Electoral Commission (CEC)** — chair **Vasile Munteanu**; owns the results-collection and -publication chain.

These institutions are the supported principals of the operation, and their credibility is the friendly center of gravity. They are also the institutions the Russian campaign most wants to discredit — a “rigged election run by a captured CEC” narrative would convert any close result into a legitimacy crisis. Reinforcing their visible credibility is therefore simultaneously a protection task and an information task.

## 3. Transnistria (Pridnestrovian region)

A breakaway region on the left bank of the Dniester, internationally unrecognized, de facto governed from **Tiraspol** under **Vyacheslav Pavlenko**, economically and militarily dependent on the Russian Federation. Salient facts:

- Hosts the **Operational Group of Russian Forces (OGRF)** (~1,500) guarding the **Cobasna** ammunition depot (~20,000 tons of legacy munitions), and the Pridnestrovian (PMR) military (~5,000–7,500 active).
- Hosts the **Cuciurgan/MGRES** power plant (Russian-owned), which supplies the majority of right-bank electricity.
- The **5+2** settlement format (sides Moldova and Transnistria; mediators OSCE, Russia, Ukraine; observers EU and US) is the standing political mechanism, currently dormant.
- Economically intertwined with right-bank Moldova: much PMR industry depends on EU-market access secured through Moldovan paperwork, and the right bank depends on Cuciurgan power.

Transnistria is best understood as a frozen conflict that gives Moscow a permanent set of levers without the cost of an active one. The region supplies a forward military presence and provocation reserve (the OGRF and PMR forces), the principal energy lever (Cuciurgan), and a standing legitimacy dispute the 5+2 format keeps alive but unresolved. The mutual economic dependence between the banks cuts both ways: it gives Chișinău some leverage but also means a coercive squeeze on Transnistria rebounds on the right bank, which constrains Moldovan options and is itself material the Russian narrative exploits. For RESOLUTE HERALD, the most important Transnistrian fact is geographic-informational: the contact line places Russian electromagnetic systems within reach of right-bank cellular networks and places a credible incident-staging area (Bender, Cobasna) within an hour of the capital.

## 4. Gagauzia

Autonomous territorial unit in the south (capital **Comrat**), Turkic-speaking and Orthodox, ~135,000 population, historically pro-Moscow in orientation. Bashkan (Governor) **Irina Cazacu**. Gagauz autonomy gives Moscow a lawful platform for influence and a recurring source of center–region friction — over budget transfers, language policy, and foreign-policy orientation — that the campaign amplifies. Gagauzia is not assessed as a Russian-directed actor, and treating it as one would be both inaccurate and counterproductive; its grievances are genuine and locally rooted. But those grievances are a reliable amplification surface, and the campaign’s “the West ignores and disrespects you” theme is tailored for Gagauz and other minority audiences. The operation’s approach to such audiences is therefore reinforcement of legitimate institutions and grievance-acknowledgment by Moldovan voices, not foreign messaging that would confirm the interference narrative.

## 5. Energy

Right-bank Moldova’s electricity supply depends heavily on the **Cuciurgan/MGRES** plant (installed capacity ~2,520 MW; supplies on the order of 70–80% of right-bank demand under contract). Gas supply runs through the **Moldovagaz** relationship with Gazprom, encumbered by a **disputed legacy debt (~US\$680 million)** that Moscow periodically revives as leverage. Interconnection with the Romanian (ENTSO-E) grid exists and has expanded, but remains capacity-limited and costlier than Cuciurgan supply.

Energy is the cleanest illustration of how the campaign fuses instruments. The same lever produces a material effect (cold homes, higher bills, industrial disruption) and an informational effect (a grievance that can be attributed to the governing coalition’s Western alignment), timed so that the material effect peaks with winter heating demand in the final weeks before the vote. This fusion is why the energy response cannot be purely economic: a backstop that keeps the lights on but loses the argument about *who caused the shortfall* would blunt only half the attack. The operation’s energy line therefore pairs material resilience (the Romanian interconnection backstop, EU support) with attribution of the coercion, and treats the two as a single effect.

## 6. Information and telecommunications environment

- **Media:** a fragmented market with several oligarch-linked broadcasters, a public broadcaster (Teleradio-Moldova), and a vigorous online/Telegram ecosystem. Russian-language broadcast and Russian-platform social media retain wide reach, especially among older, rural, Russian-speaking, and minority audiences. Romanian-language and EU-platform media dominate among younger, urban, and EU-diaspora audiences.
- **Reach segmentation (assessed):** the audience splits roughly by language (Romanian vs Russian), age (under-40 EU-platform vs over-50 TV/Telegram), geography (urban Romanian-leaning vs rural/minority Russian-leaning), and platform. No single channel reaches the whole electorate; the campaign and the defense must both be multi-channel and multilingual.
- **Telecommunications:** high mobile penetration; three principal mobile operators; right-bank cellular networks in the Dniester corridor are physically reachable by left-bank electromagnetic systems. Internet connectivity is comparatively open with multiple international links.
- **Electoral information chain:** the CEC’s results-collection and -publication infrastructure is the single highest-value information node — the target of a potential election-day disruption or “false results” perception attack, and the priority for defensive cyberspace support.

The segmentation of the information environment is the defining operational feature of the country. It means there is no single audience, no single channel, and no single language in which the contest is decided; there are at least five distinct audience segments (4–5 in-country and diaspora groupings), each reached differently and each subject to different constraints on what the United States and its partners may lawfully do. It also means the adversary’s apparent advantage — strong legacy reach into Russian-language and older rural audiences — is matched by a friendly advantage among younger, urban, and EU-diaspora audiences, provided the counter-effort actually reaches them in the right language on the right platform and before the adversary’s framing sets.

## 7. US and foreign nationals

The US Embassy in Chișinău (**Ambassador Caroline D. Whitfield**) leads the country team. An estimated **several hundred US citizens** reside in or near Chișinău: embassy personnel and dependents, NGO and business community, and dual nationals. Under baseline conditions these persons are not at risk and require no protective action. Should the Transnistrian Security-Zone situation deteriorate — a contingency, not the baseline — protection of US persons and a noncombatant-evacuation option become a planning requirement, led by the Department of State with military support, with the overland route to Romania via the Prut crossings as the primary egress. The proximity of a credible incident-staging area to the capital is what keeps this contingency on the planning board even though the baseline threat to US persons is low.

## 8. Partner posture toward US support

The Government of Moldova requests and consents to US and EU assistance against the hybrid campaign, but is acutely sensitive to the domestic-political risk of *visible* US or NATO activity on Moldovan soil before the election — exactly the activity the Russian narrative is primed to exploit as proof of Western interference and militarization. The partner’s strong preference is that Moldovan and EU institutions remain the visible face of the response, with US support enabling from behind wherever feasible. Specific partner sensitivities: visible US uniformed presence in Chișinău; any US-branded public messaging to Moldovan domestic audiences; and the optics of foreign personnel inside electoral institutions.

This posture is not an obstacle to be worked around; it is a strategic asset to be protected. The partner government’s credibility with its own electorate is the friendly center of gravity, and that credibility depends precisely on the government not appearing to be a foreign instrument. The operation’s heavy reliance on partner-fronted activity, its prohibition on US-branded domestic messaging, and its placement of visible military presence in Romania all flow from this single consideration. Partner consent is also dynamic — it can narrow as the election nears and the interference narrative intensifies — which is why it is tracked as a friendly-force information requirement (13, FFIR 1) rather than assumed.

## C9:D06 — Critical Geography

# 06 — Critical Geography

**Operation RESOLUTE HERALD** **Product type:** Geographic and infrastructure study (physical, infrastructural, and information terrain) **Producer:** JTF-RH J2 (Geospatial) / J5 **As of:** 060600ZSEP2027

For an operation whose decisive terrain is cognitive, the information terrain (paragraph 4) is studied with the same rigor as the physical, and the electromagnetic seam where the two meet (paragraph 2.3) is treated as key terrain. The organizing idea of this study is that Moldova’s physical geography matters chiefly for what it does to the *information and infrastructure* fight: it places Russian electromagnetic systems within reach of right-bank populations, it concentrates the country’s electricity supply in a Russian-owned plant on the wrong side of a contact line, and it puts a credible incident-staging area an hour from the capital. Terrain analysis here is therefore in service of the information and protection lines, not of a maneuver fight that the operation does not intend to have.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. Physical geography

Moldova is a small (~33,800 km²), landlocked state between Romania (west, across the **Prut River**) and Ukraine (north, east, and south). Terrain is rolling agricultural plain and low hills (Codru uplands in the center); no significant natural barriers other than the two rivers. Greatest extent is ~350 km north–south and ~150 km east–west. The **Dniester (Nistru) River** runs broadly north–south through the east and forms the de facto line between government-controlled right-bank Moldova and the Transnistrian left bank. Moldova has no seacoast; the nearest maritime access and the principal Black Sea military geography lie through **Romania (Constanța)** and the Ukrainian coast near Odesa, ~150–200 km from Chișinău.

The operational consequence of this geography is that Moldova has strategic depth in neither the physical nor the access sense. It cannot be reinforced or evacuated except overland through Romania (or by a small air node at Chișinău), it has no coast through which to receive support, and its populated core sits within tens of kilometers of a contact line backed by Russian forces. None of this matters for a maneuver campaign the operation will not fight — but all of it matters for the two contingencies that could turn this from an information operation into something harder: a Security-Zone provocation and a noncombatant evacuation.

Distances relevant to planning: - Chișinău to the Romanian border (Prut, nearest crossing at Leușeni/Albița): ~50 km. - Chișinău to Bender/Tighina (Security Zone): ~60 km. - Chișinău to Mihail Kogălniceanu Air Base (Romania, near Constanța): ~330 km by road via Albița. - Cuciurgan (MGRES) and Cobasna sit on the left bank near the Ukrainian border, ~90–120 km east of Chișinău.

## 2. The Dniester and the Security Zone

### 2.1 Configuration

A demilitarized **Security Zone** along the Dniester, established by the 21 July 1992 ceasefire, separates Moldovan and Pridnestrovian forces. It is monitored by a trilateral **Joint Control Commission (JCC)** with Moldovan, Russian, and Pridnestrovian peacekeeping contingents and OSCE observation. The Zone runs the length of the contact line and includes several crossing points and bridges over the Dniester. Its trilateral monitoring arrangement means that any incident within it is observed and contested in real time by all three parties — which is precisely what makes it useful to an adversary seeking a deniable, narratively ambiguous provocation.

### 2.2 Bender/Tighina

**Bender (Tighina)** sits on the **right (west) bank** but is under Transnistrian control — an anomaly that makes it the single most sensitive friction point in the Zone. Because it places Transnistrian-controlled territory on the same bank as the government-controlled core, it is the most plausible location for a staged or accidental incident that could be framed either as Moldovan aggression against Transnistria or as a threat to the Russian peacekeeping presence. It is the geographic trigger reference for the force-protection / noncombatant-evacuation sequel (Annex F).

### 2.3 The electromagnetic seam

Right-bank cellular networks in the corridor within roughly 10–20 km of the Dniester are physically reachable by left-bank electromagnetic systems (Leer-3 cellular emulation and SMS injection; R-330Zh Zhitel GNSS/GSM interference). This corridor — running through populated right-bank districts opposite Tiraspol, Bender, and Dubăsari — is where the electromagnetic and information lines fuse and is treated as key terrain for the EMSO effort. The significance is that the adversary does not need to cross the river to reach right-bank citizens’ phones; geography hands Russia a standing capability to inject messaging and degrade GNSS into the corridor from its own side of the line, with deniability intact. Friendly EMSO is accordingly oriented less on denying that capability outright (which would risk cross-border effects) than on characterizing it, geolocating it for attribution, and building partner resilience around it.

## 3. Critical infrastructure

| Node | Location | Significance |
|:---|----|----|
| **Cuciurgan / MGRES power plant** | Left bank, near Ukrainian border | Russian-owned thermal plant (~2,520 MW installed); supplies ~70–80% of right-bank electricity; both civilian lifeline and economic-coercion lever |
| **Cobasna ammunition depot** | Left bank, near Ukrainian border | ~20,000 tons legacy munitions; guarded by OGRF; standing safety/information hazard and credible provocation vector |

The single most consequential feature of Moldova’s infrastructure is that its electricity supply is concentrated in a Russian-owned plant on territory the government does not control. That one fact converts a routine commercial dependency into a strategic vulnerability and an information weapon, and it cannot be remedied inside the planning horizon — the Romanian interconnection backstop reduces but does not eliminate the dependence. The CEC results-publication chain is the other infrastructural center of gravity, but of a different kind: it is defensible inside the horizon (hardening, hunt-forward, contingency rehearsal), which is why it is the first protection priority even though Cuciurgan is the larger vulnerability.

## 4. Information terrain

- **Segmentation.** The information environment is segmented by language (Romanian vs Russian), age (under-40 EU-platform vs over-50 TV/Telegram), geography (urban vs rural), and platform. Russian-language broadcast and Russian-platform social media retain strong penetration among older, rural, Russian-speaking, and Gagauz/Transnistria-adjacent audiences; Romanian-language and EU-platform media dominate among younger, urban, and EU-diaspora audiences.
- **Diaspora geography.** The decisive “constituency” is partly outside Moldova: EU-resident voters (Italy, Germany, France, Romania) reached through EU platforms and host-country media, and Russia-resident voters reached through Russian platforms and subject to Russian information control. Each segment sits under a different legal regime for US/partner activity.
- **Cellular reach across the Dniester.** As in 2.3, the right-bank corridor along the river is reachable by left-bank EW — the physical basis for SMS-injection influence.
- **Electoral information chain.** The CEC’s results-collection and -publication infrastructure is the highest-value node for a COA 3 (election-day disruption / perception attack) and the priority for defensive cyberspace support.

The information terrain should be read as the true decisive terrain of the operation, mapped with the same discipline a maneuver planner would apply to avenues of approach and key terrain. Its “avenues” are the language-platform-demographic segments through which messaging flows; its “key terrain” is the CEC results chain and the credibility of partner institutions; its “restricted terrain” is the set of audiences (US/allied domestic; Russia-resident under Russian control) where friendly action is legally or practically constrained; and its “seams” are the diaspora populations that span jurisdictions. A counter-effort built without this mapping would mass in the wrong place — typically the easily-reached urban Romanian-language audience that is already favorably disposed — and miss the contested segments where the election is actually decided.

## 5. Staging and access geography (friendly)

- **Mihail Kogălniceanu Air Base (Romania)**, near Constanța: JTF forward headquarters and principal staging base; established US/NATO presence; air and sea access via Constanța.
- **Cincu and other Romanian training areas:** locations for the 2d Cavalry Regiment deterrence presence, held in Romania and not employed inside Moldova.
- **Prut River crossings (Romania–Moldova):** the overland line of communication for any surge or noncombatant-evacuation support; principal crossing at Leușeni/Albița; throughput and political sensitivity both matter.
- **Black Sea / Constanța:** NAVEUR/Sixth Fleet maritime domain awareness; the maritime seam to the Ukrainian coast and the broader Russian Black Sea posture.

Friendly access geography reinforces the decision to hold visible military presence in Romania. Romania provides depth, basing, and a NATO legal framework that Moldova cannot; conducting the visible elements of the operation from Romanian soil keeps them lawful, sustainable, and — critically — out of the Moldovan domestic frame. The Prut crossings are the operation’s umbilical for any contingency that requires moving people or materiel into or out of Moldova, and their finite throughput and political sensitivity make crossing capacity an explicit planning factor for the NEO sequel.

## 6. Climatology and time

The election falls on **5 December 2027 — early winter**. Mean December temperatures in Chișinău run near or below freezing; heating demand peaks just as the engineered electricity shortfalls bite, maximizing the salience of the energy-coercion narrative in the final weeks of the campaign. Reduced daylight, snow and ice, and any heating disruption compound the social and information effects and would also complicate a winter noncombatant evacuation if the sequel triggers (road conditions on the Prut egress route; reception capacity in Romania).

Climatology is not a neutral backdrop in this operation; it is an enabler the adversary has deliberately timed against. By reviving the gas-debt claim in July and cutting deliveries in September, Moscow arranged for the material hardship of the energy lever to peak with winter demand in the final weeks before the vote — converting weather into a campaign instrument. The fixed election date and the onset of winter together set the binding planning timeline: the decisive phase runs through the coldest, darkest weeks of the year, when the energy narrative is most potent, GNSS and cellular degradation are most disruptive to daily life, and any evacuation would be hardest.

## Step 1 — Define the operational environment

- **Operational area:** the Republic of Moldova (right bank, government-controlled; Transnistrian left bank, not government-controlled); Gagauzia; the Dniester Security Zone; the Romanian seam (staging); the NW Black Sea; and the trans-national information environment reaching the Moldovan diaspora in the EU and Russia.

The unusual feature of the operational area is that it extends well beyond Moldova’s borders into the diaspora information spaces of several EU states and the Russian Federation, each governed by a different legal regime for friendly activity. The “battlespace” for the decisive line of effort is therefore partly extraterritorial and legally heterogeneous — a fact that shapes both the collection problem (PIR 5 must reach audiences abroad) and the authorities problem (different lines fall under different titles depending on where the audience sits).

## Step 2 — Describe environmental effects

**Information environment.** Segmented by language (Romanian vs Russian), age, geography (urban vs rural), and platform (EU platforms/Telegram vs Russian platforms). Russian-language/Russian-platform reach is strong among older, rural, Russian-speaking, Gagauz, and Transnistria-adjacent audiences; EU-platform/Romanian-language reach dominates among younger, urban, and EU-diaspora audiences. *Effect:* neither campaign nor defense can reach the whole electorate through one channel; both must be multi-channel and multilingual, and the diaspora must be reached through host-country-specific means and legal regimes. **Cyberspace.** Comparatively open connectivity with multiple international links; the CEC results-publication chain and the telecom providers are the highest-value nodes; Moldovan defensive capacity (SIS, EUPM) is improving but limited. *Effect:* a defensible election depends on hardening a small number of high-value nodes against the TUNDRA BLIZZARD probing already observed — a tractable problem inside the planning horizon, unlike the broader information contest.

**Electromagnetic spectrum.** Right-bank cellular and GNSS along the Dniester corridor are reachable and contested by left-bank EW (Leer-3, Zhitel, Borisoglebsk-2). *Effect:* the EMS is where the electromagnetic and information lines fuse (Leer-3 SMS injection); civil messaging integrity and GNSS reliability in the corridor are at risk and require partner-resilience measures rather than counter-fires that would risk cross-border effects.

**Land / maritime.** Proxy ground forces (OGRF ~1,500; PMR ~5,000–7,500) in Transnistria and the Black Sea maritime environment are supporting; their principal effect is signaling, a provocation reserve (COA 2), and attention-shifting. *Effect:* the Security Zone (esp. Bender/Tighina, Cobasna) is the most likely incident terrain and the trigger reference for the NEO sequel.

The campaign’s components are therefore best understood as inputs to a perception, not as ends in themselves.

This is what allows a modestly resourced friendly force to contest a larger adversary campaign — by attacking shared requirements rather than chasing individual narratives.

## Step 4 — Determine adversary courses of action

**COA 1 — Sustained perception campaign (MOST LIKELY).** - *Depiction:* continue/intensify information/cyber/EW/economic pressure through election day without a kinetic provocation; rely on energy hardship and narrative saturation to flip the vote. - *Timeline:* steady escalation of cloned-media tempo and energy effects to a peak in the final two weeks. - *Indicators:* continued Mediakontur/MATREȘCA tempo; no OGRF posture change beyond signaling; energy shortfalls tracking the campaign calendar; rising diaspora-targeted messaging. - *Confidence:* MODERATE.

**COA 2 — Provocation-augmented campaign.** - *Depiction:* augment with a staged Security-Zone incident (fabricated “attack” on Transnistria/Cobasna blamed on Moldova/the West, or a manufactured incident against OGRF personnel) to dominate the narrative and shift attention. - *Timeline:* most likely in the T-21 to T-7 window to maximize disruption while preserving deniability. - *Indicators:* OGRF/PMR live-fire or mobilization beyond exercise norms; pre-positioned media blaming Moldova; PMR family evacuation signaling; movement near Bender/Tighina. - *Confidence:* MODERATE.

**COA 3 — Election-day disruption / perception attack (MOST DANGEROUS).** - *Depiction:* add a cyber-enabled disruption to CEC results infrastructure or a fabricated “results” perception attack (false announcements, cloned CEC properties) to delegitimize the outcome regardless of the count. - *Timeline:* election day (5 Dec) through the results-publication window (D to D+3). - *Indicators:* deeper TUNDRA BLIZZARD intrusion into CEC results systems; pre-staged “official results”; coordinated fraud claims prepared in advance; cloned CEC domains. - *Confidence:* LOW (limited reporting; high consequence).

The three COAs form an escalation ladder rather than a set of independent options. Moscow’s default is COA 1, the cheapest and most deniable; it can climb to COA 2 if the information campaign appears to be failing or if an attention-shift is judged useful, and to COA 3 if it judges that the result itself must be delegitimized. Because the courses are not mutually exclusive and Moscow can shift among them late, the decisive analytic product is not a single predicted COA but the set of *distinguishing indicators* — the earliest observable that signals a climb up the ladder — which are consolidated for collection in PIR 1 and PIR 4. The most dangerous course (COA 3) is also the most defensible against, because it depends on a small number of cyber accesses to the CEC chain that the friendly force can hunt for and remediate; this is why protection of that chain is the operation’s first protect priority.

## Friendly perspective — center of gravity and decisive points

- **Friendly operational COG:** the credibility and reach of Moldovan and EU institutions in the contested information environment (which US OIE supports), plus Western cohesion on attribution and cost imposition.
- **Decisive points and the effects logic:** (1) harden the CEC results chain → deny COA 3; (2) reach the diaspora and Russian-speaking audiences with credible counter-narrative before the adversary → blunt COA 1; (3) time a well-attributed exposure → strip deniability without burning sources or breaching partner consent; (4) stand up the energy backstop → blunt the “warmth” narrative. Together these raise the cost of COA 2/COA 3 and reduce the payoff of COA 1.

The friendly center of gravity is deliberately *partner* credibility rather than US capability, which is the inversion that defines this operation: the friendly force wins by making partner institutions more credible and the adversary’s narrative less so, not by applying its own capability visibly. Each decisive point above is an effect achieved largely through or behind a partner. The effects logic also reveals the operation’s internal tension — the same exposure that strips the adversary’s deniability (decisive point 3) raises friendly visibility and risks the interference narrative — which is why the timing and attribution of exposure are reserved as deliberate, conditions-based decisions rather than continuous activity.

## 1. How I see the problem (framing)

This is not a problem we solve by out-producing the adversary’s content. The Russian campaign — EMBER LATTICE — wins if Moldovan voters and the governing coalition make the choices Moscow wants while Russia never crosses a line that unifies a Western response, and if we become the story, we do Moscow’s work for it. I want the staff to internalize three things about this problem before we develop courses of action. ## 6. Risk guidance

The four risks below are interdependent, and I want the staff to plan them as a set, not in isolation. Tightening one usually loosens another, and my job — and at times the national level’s job — is to choose where we sit on that trade-space at each phase.

- **Escalation risk:** do not give Moscow a pretext. Anything that could read as US militarization of Moldovan politics or as crossing the threshold comes to me, and above me, before it happens.
- **Attribution risk:** I would rather be late, right, and survivable than fast, wrong, or source-burning — but I will not be so cautious that we cede the information space. Bring me the trade, with the intelligence gain/loss, for each disclosure decision. A wrong or source-burning attribution does not just fail; it hands the adversary a counter-narrative.
- **Partner risk:** if our visibility threatens the partner we are trying to help, we have failed. Calibrate to consent and re-check it (FFIR 1). Consent will likely narrow as the election nears; plan for that, do not be surprised by it.
- **Measurement risk:** be honest about what our assessments can and cannot show; do not confuse activity with effect, especially regarding adversary decision-making. The biggest self-inflicted danger in this operation is believing our own activity metrics.
- **Coalition risk:** expect the EU, OSCE, and Romania to diverge on timing and sequencing; plan a synchronized-but-not-unitary posture and a coordination rhythm that surfaces divergence early.

## 7. CCIR guidance

Develop PIRs against adversary decision-making (escalation/de-escalation), attribution confidence, COA 2/3 indicators, and CEC integrity; FFIRs against partner consent, approval-chain status, force-protection triggers, and coalition cohesion. Decompose each into EEIs with named collectors and reporting thresholds, and tell me which are “wake the commander” items. See (13).

## 8. Planning milestones

- Concept of operations to USEUCOM NLT 10 SEP 2027.
- Rehearsal of the COA 3 (election-day) response and the NEO sequel before T-30.

# 14 — Running Estimates (Seven Joint Functions)

**Operation RESOLUTE HERALD** **Producer:** JTF-RH staff **As of:** 060600ZSEP2027

Pre-Mission-Analysis running estimates. The Information estimate is presented first, consistent with the main effort. Each estimate gives the situation, capability to support, key shortfalls, critical issues, an assessment narrative, and recommendations.

## Information (supported / main effort)

- **Situation.** The decisive contest is in a segmented information environment against the integrated EMBER LATTICE campaign (Mediakontur cloned media; MATREȘCA amplification; Leer-3 SMS injection; the “RĂZBOI/CĂLDURĂ” themes; energy coercion), nationally directed and synchronized to the 5 December election. Moldovan (SIS, StratCom Center, CEC) and EU (EEAS East StratCom, EUPM) institutions are the visible principals.

- **Capability to support.** TIAD integrates and assesses the OIE and owns the MOE/MOP framework and the five-segment audience model. MISTF-EUROPE provides two MISO companies plus a tactical PSYOP detachment (~120). PA, CMO/KLE, defensive cyber (CNMF), and EMSO (Joint CEMA) are in direct support. EEAS/EUPM provide EU-platform reach and exposure capacity.

- **Key shortfalls.** Third MISO company (RFF-RH-001); approval-chain latency for product release and attribution against a hard date; constrained reach into the Russia-resident diaspora; proxy-only measures of effect on adversary decision-making.

- **Critical issues.** Winning the authenticity contest before the adversary’s narrative consolidates; keeping the United States out of the narrative; segment fidelity (no domestic-audience bleed via diaspora platforms).

- **Assessment.** The function can support the operation, but it is resourced for a contest of quality and timing, not of volume — which is appropriate, because volume is the adversary’s game and authenticity is ours. The binding risks are not capability but throughput and reach: the approval chain must be fast enough to release counter-narrative before the adversary’s framing sets, and the two-company MISTF must husband its effort to cover the contested segments (diaspora, Russian-speaking) rather than the already-favorable urban audience. The hardest unresolved problem is assessment itself; the function can measure what it does far better than what it achieves, and must say so plainly.

- **Recommendations.** Weight the decisive phase (T-60 to election day); stage attribution recommendations now; resolve the Title 10/22/50 release picture early; keep partners in front; build the information-fires synchronization matrix.

## Command and Control

- **Situation.** Supported command USEUCOM; CJTF = COMSOCEUR; Chief-of-Mission authority over in-country activity; supporting USCYBERCOM/USSPACECOM/USTRANSCOM; multinational EU/OSCE/Romania; interagency State/Treasury/IC.

- **Capability to support.** JTF HQ at Mihail Kogălniceanu with a Chișinău coordination element; J39 (Col Reyes) as OIE coordinating authority; liaison established with Embassy, EEAS, EUPM, OSCE, and SRI; succession of command established.

- **Key shortfalls.** Unresolved Title 10/22/50 authority seams across information lines; multiple approval chains with differing latencies; partner-consent constraints on visible activity; synchronized-but-not-unitary multinational messaging.

- **Critical issues.** A single integrated approval/synchronization picture; the attribution/disclosure recommendation chain and its timelines against the election calendar.

- **Assessment.** The principal C2 challenge in this operation is not the military chain of command, which is clear, but the lateral and interagency coordination architecture, which is not. Three authority families, several approval chains, a chief-of-mission with authority over in-country activity, and four external partners with divergent risk tolerances must be made to act coherently against a fixed deadline. The function’s success will be measured by whether decisions — especially attribution and product release — can be made at the speed the election demands without sacrificing the coordination that keeps the partner in front and the coalition together.

- **Recommendations.** Stand up a combined information-coordination cell with EEAS/EUPM liaison; codify the approval chain and map every approval against 5 December; designate decision authorities for each LOE.

## Intelligence

- **Situation.** Three retained adversary COAs; binding gaps are adversary intent/escalation thresholds (PIR 1/2), true campaign effect (PIR 5), releasable attribution (PIR 3), and CEC integrity (PIR 4).

- **Capability to support.** JIOC-EUR all-source fusion; CNMF (cyber indicators); Joint CEMA (EW characterization/geolocation); IC national assets; partner sharing (SIS, SRI, EEAS/INTCEN, OSCE) under foreign-disclosure rules.

- **Key shortfalls.** Shared- (not dedicated-) priority theater ISR over the election period (RFF-RH-003); intent-vs-capability ambiguity; attribution-vs-gain/loss tension; partner-by-partner attribution-confidence differences.

- **Critical issues.** Distinguishing campaign volume from effect; warning of a shift to COA 2/COA 3; protecting sources while supporting timely attribution.

- **Assessment.** Intelligence is the function under the most strain because the operation’s central object — adversary intent and the effect of both campaigns on cognition — is exactly what intelligence is least able to observe directly. The function can characterize Russian capability and activity well; it can warn on the physical and cyber indicators of COA 2 and COA 3; but it can offer only proxy-based, low-confidence judgments on Moscow’s decision calculus and on whether the influence campaign is actually moving the electorate. The function’s discipline must be to report that uncertainty honestly rather than resolve it artificially, and to give the commander the indicators that would change the assessment.

- **Recommendations.** Orient collection on decision-making indicators and the CEC chain; flag intent ambiguity in every assessment; submit RFF-RH-003; reconcile partner attribution positions before any combined public attribution.

## Fires (information and non-lethal effects)

- **Situation.** Fires are predominantly informational and non-lethal: MISO series, partner-fronted exposure, EMSO counter-effects, recommended attribution, and Treasury financial measures. No lethal fires in the baseline.

- **Capability to support.** MISO product series by audience segment; exposure packages for EU StratCom/StratCom Center; EMSO characterization/counter; nominated OFAC targets (Mediakontur, Ostrovschi networks); recommended-attribution packages.

- **Key shortfalls.** Synchronization of information fires with PA and EU StratCom; release/approval latency; deconfliction of attribution timing with partners; measuring effect.

- **Critical issues.** Applying targeting discipline (TAA, synchronization, assessment) to non-lethal/information effects; timing exposure to strip deniability at maximum effect.

- **Assessment.** The fires function in this operation is almost entirely non-lethal and informational, which makes the *timing* and *attribution* of effects more important than their volume. A single well-timed, well-attributed exposure that strips the adversary’s deniability at the right moment is worth more than a steady stream of rebuttals, but it also depends on intelligence maturity and national release authority that the function does not control. The function should therefore plan exposure and attribution as deliberate, conditions-based engagements with clear decision points, not as a continuous activity.

- **Recommendations.** Build an integrated information-fires synchronization matrix tied to the election calendar; pre-coordinate attribution and sanctions sequencing with State/Treasury/EU.

## Movement and Maneuver

- **Situation.** Visible maneuver held in Romania (2CR −). Nothing employed inside Moldova in the baseline. NEO sequel egress overland via the Prut crossings (Leușeni/Albița).

- **Capability to support.** 2CR (−) rotational presence (coordinating, under USAREUR-AF); conditional lift for the NEO sequel pending (RFF-RH-004); USTRANSCOM movement support; reception capacity at Mihail Kogălniceanu.

- **Key shortfalls.** No dedicated lift for NEO unless conditions trigger it; presence must avoid feeding the “militarization” narrative; winter road conditions complicate a potential evacuation.

- **Critical issues.** Maintaining deterrent presence visible to Moscow but not provocative to Moldovan domestic audiences; readiness of the NEO sequel without visible escalation.

- **Assessment.** Maneuver in this operation is principally a signaling instrument. The 2d Cavalry Regiment’s presence in Romania is meant to be seen by Moscow as a credible reminder of Alliance proximity and by the Moldovan public as something happening on Romanian, not Moldovan, soil. The function’s challenge is to keep that presence visible enough to deter without becoming material for the interference narrative, and to hold the NEO sequel at a high state of readiness without visible preparation that would itself signal escalation.

- **Recommendations.** Keep maneuver visible-but-restrained in Romania; pre-coordinate NEO routes, crossing capacity, and lift triggers; rehearse the sequel before T-30.

## Protection

- **Situation.** Protect partner electoral/information infrastructure (cyber, EMS), the Chișinău coordination element, US persons, and friendly PNT/communications.

- **Capability to support.** CNMF (network defense), Joint CEMA (spectrum protection/PNT resilience), RSO/force protection at the coordination element, USSPACECOM (assured SATCOM/PNT), J6 alternate communications.

- **Key shortfalls.** Hunt-forward team allocated only 60 days (RFF-RH-002), not covering election day; GNSS interference along the Dniester; physical security of the Chișinău element in a deteriorating-Security-Zone branch.

- **Critical issues.** Hardening the CEC results chain before the adversary acts; assuring partner civil messaging integrity against Leer-3.

- **Assessment.** The protection function is where the operation’s most dangerous course of action — a perception attack on the result — is defeated or not. Unlike the larger but unfixable energy vulnerability, the CEC results chain can be meaningfully hardened inside the planning horizon, which is why it is the protection main effort. The function’s principal exposure is temporal: the hunt-forward team’s 60-day allocation ends before the moment of greatest risk, making RFF-RH-002 the single most consequential force-management decision for this function.

- **Recommendations.** Prioritize the CEC results chain; extend hunt-forward to cover election day and after; emplace PNT-resilience and alternate communications for the coordination element; rehearse a results-integrity contingency with SIS/CEC.

## Sustainment

- **Situation.** Light footprint sustained from Romanian basing (Mihail Kogălniceanu); USTRANSCOM movement support; reachback for product development and cyber/EW analysis.

- **Capability to support.** Adequate for the baseline information-led campaign.

- **Key shortfalls.** The binding constraint is authorities/approval throughput, not logistics; surge sustainment required only if the NEO sequel activates.

- **Critical issues.** Reception and onward movement capacity in Romania if the NEO sequel triggers in winter.

- **Assessment.** Sustainment is not a limiting factor for the baseline operation; the deliberately light footprint is sustainable from Romania indefinitely. The function’s planning attention belongs almost entirely to the branch — the NEO sequel — where a winter evacuation through finite Prut crossings into Romanian reception facilities would impose real and time-sensitive sustainment demands the baseline does not.

- **Recommendations.** Maintain the light footprint; pre-plan NEO-sequel sustainment (medical, transport, reception) as a branch; confirm Romanian host-nation support arrangements.
