# **STRATEGIC CONTEXT (SOUTHERN SHIELD)**

**DTG: 230400ZJUN2026**

*(E) This paper orients the Commander to the national and theater setting for Operation SOUTHERN SHIELD. It draws on historical background, recent events, the current situation, national strategy guidance, and CJCS WARNORD, but it does not substitute for those references.*

**1. (E) HISTORICAL BACKGROUND.**

1.  \(E\) Since Papua New Guinea’s independence (and United States formal recognition) in 1975, the 760 kilometer-long border between it and what is collectively called Indonesia’s West Papua region[^1] on New Guinea has been a focus for mutual suspicion. Using both diplomacy and intimidation, Indonesia has sought to prevent Papua New Guinea (PNG) from becoming a sanctuary for the Free Papua Movement separatists seeking either independence from Indonesia or reunification with the PNG side of New Guinea. PNG’s policy on the border situation is conditioned by fears of Indonesian expansionism and sympathy for West Papuan efforts to defend their cultural identity against “Indonesianization.”

2.  \(E\) West Papua, which Indonesia views as an intrinsic part of their country, was formally incorporated into Indonesia in 1969 following the Act of Free Choice plebiscite. Although there were accusations of widespread electoral fraud and voter suppression, 84 member states, including the United States and Australia, voted in favor for the UN to accept the result. As a result, a significant number of Papuans refused to accept the territories’ integration into Indonesia and formed the separatist armed insurgency group *Organisasi Papua Merdaka* (OPM, “Free Papua Movement”) which has waged an insurgency against Indonesian authorities to this day. Most of the Eastern Pacific islands, including neighboring Solomon Islands, have denounced the Act of Free Choice and/or support Papuan self-determination. The PNG government, while sympathetic to OPM, remains keenly aware of the economic and military imbalance between the two countries and offers no political support to OPM.[^2] The U.S. has not designated OPM as a foreign terrorist organization (FTO).

3.  \(E\) Talks to draw up a new agreement to regulate relations and define rights and obligations along the shared border between PNG and Indonesia culminated in the signing of the Treaty of Mutual Respect, Cooperation, and Friendship on October 27, 1986. (See “Treaty of Mutual Respect, Cooperation, and Friendship”) The Treaty was, in effect, a bi-lateral non-aggression pact in which Indonesia and PNG agreed to “avoid, reduce, and contain disputes or conflicts between their nations and settle any differences that may arise only by peaceful means” \[Article 2\], and they promised that “they shall not threaten or use force against each other \[Article 7\]. The treaty also provided a basis for building a lasting structure of peace and cooperation. The structure for peace was enhanced by the 1987 ASEAN decision to allow PNG to become the first non-ASEAN country to accede to the 1976 ASEAN Treaty of Amity and Cooperation in Southeast Asia, as a non-voting observer. Since 1992, member states of ASEAN have routinely blocked PNG’s accession to full ASEAN membership due to PNG’s membership in the Pacific Islands Forum (PIF) as well as PNG’s challenges of political instability, crime, poor infrastructure, and lack of skilled labor.

4.  \(E\) The 1986 treaty left many issues unresolved. It did not resolve the problem of West Papuan refugees, fleeing insurgent violence, crossing into PNG. Additionally, PNG refused to agree to joint security operations in the border regions, while Indonesia avoided categorical assurance that its military would not cross the border in response to insurgent operations from PNG territory or in hot pursuit of OPM insurgents.

5.  \(E\) It is noteworthy that Indonesia has long supported PNG’s application to join ASEAN for regional stability reasons and security cooperation. It is likely that Indonesian support comes with a price: PNG admission to ASEAN requires PNG renunciation of any support for OPM as well as full security cooperation with Indonesia at the border to deny OPM any possibility of sanctuary in PNG territory. Politically, this would be difficult for the PNG government considering that the PNG people sympathize with the cause of OPM.

**2. (E) CURRENT STRATEGIC SITUATION.**

1.  \(E\) In February 2026, a recently armed wing of OPM calling itself the National Liberation Army (NLA) made a declaration of war against Indonesia. This followed unification of all various armed insurgent groups in West Papua. The NLA’s stated aim is to drive Indonesian forces out of West Papua and shut down operations of foreign companies exploiting New Guinea’s natural resources, for which indigenous peoples receive no benefit. This was followed by ~10,000 refugees fleeing the violence in West Papua and crossing the border into PNG, settling in the Komavi area. There are unconfirmed reports that large numbers of well-equipped insurgents crossed with them and have established numerous camps and weapons’ caches in the outlying hills on the PNG side.

2.  \(E\) Increasing reports of instability within West Papua and along the shared border are raising flags of alarm in Indonesia and PNG. Increasingly bold rebel action is likely to inspire a massive Indonesian military response. If Indonesia decides to invade Papuan territory to suppress OPM/NLA separatist activities, then the 2023 Agreement on Defense Cooperation signed by the U.S. and the government of PNG commits the U.S. to protect and secure U.S. personnel in PNG and thus, provides the U.S. the ability to cooperate in the defense of PNG territory from foreign invasion, IAW Article 5.[^3] This may involve a fight not only with West Papua’s insurgent NLA but Indonesia’s military as well. The political risk is that engaging in conflict on behalf of PNG may alienate Indonesia, which is an ASEAN partner to the U.S. Fighting the NLA risks alienating the Pacific Islands Forum (PIF)--many of which are supportive of U.S. policy in the Pacific – which supports OMP.

**3. (E) THE PRECIPITATING INCIDENTS TO CURRENT CRISIS.**

1.  \(E\) Regional violence has spread, with the flow of West Papua refugees increasing. Port Moresby, the capital of PNG, has been flooded by thousands of internally displaced persons (IDPs) fleeing the border. PNG in general and Port Moresby in particular are woefully equipped to handle this influx and have requested international assistance with this humanitarian crisis. USAID and several NGOs and International Organizations are flowing into Port Moresby and reporting an immediate need for food, shelter, clothing, and medical supplies. Due to the poor state of PNG infrastructure outside of the capital region, distribution of humanitarian supplies is expected to be a major problem.

2.  \(E\) USPACOM has received a report that Indonesia is continuing to fortify its outposts along the West Papua–PNG border, and now has an estimated 20,000 troops throughout West Papua, supported by at least two squadrons of attack aircraft (helicopters and fixed-wing). The Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) assesses that Indonesian forces are preparing for a limited offensive into PNG to eliminate OPM/NLA strongholds and camps.

3.  The U.S. Department of State (DoS) has been consulting other regional powers, particularly Australia, regarding cooperation and assistance to prevent a regional war between Indonesia and PNG.

4.  The U.S. Ambassador in Port Moresby reports that the PNG Prime Minister is convinced that an Indonesian invasion of Papuan territory is imminent. The Prime Minister is requesting immediate U.S. and Australian intervention to deter such an attack, or, if an attack occurs, to assist in the defense of PNG. DoS has issued a travel advisory for PNG and encouraged the 2,000+ American citizens living in PNG to voluntarily leave for their own safety.

**4. (E) NATIONAL STRATEGY AND STRATEGIC DIRECTION.**

1.  \(E\) In the Pacific, China remains the key competitor of the United States. It is the strategy of the United States to cooperate and compete internationally from a position of strength in order to sustain peace in the Pacific. This strength is derived from America’s alliances and partnerships, which form a defensive perimeter around Eurasia.[^4] To that end, as the NSS directs, we will build, posture, and sustain a strong denial defense. We will also work closely with our allies such as Australia and partners such as Indonesia and Papua New Guinea to incentivize and enable them to do more for our collective defense, which is in U.S. interests. The Indo-Pacific will soon make up more than half of the global economy. The American people’s security, freedom, and prosperity are therefore directly linked to our ability to trade and engage from a position of strength in the Indo-Pacific. Were China—or anyone else, for that matter—to dominate this broad and crucial region, it would be able to effectively veto Americans’ access to the world’s economic center of gravity, with enduring implications for our nation’s economic prospects, including our ability to reindustrialize. That is why the NSS directs DoW, and thus USPACOM, to maintain a favorable balance of military power in the IndoPacific, including in New Guinea.

2.  \(E\) The President recently held a press conference on the growing crisis in New Guinea and stated that while the United States would pursue peaceful settlement of all regional disputes and does not wish to take sides in this dispute between Indonesia, OPM separatists, and PNG, the U.S. will honor the 2023 Agreement on Defense Cooperation with PNG. He strongly stated that protection of U.S. military personnel, contractors, citizens and their property is a top priority. Finally, the President emphasized that while the U.S. did not wish to get involved in the internal activities of either Indonesia or PNG, he would authorize military force to protect U.S. personnel and non-combatants, contain the situation to the immediate area, and to de-escalate the conflict.

    1.  \(E\) Diplomacy. DoS will seek to defuse the situation by offering a third-party state broker talks between Indonesia and PNG. New Zealand, which is a member of Pacific Islands Forum and a founding member of Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) has committed to host these negotiations.

    2.  \(E\) Informational. The public position of the U.S. remains clear: this dispute will not be resolved through force of arms by Indonesia and PNG sovereignty must not be violated. Concurrently, the PNG government has a responsibility to regional stability to deny sanctuary to any armed insurgent or separatist groups.

    3.  \(E\) Military. Australia is a treaty ally of PNG and is obligated to use its military to defend the sovereignty of PNG: they will get involved militarily. While the U.S. is only obligated to protect U.S. citizens and their property, the President is willing to intervene with an expeditionary military force to ensure PNG sovereignty is not violated and that armed insurgent groups do not find illegitimate safe haven in PNG.

    4.  \(E\) Economics. U.S. tariffs on Indonesian exports to the U.S., worth \$45 billion, was recently dropped to 19%; the President will immediately raise tariffs to 100% should Indonesia violate PNG territory. PNG is heavily dependent upon U.S. mining machinery and petroleum products and the President has noted that an immediate sanction on these vital goods could be implemented should the PNG government balk at dealing with armed insurgent groups seeking safe haven on their side of the border.

3.  \(E\) The levers of national power, working together, will achieve the strategic endstate, which is to keep Indonesia and PNG as valuable cooperative partners in the region and avoid the possibility of armed conflict. As well, it will also strengthen the U.S. alliance with Australia, who will naturally take a leading role in strengthening PNG economically and militarily.

**5. (E) STRATEGIC RISK.**

1.  With Indonesia marshalling forces at the West Papuan/PNG border, there is limited time to achieve a diplomatic solution. Economic pressure, such as sanctions and tariffs, also require extended time to achieve the desired end. While Australia is both willing and able to deploy military forces to the West Papuan/PNG border, they will need time to alert and deploy sufficient forces capable of deterring or assisting PNG in repelling an Indonesian invasion. U.S. military forces can deploy sufficient military forces capable of repelling an Indonesian invasion, but this places U.S. military forces at risk of being perceived as taking sides in a regional dispute between U.S. regional partners, which would have a negative impact on U.S. interests in the Pacific and perhaps open the possibility of Chinese intervention on one side or the other, or even as a “peace-deal” broker, which is unacceptable to POTUS.

**1. (E) Strategic Setting (Papua New Guinea).**

1.  \(E\) While PNG has sought to maintain good relations with its neighbours Australia, [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia), and the [Solomon Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_Islands), tensions have emerged with Australia due to changes in aid, while regional conflicts have complicated relations with the Solomon Islands and Indonesia, due to the Bougainville independence movement and the West [Papua conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_conflict) respectively. The PNG government has recognised and supported Indonesia's control of neighbouring [Western New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_New_Guinea), the focus of the [Papua conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_conflict) where numerous human rights violations have reportedly been committed by the Indonesian security forces. Residents of PNG border communities, however, support West Papuan separatism and may cross the border for customary purposes or provide aid and shelter to West Papuan separatist groups, with whom they have cultural ties. Australia remains linked to PNG through institutional and cultural ties, and has remained the most consistent provider of foreign aid, as well as providing peacekeeping and security assistance. There are growing ties to China, mostly as a source of infrastructure investments. The strategic position of the country, linking Southeast Asia to the Pacific, has increased geopolitical interest in the 21st century.

2.  PNG has been an observer state in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations ([ASEAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASEAN)) since 1976, followed later by special observer status in 1981. It has filed its application for full membership status. PNG is a member of the [Non-Aligned Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Aligned_Movement), the [Commonwealth of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations), the [Pacific Islands Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Islands_Forum), [APEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia-Pacific_Economic_Cooperation), and the United Nations.

3.  \(E\) Since 2019, tensions between Indonesia and OPM have escalated. <span class="mark">On 1 December 2018, an armed group with ties to OPM kidnapped 25 civilian construction workers in [Nduga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nduga_Regency) regency, Papua. The following day, the group killed [19 of the workers and a soldier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nduga_massacre).One of construction workers had allegedly photographed the group raising the Morning Star flag at an independence celebration - considered illegal acts by Indonesian authorities.The construction workers were building a part of the Trans Papua highway that aims to connect remote communities in Papua. In reprisals to obtain the massacred workers' bodies, the Indonesian military allegedly carried out airstrikes on at least four villages and used [white phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_phosphorus), a [chemical weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_weapon) banned by numerous countries and international organizations. This was however denied by the Indonesian government. Other significant incidents include:</span>

    1.  <span class="mark">(E) Protests involving thousands of participants demanding an independence referendum. In several locations, the protests have turned into general riots, resulting in the destruction of government buildings in [Wamena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wamena), [Sorong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorong) and [Jayapura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jayapura). Clashes between protesters and counter-protesters and police resulted in injuries, with over 31 people killed from both the clashes and the rioting, mostly non-Papuan trapped when rioters burned houses. In response to the rioting, the [government of Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Indonesia) implemented an [internet blackout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_blackout) in the region. A [Reuters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuters) reporter from the [Jakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakarta) bureau described the unrest as "Papua's most serious in years".</span>

    2.  <span class="mark">On 25 April 2021, Special Forces major-general [I Gusti Putu Danny Karya Nugraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Gusti_Putu_Danny_Nugraha_Karya), head of the Papua intelligence agency, was killed when he was shot in the head while in an ambush in a heavily armed military convoy.</span>

    3.  <span class="mark">On 5 March 2022, Sebby Sambom, an OPM spokesperson supported the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), claiming alleged similarity of Ukrainian police and military treatment of the Russian minority as *genocide* with Indonesian police and military treatment of Papuans. OPM claimed Russian government support of [pro-Russian separatists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_separatist_forces_in_Donbas) as justified, and claimed that both Indonesia and Ukraine are "capitalist puppets" of the United States.</span>

    4.  <span class="mark">On 7 February 2023, [Papuan separatists attacked and set fire to a plane, after taking the pilot and five passengers hostage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nduga_hostage_crisis). The passengers were soon released. The New Zealander pilot, Philip Mehrtens, remained in captivity until 21 September 2024.The flight was operated by Indonesian airline [Susi Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susi_Air) that operates flights in and out of Papua. OPM has claimed responsibility for the kidnapping and attack, stating that they would be targeting all foreigners as a part of their campaign.</span>

    5.  <span class="mark">On 3 April 2023, four Indonesian soldiers died and another five suffered gunshot injuries in an OPM attack led by [Egianus Kogoya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egianus_Kogoya) in [Nduga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nduga_Regency), [Papua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_(province)).</span>

    6.  <span class="mark">On 11 February 2026, the pilot and co-pilot of a small commercial Smart Air plane were shot and killed during landing. Sambom claimed "the pilot was killed because this airline often carried Indonesian security forces throughout Papua". Another attack occurred on the same day that targeted a [Freeport Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeport_Indonesia) convoy, killing a soldier and injuring a Freeport employee and an officer.</span>

**2. (E) Escalation Arc (preceding 90 days).**

1.  \(E\) In February 2026, after an armed wing of OPM calling itself the National Liberation Army (NLA) made a declaration of war against Indonesia, ~10,000 refugees fled the violence in West Papua and crossed the border into PNG, settling in the Komavi area, which lies along the Fly river just across the international border. There are unconfirmed reports that large numbers of well-equipped insurgents crossed with them and have established numerous camps and weapons’ caches in the outlying hills on the PNG side.

2.  \(E\) Regional violence has spread, with the flow of West Papua refugees increasing. Port Moresby, the capital of PNG, has been flooded by thousands of internally displaced persons (IDPs) fleeing the border. Meanwhile, Indonesia is continuing to fortify its outposts along the West Papua–PNG border, and now has an estimated 20,000 troops throughout West Papua, supported by at least two squadrons of attack aircraft (helicopters and fixed-wing). DIA assesses that Indonesian forces are preparing for a limited offensive into PNG to eliminate OPM/NLA strongholds and camps.

3.  \(E\) The U.S. Ambassador in Port Moresby reports that the PNG Prime Minister is convinced that an Indonesian invasion of Papuan territory is imminent. The Prime Minister is requesting immediate U.S. and Australian intervention to deter such an attack, or, if an invasion occurs, assistance in repelling the Indonesians.

**3. (E) Strategic Environment (Relationships, Alliances, Frictions, Tensions).**

1.  **(E) Combatants - Summary.** The U.S. maintains a partnered relationship with Indonesia and PNG.

    1.  \(E\) Indonesia: **Indonesia is a U.S. partner**[^1] and plays an essential role in the security architecture of the region. We maintain a robust defense relationship particularly in maritime security. The country straddles vital sea lanes and borders the Strait of Malacca, one of the world's busiest trade routes, as well as the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Over the past 25 years, Indonesia has become a robust democracy, holding five direct presidential elections, each considered by international observers to have been largely free and fair. In the most recent, held in February 2024, Prabowo Subianto, a former defense minister who served as commander of the Indonesian military's special forces in the 1990s, was elected to succeed President Joko Widodo. Prabowo took office in October 2024 and is to serve a five-year term. The U.S.-Indonesia relationship has broadened over the past two decades, with closer military and counterterrorism cooperation and a range of new educational, environmental, and energy programs. Indonesia's foreign policy is guided by its historical role as a leader of the Non-Aligned Movement, and successive Indonesian governments have resisted aligning too closely with the United States or others, including the People's Republic of China. Indonesia is an active member in regional diplomatic institutions including the G-20 and Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN). Some 87% of Indonesians are Muslim, with the vast majority subscribing to moderate, syncretic forms of Sunni Islam. While religious diversity is enshrined in the constitution, some observers express concern about growing political influence of conservative religious groups. Non-Sunni Muslims (such as people in West Papua) and other religious minorities have been targets of violence, and some conservative groups have organized mass demonstrations against non-Muslim politicians. Indonesia also has a history of violent extremism: several bombings in Jakarta and Bali targeted Westerners in the early 2000s, and smaller-scale attacks have occurred periodically. Indonesia desires to play a larger role in international economic and security issues, especially in New Guinea.

    2.  \(E\) Though sizeable for this region, Indonesian armed forces are occupied with “other internal issues” and in providing security around Jarkarta. Consequently, beyond the forces already in West Papua, not much additional reinforcement capability is expected to exist to support counterinsurgency action along the West Papua–PNG border. Indonesia’s three armed services–collectively called ABRI–consist of the army, navy, and air force. A second strike force command is the Special Forces Command (*Kopassus*). *Kopassus* personnel are identified by their distinctive red berets. These Special Forces units are trained in intelligence gathering, a variety of special operations techniques, sabotage, airborne insertion, and seaborne landings. ABRI also has a border security force which guards the international borders of Indonesia; this force is called *Kostrad Perbatasan* (Border Army Strategic Reserve Command). It guards the border between Indonesia and PNG.

        1.  \(E\) Indonesian Army. The Indonesian Army has about 300,000 personnel on active military duty and almost the same number to draw from its reserve. The Army forces are organized into 13 Military Area Commands (*Kodams*), or regional commands. The *Kodams* incorporate provincial and district commands, each with roughly 6 infantry battalions, sometimes a cavalry battalion, artillery, or engineers.

            1.  *(E) Kodam* XVII/Cenderawish is the military area command which oversees the entire West Papua region. It has been reported that the 6 infantry battalions (1,400 each/8,000 total) are based at Manokwari, Jaypura, and Sorong. Currently there are additional battalions at Tanah, Merah, Mappi, and Merauke respectively.

        2.  \(E\) Indonesian Naval Forces. The Indonesian Marine Corps has 3 brigades, the closest being at Surabaya on East Java, approximately 4 days away by amphibious ship (5 LPDs, 19 LSTs, 20 LCMs, and 20 LCVPs).

            1.  \(E\) Submarines. One CAKRA diesel-electric (German Type-209) at Jakarta. One CAKRA Type 209 is believed to be operating in the Arafura Sea. One NAGAPASA (South Korean Type 209) is at Surabaya naval base.

            2.  \(E\) Surface ships: 33 former Dutch, British, and home-grown frigates and corvettes of various classes. 3 Corvettes and 6 frigates are believed to be based in Manokwari Naval Base in West Papua.

            3.  \(E\) It is believed that the Indonesian Navy also has over 100 armed fast boats (80-100 tons) available for troop transport, relocation, or interdiction.

        3.  \(E\) Indonesian Air Force. The Air Force posses over 100 combat-capable aircraft including 26 U.S.-built F-16A A/B fighters. Additionally, the Air Force has 5 Su-27SK/SKM ground attack aircraft and 11 Su-30MK/MK2 fighter-bombers. Helicopters include 6 Mi-35P attack helicopters, 8 AH-64A Apaches, and 30 BAE Hawk light attack/trainer aircraft.

            1.  \(E\) It is assessed that a squadron of Su-30/F-16 A/B aircraft are stationed on West Papua at Jayapura Air Base, along with at least 4 Mi-35 helicopters supporting ABRI forces at Timika.

    3.  \(E\) Likely objectives of Indonesia:

        1.  \(E\) Destroy OPM/NSA sanctuary locations in West Papua and in the PNG border region.

        2.  \(E\) Avoid open conflict with the United States and Australia.

        3.  \(E\) Achieve acknowledged regional power dominance in the New Guinea and South Pacific island areas.

    4.  \(E\) **(Partner)** Papua New Guinea. Despite deep historical ties going back to WWII, from 1993-2022 there was little bi-lateral engagement between the U.S. and PNG except U.S. assistance in the removal of millions of UXOs left over from WWII, which remain a significant hazard to this day. Relations have expanded significantly since 2023, driven by Papua New Guinea's strategic location in the Indo-Pacific and U.S. efforts to counter growing Chinese influence in the region. The bilateral partnership focuses heavily on defense, maritime security, and economic development.[^2]

    5.  The armed forces are administratively integrated under the PNG Defence Force (PNGDF) and are equipped and trained almost entirely with Australian assistance. There are approximately 3,600 personnel in the PNGDF and service is entirely voluntary.

        1.  \(E\) Army. Ground forces consist of:

            1.  Army Command HQ with signals squadron at Port Moresby.

            2.  The Royal Pacific Islands Regiment (RPIR) with one battalion at Port Moresby and one battalion at Wewak. (3300 personnel total)

            3.  There are company size units at Vanimo and Kiunga.

            4.  One long-range reconnaissance/counter-terrorism unit.

            5.  One engineer battalion at Lae with EOD capability.

        2.  \(E\) Navy. The Maritime Operations Element is the naval branch of the PNGDF, being mainly a light patrol force responsible for defending local littorals. While capable of providing shore logistics to the Army, they are not capable of ‘blue-water’ operations. Australia has supplied the PNGDF with four Pacific-class patrol boats and three Balikpapan-class landing craft, also supplied by Australia.

        3.  \(E\) Air Force. The Air Operations Element is the air force branch of the PNGDF, operating a small number of light aircraft and rotary wing assets in support of Army operations. It operates one CAVA CN-235 utility aircraft, one leased Bell 412 and two leased Bell 212 helicopters, and two IAI ARAVA light STOL transport aircraft (20 pax or bulk cargo).

    6.  \(E\) Likely objectives of PNG:

        1.  \(E\) Defend PNG territory against both Indonesian aggression and OPM infiltration.

        2.  \(E\) Achieve a negotiated settlement with Indonesia regarding their common border.

    7.  \(E\) Free Papua Movement (OPM). OPM formed in 1965 as a separatist movement and began receiving funding from Libya in 1969. In the 1980s, OPM received training from the pro-communist, Maoist New People’s Army, which is a foreign terrorist group seeking to overthrow the Philippine government. After 9/11, OPM has created its own indigenous cadre to provide rudimentary training and arms to the various West Papuan disparate armed groups. It is estimated that OPM has between 2500-3000 committed members with at least 2,000 armed and organized into small insurgent groups. They are believed to be equipped with a variety of small arms, RPGs, and light mortars.

    8.  \(E\) Likely objective of OPM: West Papua region unification with PNG.

2.  **(E)** The United States is allied or partnered with most nations of the South Pacific region and East Pacific islands.

    1.  \(E\) **(Ally)** Australia: In October 2025, Australia and PNG signed a mutual defense treaty that secured their mutual defence and security interests and affirmed that each would come to the other’s aid in defense of sovereignty.[^3] In addition, the U.S.-Australia alliance anchors peace and stability in the region. Australia plays a leading role in regional security, capacity-building efforts and addressing disaster response. Australia has procured high-tech U.S. platforms that will further increase interoperability. The Royal Australian Navy (RAN) is modern and interoperable with the USN, deploying two amphibious assault ships, three AEGIS-capable destroyers, and several dozen frigates, submarines, and multirole vessels. It is a certainty that Australia will honor their defense treaty with fellow commonwealth nation PNG and deploy forces to assist PNG in the defense of their sovereignty.

    2.  \(E\) **(Alliance)** AUKUS: <span class="mark">AUKUS is a trilateral security pact between Australia, the UK, and the US, launched in September 2021. This is significant since PNG is a Commonwealth nation that recognizes UK Charles III as king, along with Australia. Its core focus is countering China's influence in the Indo-Pacific region through two "pillars", Nuclear-Powered Submarines and Advanced Capabilities. The U.S. has agreed to provide Australia Nuclear powered submarines to replace its aging fleet and Australia will provide submarine basing access in the near future. Pillar II has the allies collaborate on sharing military technology and expertise across six key technological areas: artificial intelligence and autonomy, quantum technologies, undersea robotics, hypersonic missiles, advanced cyber, and electronic warfare.The pact is designed to foster deep integration of the three nations' defense industrial bases.</span>

    3.  \(E\) **(MN Partner)** East Pacific Islands (Oceana): Maintaining strategic influence in Oceania is becoming ever more important to U.S. national security. The provisions included in the Compacts of Free Association with the Federated States of Micronesia, the Republic of the Marshall Islands, and the Republic of Palau are important mechanisms that guide the relationships, including U.S. obligations for their defense. In return, these agreements provide assured access to the three Compact Nations in a contingency situation. They also give the U.S. authority to grant or deny access to another nation’s military forces which allows the U.S. to maintain a clear strategic line of communication across the Pacific.

    4.  \(E\) **(Bi-Lateral)** Solomon Islands. The U.S. has strong historical ties to the Solomon Islands. Between 1942-44, the U.S.drove the Japanese out of the Solomon Islands in the largest, costliest, and most complex air/land/sea campaign in the Pacific War. Relations between the U.S. and the Solomon Islands have only recently warmed, however. Between 1993-2022, there was no U.S. embassy in the Solomon Islands; however, with Chinese growing influence in the region, in 2023 the embassy was re-opened in Honiara (Guadalcanal) and an Incentive Investment Agreement was signed soon after, boosting economic ties between the U.S. and the Solomon Islands. Both nations are parties to the U.S.-Pacific Islands Multilateral Tuna Fisheries Treaty.

    5.  \(E\) **(Multi-Lateral)** Pacific Islands Forum (PIF): Founded in 1971, PIF comprises 18 nations - most in the South and Eastern Pacific, including Australia and PNG. The U.S. actively engages with PIF to deepen strategic and economic ties. Recent high-level engagements include PIF leaders attending the U.S.–Pacific Investment Summit in Honolulu to unlock infrastructure investments and collaborate on climate resilience.

    6.  \(E\) **(Multi-Lateral)** ASEAN: The U.S. and ASEAN share the common principles of a rules-based order, respect for international law, and the peaceful resolution of disputes. The ten ASEAN member states continue to seek ways to improve multilateral security engagements and advance stability in the Indo-Asia-Pacific.

## 1. Scope and Bounding

Covered: the armed forces of the Republic of Indonesia assessed present in, committed to, or able to deploy into the SOUTHERN SHIELD area of responsibility within the planning window, together with the Free Papua Movement (OPM) and the OPM armed wing, the National Liberation Army (NLA). The Indonesian national force structure is far larger than the laydown here; formations west of the Papua provinces are addressed only where movement into the region is assessed feasible — the Marine Corps brigade at Surabaya, the amphibious lift pool, and the submarine force. Not covered: the Papua New Guinea Defence Force, Australian Defence Force elements, and third-nation activity in the Arafura and Coral Seas.

**Confidence conventions.** HIGH — multiple independent streams in agreement, corroborated by imagery or signals collection. MODERATE — single credible or partly corroborated stream, consistent with known Indonesian patterns; material error possible. LOW — fragmentary, dated, single-source or inferential; a working estimate, not a planning factor. UNCONFIRMED — reporting validated by no collection discipline; carried for awareness only.

Strengths are reported as collected. Where unit-level and aggregate reporting disagree, both figures are reproduced.

## 2. National Command and Higher Headquarters Context

Executive authority over the Indonesian armed forces rests with President Prabowo Subianto, in office since October 2024 for a five-year term and formerly commander of Indonesian special forces in the 1990s. Release authority for cross-border kinetic action is assessed at HIGH confidence to be retained in Jakarta rather than delegated to the Military Area Command in Papua.

The three Indonesian armed services — army, navy and air force — are known collectively as **ABRI**. Two national commands bear directly on the SOUTHERN SHIELD area:

- **Kopassus (Special Forces Command).** A second strike force command, identified by the red beret, trained in intelligence gathering, special operations technique, sabotage, airborne insertion and seaborne landing. Presence in the Papua provinces assessed MODERATE; detachment identity, garrison and strength not established.

- **Kostrad Perbatasan (Border Army Strategic Reserve Command).** The ABRI border security force guarding Indonesian international borders, including the border with Papua New Guinea. Rotational border task groups in the Papua sector are assessed subordinate to Kostrad Perbatasan.

The Indonesian Army fields approximately 300,000 active personnel with a near-equal reserve pool, organised into **thirteen Kodam** (Military Area Commands). Each Kodam incorporates provincial and district commands and holds roughly six infantry battalions, in some cases with a cavalry battalion, artillery, or engineers.

Reinforcement beyond the forces already in West Papua is assessed limited: Indonesian ground forces are heavily committed to internal security and to the security of Jakarta (MODERATE).

## 3. Kodam XVII/Cenderawasih — Core OPFOR

**Kodam XVII/Cenderawasih** is the Military Area Command responsible for the entire West Papua region, headquarters assessed at Jayapura. The command controls infantry, territorial and combat support formations and coordinates with Indonesian Air Force and Navy elements at Sentani, Timika and Manokwari.

### 3.2 Territorial structure

Control is exercised through the standard three-tier structure: **Korem** (Military Resort Command), three assessed subordinate to Kodam XVII/Cenderawasih at Jayapura, Biak and Merauke; **Kodim** (District Military Command), one per regency, co-located with civil administration; and **Koramil** (Sub-district Military Command), extending via the Babinsa non-commissioned officer system to village level. Aggregate territorial personnel are assessed at approximately **4,800** (MODERATE) — lightly armed, not organised for manoeuvre, employed for population control, intelligence collection and civil administration.

### 3.3 Border and supporting formations

- **Satgas Pamtas (border security task groups).** Three rotational battalions, aggregate approximately **2,200**, under Kostrad Perbatasan on border outpost duty (MODERATE). Rotation dates and outpost assignments not established.

- **Brimob (police mobile brigade).** Approximately **2,600** across the Papua provinces (MODERATE). Brimob is a National Police formation, not subordinate to Kodam XVII/Cenderawasih; whether Brimob personnel fall inside the aggregate West Papua figure is not established.

- **Kodam combat support.** One field artillery battalion, one cavalry squadron with wheeled armoured personnel carriers and one combat engineer battalion assessed present (LOW); strengths, equipment and garrisons not established.

- **Kopassus.** Presence assessed MODERATE; all other particulars LOW.

**Border posture.** Indonesia has continued to fortify outposts along the West Papua–Papua New Guinea border, the densest construction and clearance activity opposite the Fly River headwaters. ## 7. Free Papua Movement (OPM) and National Liberation Army (NLA)

### 7.1 Origins, strength and organisation

OPM formed in **1965** as a separatist movement and began receiving Libyan funding in **1969**. During the 1980s OPM received training from the Maoist New People's Army of the Philippines. After 2001 OPM developed an indigenous cadre providing rudimentary training and arms to the disparate West Papuan armed groups. No current state sponsor has been identified.

- **Committed membership:** 2,500 to 3,000 (MODERATE).

- **Armed strength:** at least 2,000, in small insurgent groups (MODERATE).

- **Armament:** small arms, rocket-propelled grenades, light mortars (MODERATE). No man-portable air defence holding identified; the assessment of absence is LOW.

- **Organisation:** regionally based defence commands, each controlling several field groups assessed to operate in cells of roughly 15 to 40 fighters, with limited lateral communication and no centralised fire control (LOW).

### 7.2 Armed wing, aims and leadership

In **February 2026** an armed wing designated the **National Liberation Army (NLA)** declared war against Indonesia after unifying the previously disparate West Papuan armed groups. NLA stated aims are to drive Indonesian forces from West Papua and to shut down foreign resource extraction. The OPM objective is unification of West Papua with Papua New Guinea.

The OPM spokesman is **Sebby Sambom**. **Egianus Kogoya** is identified in reporting as the field commander responsible for the Nduga attack of 3 April 2023. No NLA command structure has been identified below the public spokesman, and the relationship between the February 2026 declaration and the pre-existing regional commands is not established (LOW). OPM is **not** a designated United States Foreign Terrorist Organization.

### 7.3 Incident record

| **Date** | **Event** |
|:---|----|
| 1 Dec 2018 | Nduga: 25 Trans Papua highway workers kidnapped by an OPM-linked group; 19 workers and one soldier killed the next day. Alleged Indonesian reprisal airstrikes on at least four villages, alleged white phosphorus use; denied by Jakarta. |

The pattern is attacks on aviation, resource-extraction personnel and infrastructure, security-force convoys and construction projects, with hostage-taking of foreign nationals and deliberate public messaging for international effect.

### 7.4 Assessed disposition in the Komavi area

Following the NLA declaration in February 2026, approximately **10,000 refugees** fled the violence in West Papua and settled in the **Komavi area**, along the Fly River just inside Papua New Guinea. Komavi-area clans belong to the Awin and Yonggom language groups and hold kin ties across the border.

**There are unconfirmed reports that large numbers of well-equipped insurgents crossed with the refugee flow and have established numerous camps and weapons caches in the outlying hills on the Papua New Guinea side of the border.** This reporting is **UNCONFIRMED**. No camp or cache location, strength or organisational identity on Papua New Guinea territory has been validated by any collection discipline; the reporting is carried for awareness only.

OPM and NLA elements are assessed to avoid eradication by both Indonesian and Papua New Guinea security forces and to blend in with the displaced population (MODERATE). The most dangerous assessed course is deliberate action to foment full-scale war between Papua New Guinea and Indonesia, forcing international dialogue on West Papuan independence (MODERATE).

## 10. Strengths and Vulnerabilities — Synthesis

**Assessed strengths.**

- **Acclimatisation.** Forces in the Papua provinces are acclimatised and have operated in the terrain for decades.

- **Territorial depth.** The Korem, Kodim and Koramil structure provides persistent presence, a mature human intelligence network, and administrative control.

- **Interior lines.** Sentani, Timika and Manokwari form a triangle of full-service bases inside Indonesian territory.

- **Air basing quality.** Sentani offers 3,000 m of asphalt, full military fuel, night and instrument capability, and hardened dispersal.

- **Combined arms.** Fixed-wing strike, armed rotary wing, and naval surface and subsurface units are available to the command.

- **Mass.** The assessed 20,000 in West Papua exceeds any locally available opposing ground force.

**Assessed vulnerabilities.**

- **Limited reinforcement.** Ground forces are committed to internal security and the security of Jakarta.

- **Sustainment architecture.** Forward outposts are supported by air and by coastal and riverine movement rather than by road; no forward bulk fuel installation is assessed east of Timika (LOW).

- **Small attack helicopter fleet.** Six Mi-35P nationally, at least four at Timika; attrition or maintenance downtime in a fleet this size is disproportionate.

- **Weather-constrained air employment.** Cloud base below 1,500 ft AGL on some 60 percent of Fly headwaters mornings restricts the daily window.

- **Small submarine force.** Three Type 209 boats nationally, one assessed at sea in the region.

- **Territorial dispersion.** A substantial share of the ground force is committed to static territorial and population-control tasks.

- **Materiel heterogeneity.** Former Dutch, former British and domestic ship classes of varying age; US, Russian and British aircraft with separate logistic chains.

- **Political constraint.** Indonesian objectives include avoiding open conflict with the United States and Australia, with diplomacy assessed to continue in parallel with military preparation.

**Assessed Indonesian objectives.** Strategic: coerce Papua New Guinea cooperation by a border demonstration of military power; failing coercion, a limited ground offensive to search for, isolate and destroy OPM sanctuary camps; diplomacy and information operations to limit United States and Australian involvement. Operational: establish forward bases along the international border; establish local air superiority over the Komavi area; isolate and destroy OPM camps. Red lines: no West Papuan independence, no West Papua joining Papua New Guinea, no OPM safe haven on Papua New Guinea territory. Sponsorship of full ASEAN membership has been offered for border security cooperation.

**1. (E) Papua New Guinea (PNG) Geography and Demography.**

1.  \(E\) Located in Melanesia just south of the equator, New Guinea is the second largest island in the world, behind Greenland, with an area of 312,000 square miles (larger than Texas) and at its closest point is only 90 miles from the northern tip of Australia, south across the Torres Strait. From west to east New Guinea is 1,600 miles long and at its widest point is 500 miles north to south. Two parallel mountain chains run west to east through the interior of New Guinea with peaks towering to a height of 16,000 feet. In Papua New Guinea (PNG), the Owen Stanley Mountains, running northwest to southeast, dominate the eastern peninsula. As far back as 1942, warfare in Papua New Guinea was largely a war of supply, protected by air, and this has remained true unto this day.[^1] The American, Australian, and New Zealand Armies conducted combat operations in Papua New Guinea from 1942-44 and weather and terrain-related casualties far outpaced combat casualties. PNG shares a challenging, unpleasant climate: hot, constantly wet, equatorial, and tropical. Heat, heavy rains, and high humidity degrade supplies, equipment, and troops at an alarming rate and the ground off of improved roads is sticky morass of mud most of the year. For troops operating in the field, priority should be given to improved roads, facility construction, and living conditions; otherwise, troops and equipment will quickly become debilitated by the environment.

2.  \(E\) Distances (nautical miles):

    1.  San Diego to Lae, PNG: 6,750 nm

    2.  Pearl Harbor to Lae, PNG: 3,540 nm

    3.  Yokosuka, Japan to Lae, PNG: 3,260 nm

    4.  Okinawa to Lae, ONG: 2,626 nm

    5.  Guam to Lae, PNG: 1,400 nm

    6.  Kwajalein to Lae, PNG: 1,782 nm

    7.  Lae to Port Moresby: 200 air miles (no direct road)

    8.  RAAF Darwin, Australia to Port Moresby, PNG: 1,120 nm

    9.  RAAF Tindal, Australia to Port Moresby, PNG: 726 nm

    10. Lae to Daru, Western Province: 297 air mile (no direct road)

    11. Port Moresby to Daru, Western Province: 275 air miles (no direct road)

    12. Daru to Komavi area: 190 air miles (no roads at all)

3.  \(E\) Known for its rugged, volcano-prone terrain and vast tropical rainforests, PNG is recognized as the most linguistically diverse country in the world, home to over 800 distinct languages, including English which is recognized as an official language and used primarily in the education, business, and political systems.

4.  \(E\) The population of 17 million comprises hundreds of indigenous tribal groups, each with unique cultural traditions, art, and ceremonies and it is estimated that 85% of this population resides in rural areas, largely depending on subsistence agriculture and a cash economy. Infrastructure is limited and largely poorly maintained, which in conjunction with the geography, increases the isolation of communities from the population centers. Port Moresby, the capital, struggles to impose federal control of outlying areas: in 2019, the island of Bougainville (the largest of the Solomon Islands archipelago) began negotiations for independence. The U.S. government does not recognize Bougainville independence and has remained hesitant to offer any support to the independence movement.

5.  The Komavi area is located in the Western Province close to the headwaters of the Fly River, which runs southeast to the southern coast of PNG at Daru. The province is the largest province by area but the least densely populated, with roughly 5 people per square km. Travel between Daru (provincial capital) and remote Komavi is by river or air: there are no roads.

6.  \(E\) The 2011 census found that 95.6% of citizens identified themselves as Christian. Virtually no respondent identified as being non-religious.[Religious syncretism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_syncretism) is common, with many citizens combining their Christian faith with some traditional indigenous religious practices. The largest denomination is the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church), followed by 26.0% of the population. The government and judiciary have upheld the constitutional right to freedom of speech, thought, and belief. A constitutional amendment in March 2025 recognised Papua New Guinea as a Christian country, with specific mention of "God, the Father; Jesus Christ, the Son; and Holy Spirit", and the Bible as a national symbol.

**2. (E) Economy.** <span class="mark">Papua New Guinea is classified as a [developing economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developing_country) by the [International Monetary Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Monetary_Fund). The economy is largely dependent on [natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resource), with capital investment concentrated in mining and oil, while most labour is employed in agriculture. As of 2018, natural resource extraction made up 28% of overall GDP, with substantial contributors including [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining_in_Papua_New_Guinea), oil, and [natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_in_Papua_New_Guinea)</span>. <span class="mark">Significant exported minerals include gold, copper, [cobalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt), and [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel). **3. (E) Infrastructure.** The country's terrain has made it difficult for the country to develop [transportation infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Papua_New_Guinea), resulting in air travel being the most efficient and reliable means of transportation. There are five highways, although only two go into the interior. Domestic shipping is limited. There are 22 international ports, although not all are operational. The biggest is [Lae Port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lae_Port?action=edit&redlink=1), which handles about half of all international cargo. Papua New Guinea has over 500 airstrips, most of which are unpaved.Many roads are poorly maintained, and some cannot be used during the wet season. Nonetheless, the majority of the population lives within 5 kilometres (3.1 mi) of a national road, and even more live near district or rural roads. The [Sirinumu Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirinumu_Dam) and [Yonki Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonki_Dam) provide some hydropower. There is limited sewage treatment, even in the capital of Port Moresby. Some is discharged directly into the ocean, leading to issues with pollution.[Renewable energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy) sources represent two-thirds of the total electricity supply. The major international port is Lae, located in the Huon Gulf just south of the Vitiaz and Dampier Straits. On the southern coast of PNG, Port Moresby is the major port. Regional ports include Madang, to the northwest of the Vitiaz Strait; Kimbe Bay on the northern coast of the Island of New Britain; and Rabaul.

**4. (E) Political-Strategic Activities.**

1.  \(E\) <span class="mark">Papua New Guinea is a [Commonwealth realm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_realm) with [Charles III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_III) as [king](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Papua_New_Guinea). 2.  \(E\) The political culture of PNG is influenced by existing kinship and village ties, with communalism an important cultural factor given the many small and fragmented communities. Regional and local identities are strong, and traditional politics has integrated with the modern political system. There is a broad [Papuan regional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Region,_Papua_New_Guinea) identity, and to some extent a highlands one, which can affect politics. However, outside of Bougainville, regional politics are autonomist rather than separatist, with separatism often used as rhetoric rather than as an ultimate goal, which is in contrast to West Papuan separatists such as OPM. The importance of community ties to their land are reflected in the legal system, with 97% of the country designated as [customary land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customary_land), held by communities. Many such effective titles remain unregistered and effectively informal. Voting often occurs along tribal lines, an issue exacerbated by politicians who might be able to win off the small vote share provided by a unified tribe.

## 1. Scope and Limitations of the Data

This compilation covers the physical environment of the operational area described in the Joint Intelligence Preparation of the Operational Environment brief: the Western Province of Papua New Guinea, the international border with the Indonesian West Papua region, the Fly River basin, the Torres Strait and Arafura Sea approaches, and the Papua New Guinea aerial and maritime ports of entry at Lae, Port Moresby and Daru.

Data confidence is uneven and is annotated throughout. Coastal hydrography in the Gulf of Papua and the Torres Strait is surveyed to modern standard. Inland hydrography above Kiunga rests on commercial river-operator soundings taken for barge traffic and is not surveyed to hydrographic standard. Aeronautical data for the Papua New Guinea highland and border strips derives from the national aerodrome register, which is updated by operator report rather than by survey; pavement classification numbers and California bearing ratio data are not held for any airfield in the Western Province. Terrain elevation derives from Shuttle Radar Topography Mission data at 30-metre posting; persistent cloud cover over the border cordillera degrades optical currency, and several 1:100,000 sheets covering the border zone have not been revised since 1974.

No engineer or airfield reconnaissance has been conducted in the Western Province by United States forces since 2024.

## 2. The Island of New Guinea — Regional Physical Setting

New Guinea is the second-largest island in the world, with an area of 312,000 square miles, extending 1,600 miles from west to east and 500 miles from north to south at its widest point. At its closest point the island lies 90 miles from the northern tip of Australia across the Torres Strait. The island is divided along the 141st meridian east for most of its length, with the Indonesian West Papua region to the west and Papua New Guinea to the east; the border departs from the meridian only in the south, where it follows the Fly River for a short segment before returning to the meridian line. The total land border measures 760 kilometres.

Two parallel mountain chains run west to east through the interior, with peaks reaching 16,000 feet. In Papua New Guinea the Owen Stanley Range, running northwest to southeast, dominates the eastern peninsula. The central cordillera forms an unbroken barrier between the northern and southern coastal plains for the length of the country, and is crossed by no road.

South and southwest of the cordillera the land falls away into the Fly–Digul lowland, a wet alluvial plain of low relief, extensive seasonal swamp, and braided river systems. The Komavi area lies at the northern margin of this lowland where it meets the foothills of the border ranges.

## 4. The Fly River System

The Fly is the largest river in Papua New Guinea by discharge and the only inland waterway in the country that carries commercial cargo of consequence. It rises in the border cordillera, runs south and southeast for approximately 650 miles, and enters the Gulf of Papua through a delta 30 miles wide at Daru.

### 4.1 Navigability

| **Reach** | **Distance** | **Controlling depth** | **Traffic** |
|:---|----|----|----|
| Delta bar to Obo | 190 river miles | 6.0 m year-round | Ocean-going vessels to 5,000 DWT |
| Obo to Kiunga | 320 river miles | 4.5 m December–April; **2.4 m June–November** | Self-propelled barges to 3,000 DWT |
| Kiunga to Ok Tedi confluence | 40 river miles | 1.5 m seasonal | Shallow-draft workboat only |
| Above the Ok Tedi confluence | — | Not navigable by powered vessel | Canoe |

Total channel distance Daru to Kiunga is 510 river miles. Loaded upriver transit is four to five days; downriver three. The channel is unmarked above Obo and is navigated by local knowledge; the Ok Tedi river operation maintains its own pilots and issues seasonal notices to mariners. Bank erosion and mid-channel bar migration are continuous, and the surveyed line of the channel is revised by the operator at approximately six-month intervals.

### 4.2 Commercial river capacity

Ok Tedi Mining Limited operates the only commercial fleet on the river: eleven self-propelled barges of 1,200 to 3,000 DWT, four tugs, and two fuel barges of 1,800 tonnes capacity. The fleet is fully committed to mine inbound consumables and outbound concentrate, on a cycle of approximately eleven days. Three additional barges of 800 DWT are operated by two Daru-based coastal traders serving delta communities.

### 4.3 Hydrology

The Fly basin receives 7,600 millimetres of rainfall per year in the headwaters and 2,100 millimetres at Daru. The upper basin has no dry season; the delta and lower reaches are seasonal, with a pronounced low-water period from June to November. Discharge at Obo ranges from 1,900 cubic metres per second at low water to 8,700 at flood. Flood peaks in the upper reaches arrive within 12 to 30 hours of a heavy rainfall event and recede over three to five days. Suspended sediment load is high; turbidity above 200 nephelometric turbidity units is normal following rainfall.

### 5.3 Aviation operating environment

**Density altitude.** Sustained 27 °C ambient at 88 percent relative humidity places density altitude at Komavi in the band 900 to 1,150 metres against a field elevation of 268 metres. At Kiunga the equivalent band is 640 to 880 metres. Density altitude is highest between 1300 and 1600 local.

**Cloud and visibility.** Cloud base in the Fly headwaters lies below 1,500 feet above ground level on approximately 60 percent of mornings, lifting through the middle of the day. The reliable visual meteorological conditions window at Komavi is 1000 to 1500 local. Afternoon convective build-up over the border cordillera regularly reaches 45,000 feet and closes the northwest approach. Below-minima conditions persist for the whole day on an average of 62 days per year.

**Surface wind.** Light and variable; the Komavi strip has no crosswind limitation of consequence. Down-valley drainage flow of 5 to 10 knots occurs before 0900 local.

**Bird hazard.** Significant at Daru and in the delta; flying-fox activity at Komavi from 1700 local.

**Airspace.** Port Moresby Flight Information Region covers Papua New Guinea to the 141st meridian. No radar coverage exists west of Port Moresby below 24,000 feet. Procedural separation only. Indonesian Ujung Pandang Flight Information Region abuts at the meridian; there is no direct controller-to-controller coordination circuit between the two.

### 6.2 Approaches

**Torres Strait.** The strait separates the New Guinea south coast from Cape York, 90 miles at the narrowest. Controlling depth in the Prince of Wales Channel is 12.2 metres. Pilotage is compulsory under Australian regulation for vessels over 70 metres and for all loaded tankers. Tidal streams reach 6 knots in the Prince of Wales Channel. The Torres Strait Protected Zone governs traditional inhabitant movement across the strait.

**Gulf of Papua and the Fly delta approach.** Open water to within 25 miles of Daru, then a shoaling delta with a shifting bar. The Daru approach carries 6.0 metres over the bar at all states of tide. Ocean swell in the gulf is moderate from the southeast May to October.

**Arafura Sea.** Shallow, with general depths of 50 to 80 metres over the continental shelf; a poor acoustic environment. The sea provides the shortest approach from Indonesian bases in West Papua to the Papua New Guinea south coast and to the Torres Strait.

**Vitiaz and Dampier Straits.** North of Lae, between New Guinea and New Britain. The principal approach to Lae from the north and northeast. Deep water; no navigational constraint of consequence.

**Coral Sea approach.** Open ocean approach to Port Moresby and the south coast from the southeast, and the route from Townsville and Brisbane.

## 7. Surface Movement

Papua New Guinea has five highways, of which two reach the interior, and no road network connecting the principal population centres to one another. The following pairs, all of operational relevance, have **no direct road connection**: Lae to Port Moresby, Lae to Daru, Port Moresby to Daru, and Daru to the Komavi area.

| **Route** | **Status** |
|:---|----|
| Highlands Highway (Lae to Mount Hagen) | Sealed and unsealed, 430 miles; the principal interior road; landslip closures common in the wet |
| Kiunga to Tabubil mine road | Sealed, 85 miles, privately maintained by the mine operator, gated |
| Daru Island internal roads | 14 miles, unsealed |
| Komavi area tracks | Foot and light four-wheel-drive; interfluve tops only; impassable in and after rain |

Off improved roads the ground is a sticky morass of mud for most of the year. Cross-country vehicle movement in the Fly lowland is not practicable outside the interfluve tops, and is not practicable on the interfluve tops within 48 hours of heavy rainfall. Unexploded ordnance from the 1942 to 1944 campaign remains a significant hazard nationwide and has not been cleared in the Western Province interior.

## 8. Climate

Papua New Guinea is classified across three Köppen zones: Af tropical rainforest, Am tropical monsoon, and Aw tropical wet savanna. The Komavi area and the border cordillera lie in the Af zone and have no dry season. The Daru coastal plain lies in the Aw zone and has a pronounced dry season from June to November.

| **Station** | **Zone** | **Annual rainfall** | **Rain days** | **Mean max** | **Mean min** | **Mean RH** |
|:---|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| Komavi area | Af | 7,600 mm | 280 | 31 °C | 22 °C | 88% |
| Kiunga | Af | 6,900 mm | 265 | 32 °C | 22 °C | 86% |
| Daru | Aw | 2,100 mm | 130 | 31 °C | 24 °C | 79% |
| Lae | Af | 4,600 mm | 220 | 31 °C | 23 °C | 83% |
| Port Moresby | Aw | 1,010 mm | 90 | 32 °C | 23 °C | 74% |

**June conditions.** June falls within the southeast trade season. Daru, Port Moresby and the southern coastal plain are in their driest months. The Fly headwaters and the Komavi area are not; June rainfall at Komavi averages 520 millimetres across 22 rain days. The distinction between the coastal and headwater regimes is the single largest source of error in seasonal planning for the Western Province.

**Tropical cyclones.** Papua New Guinea lies close to the equator and is not subject to tropical cyclones within the operational area. The Coral Sea approach south of 10°S is within the Australian region cyclone zone, season November to April.

### 8.1 Effects on materiel and personnel

Heat, heavy rain and high humidity degrade supplies, equipment and troops at a high rate. Documented effects in this environment include fungal growth on optics and electrical insulation within 14 days of exposure; corrosion of unprotected ferrous components within 30 days; degradation of packaged rations and medical stores held outside climate control; saturation of textiles and leather that does not reverse without forced drying; and reduced service life of rotary-wing dynamic components from ingested moisture and particulate.

Personnel not acclimatised to a sustained wet-bulb globe temperature in the range experienced in the Fly lowland require a phased acclimatisation period, during which work-rest cycles and water intake requirements are substantially more restrictive than in temperate conditions. The 1942 to 1944 campaign in Papua New Guinea produced more casualties from weather and terrain than from combat.

## 10. The International Border

The border runs 760 kilometres from the north coast at Wutung near Vanimo to the south coast at the mouth of the Bensbach River, following the 141st meridian east except for the segment where it follows the Fly River thalweg. It is marked by 14 principal monuments and a number of intermediate markers, many of which are overgrown or displaced. There is no fence, no continuous cleared trace, and no border road on either side.

| **Segment** | **Character** | **Crossing conditions** |
|:---|----|----|
| North coast to Bewani | Coastal plain and low hills | Vehicle-capable in the dry on tracks; two informal crossing points |
| Bewani to Telefomin | Cordillera, 1,200–2,600 m, karst | Foot only; four known passes |
| Telefomin to the Fly | **Border ranges adjacent to the Komavi area**; 400–1,400 m, dissected | Foot only; at least nine established foot routes and an unknown number of hunting tracks |
| Fly River segment | River thalweg | Canoe; unregulated |
| Fly to the south coast | Bensbach lowland, seasonal swamp | Foot and canoe; heavily used for customary movement |

Populations on both sides of the segment adjacent to the Komavi area belong to the Awin and Yonggom language groups and hold kinship, land and ceremonial ties across the border. Customary movement across the line is continuous, long-established, and unrecorded by either government. Ninety-seven percent of the land on the Papua New Guinea side is customary land held by communities, and titles are largely unregistered.

Indonesian border posts on the West Papua side of this segment are established at intervals of 15 to 40 kilometres and are resupplied by helicopter and by track. Papua New Guinea maintains no permanent presence on the border in the Western Province; the nearest Papua New Guinea Defence Force element is the company at Kiunga.

## 1. (E) SITUATION

**1.A. (E) General.** A dispute between the Republic of Indonesia and the Independent State of Papua New Guinea over the armed insurgent group known as the National Liberation Army, the armed wing of the Free Papua Movement, has escalated to a point at which armed conflict between two United States partners is assessed as a near-term possibility. The National Liberation Army declared war on Indonesia in February 2026 following the unification of the armed separatist groups of the West Papua region. Approximately 10,000 refugees subsequently crossed the international border into Papua New Guinea and settled in the Komavi area of Western Province. Reporting that armed and well-equipped insurgents crossed with the refugee population and have established camps and weapons caches on the Papua New Guinea side of the border is unconfirmed.

**1.B. (E) Escalation arc.** The present crisis has developed over approximately 120 days. Following the unification of the West Papua armed separatist groups and the declaration of war by the National Liberation Army in February 2026, refugee movement across the border into Western Province began and has continued. Regional violence has spread and the refugee flow has increased. Port Moresby has received several thousand internally displaced persons. Indonesia has responded by reinforcing and fortifying its border outposts. Diplomatic exchanges between Jakarta and Port Moresby over responsibility for border security have hardened. Indonesia has accused Papua New Guinea of failing to secure its border and has demanded that the Papua New Guinea government use force to deny sanctuary to the Free Papua Movement. Papua New Guinea has refused this demand, stating that there are no armed Free Papuan Movement groups on their side of the border. Further, they have demanded that Indonesia reduce its armed forces presence at the border.

**1.C. (E) Adversary and other actors.** Indonesia has fortified its outposts along the West Papua to Papua New Guinea border and maintains an estimated 20,000 troops throughout the West Papua region, supported by at least two squadrons of attack aircraft. The Defense Intelligence Agency assesses that Indonesian forces are preparing a limited offensive into Papua New Guinea to eliminate Free Papua Movement and National Liberation Army strongholds and camps. Further detail is at REF A.

The Free Papua Movement is an armed separatist organization of long standing, estimated at 2,500 to 3,000 committed members. The United States has not designated it a foreign terrorist organization. Its objectives are its own and are not aligned with those of either government.

**1.D. (E) Strategic guidance.** The Indo-Pacific is the priority theater. The National Security Strategy directs the maintenance of a favorable balance of military power in the region and the strengthening of allies and partners such that they contribute more to collective defense. Both parties to the present dispute are United States partners. The strategic interest is served by the preservation of both relationships and by the prevention of a conflict that would advantage competitors and invite external mediation of a regional dispute. Detail is at REF C.

**1.E. (E) Friendly.** Papua New Guinea is a partner of the United States under REF D. The Prime Minister of Papua New Guinea, as reported by the United States Ambassador in Port Moresby, assesses that an Indonesian invasion of Papuan territory is imminent, and has requested immediate United States and Australian intervention to deter such an attack or, should an invasion occur, assistance in repelling it. Australia and Papua New Guinea concluded a mutual defence treaty in October 2025. Indonesia is also a United States partner and holds an established defense relationship with the United States, particularly in maritime security.

The Papua New Guinea Defence Force numbers approximately 3,600 personnel and is equipped and trained almost entirely with Australian assistance. Its capacity for sustained operations in Western Province is limited.

**1.F. (E) Civil.** The displaced population in the Komavi area and internally displaced persons arriving in Port Moresby have generated a humanitarian requirement for which the Government of Papua New Guinea has requested international assistance. The Department of State, the United States Agency for International Development, and nongovernmental and international organizations are engaged. More than 2,000 United States citizens reside in Papua New Guinea. The Department of State has issued a travel advisory and has encouraged voluntary departure. Populations on both sides of the border in the Komavi sector hold customary and kinship ties across the line.

**1.G. (E) Diplomatic and economic activity.** The Department of State is consulting regional powers, principally Australia, on cooperation and assistance to prevent a war between Indonesia and Papua New Guinea. New Zealand has committed to host third-party talks. Economic measures affecting both parties are under consideration at the national level and are not within the purview of this order. Planning is to proceed on the basis that diplomatic and economic efforts continue in parallel and that military planning supports rather than displaces them.

**1.H. (E) National direction.** The President has directed that United States military personnel, American citizens, contractors, and their property in Papua New Guinea be protected; that the situation be contained to the immediate area; and that the conflict be de-escalated. The President has further directed that the United States pursue peaceful settlement of the dispute, has stated that the United States does not wish to take sides between Indonesia, the Free Papua Movement separatists, and Papua New Guinea, and has confirmed that the United States will honor REF D. Detail is at REF C.

**1.I. (E) Assumptions for strategic-level planning.** The following are directed for use at the strategic level. CDRUSPACOM is to develop such further assumptions as are necessary to continue planning, and is to report them.

**1.J. (E) Limitations directed at the national level.**

## 1.4 PMESII-PT survey

**Political.** Papua New Guinea is a Commonwealth realm with politics shaped by kinship and village ties. Port Moresby recognises Indonesian sovereignty over West Papua while border communities support West Papuan separatism. The 1986 Treaty of Mutual Respect binds both states to peaceful settlement (Article 2) and non-use of force (Article 7), but left refugee status unresolved and produced no Indonesian assurance against hot pursuit. Australia and Papua New Guinea signed a mutual defence treaty in October 2025.

**Military.** Indonesia maintains an estimated 20,000 troops throughout West Papua under Kodam XVII/Cenderawasih, supported by at least two squadrons of attack aircraft, helicopter and fixed-wing, and continues to fortify border outposts. Papua New Guinea Defence Force strength is approximately 3,600, with company-size elements at Kiunga and Vanimo. OPM fields 2,500 to 3,000 members, at least 2,000 armed.

**Economic.** Resource extraction was 28 percent of Papua New Guinea gross domestic product as of 2018, and extractive resources are 86 percent of exports; Ok Tedi mine resupply through Kiunga dominates the upper Fly basin. United States tariffs on Indonesian exports stand at 19 percent, with a stated intention to raise the rate should Papua New Guinea territory be violated.

**Social.** Papua New Guinea holds 17 million people, 85 percent rural. The 2011 census recorded 95.6 percent Christian identification, Catholics 26.0 percent, and a March 2025 amendment recognised Papua New Guinea as a Christian country; approximately 87 percent of Indonesians are Muslim. Western Province holds roughly 5 persons per square kilometre. The Komavi-area population is Awin and Yonggom, with kin ties across the border.

**Information.** Over 800 languages are spoken, English being official. Indonesian information operations are assessed to be directed at limiting United States and Australian involvement. OPM speaks externally through Sebby Sambom. Indonesian authorities imposed a region-wide internet blackout during the 2019 unrest at Wamena, Sorong, and Jayapura.

**Infrastructure.** Five highways exist, only two reaching the interior; over 500 airstrips, most unpaved; and 22 international ports. Lae Port handles about half of all international cargo.

# ENVIRONMENTAL EFFECTS

## 2.1 Terrain — Komavi area (OAKOC)

**Observation and fields of fire.** Closed-canopy rainforest restricts ground observation to under 50 metres, confining direct-fire engagement to trails, clearings, and river banks. The outlying hills north and west hold the only elevated observation over Komavi and the approach from the border 11 miles west.

**Avenues of approach.** Three ground avenues lead in from the west and northwest, all foot and trail movement through forest and swamp, none trafficable to wheeled vehicles. The Fly River, the waterborne avenue from the southeast, is not navigable within 20 miles of Komavi. Air avenues are constrained by cloud rather than terrain.

**Key terrain.** The Komavi airstrip, the only prepared landing surface within 42 air miles; the trail junctions east of the border; the outlying hills reported to hold OPM camps; and the head of navigation at Kiunga.

**Obstacles.** Continuous forest, swamp, and unbridged watercourses, with Second World War unexploded ordnance a significant hazard. The absence of any road across the 190 air miles between Daru and Komavi is the most consequential terrain feature in the joint operations area.

**Cover and concealment.** Canopy and undergrowth give near-continuous concealment, cover from direct fire being limited to terrain folds and watercourse banks. The displaced-persons concentration is concealment of a different order, within which armed personnel are not visually separable from protected civilians.

## 2.2 Terrain — border corridor (OAKOC)

The corridor runs 760 kilometres from Vanimo to the Torres Strait littoral along the 141st meridian, and between 04°30'S and 09°00'S consists of low-relief rainforest and seasonal swamp with no crossing infrastructure. Observation is limited to cut lines, river reaches, and fortified outposts. Indonesian avenues of approach originate on the Tanah Merah and Merauke–Mappi axes. Key terrain is the outpost line and the few trails able to carry battalion-scale foot movement; obstacles are natural and concealment favours dismounted infiltration.

Cloud base in the headwaters lies below 1,500 feet above ground level on 60 percent of mornings, the visual meteorological conditions window typically opening between 1000 and 1500 local.

## 2.4 Effects on aviation

Rotary-wing and short take-off and landing operations in the Fly headwaters are compressed into the 1000–1500 local window on approximately 60 percent of days, morning and late afternoon sitting below approach minima at unlit strips. Komavi, Kiunga, and Daru have neither night capability nor bulk fuel. Indonesian fixed-wing aviation operates from Sentani, 265 nautical miles from Komavi, with 14 hardened revetments, 8 open parking positions, and an assessed 24 to 30 sorties per day in surge, 14 sustained; attack helicopters operate from Timika, 210 nautical miles out. Canopy limits the effect of air-delivered fires, and village proximity to suspected camps raises collateral-damage risk.

## 2.5 Effects on ground movement

Off improved roads the ground is a sticky morass of mud most of the year. Cross-border movement in either direction is therefore dismounted, trail-bound, and slow, favouring light infantry, special operations elements, and insurgent groups over motorised formations. Trafficability collapses within hours of heavy rain.

## 2.6 Effects on riverine and littoral movement

The Fly River is navigable from Daru to Kiunga over 510 river miles, or 820 kilometres, at a controlling depth of 4.5 metres in the wet season falling to 2.4 metres from June through November; upriver transit takes four to five days, downriver three. Three-thousand deadweight-tonne Ok Tedi barges reach Kiunga year-round; above Kiunga the channel carries shallow-draft workboats only. The Daru wharf carries 3.5 metres alongside depth. Torres Strait controlling depth is approximately 12 metres, with mandatory pilotage in the Prince of Wales Channel. Indonesia maintains over 100 armed fast boats of 80 to 100 tonnes suited to such waters.

## 2.7 Effects on communications

Terrain masking denies reliable line-of-sight very-high-frequency and ultra-high-frequency paths between the Komavi area, Kiunga, and Daru, forcing dependence on satellite and high-frequency links. Equatorial ionospheric behaviour and daily convection produce diurnal high-frequency instability and scintillation on satellite links. Sustained 88 percent humidity accelerates connector and cable failure; commercial infrastructure in Western Province is negligible.

## 2.8 Civil considerations — ASCOPE

**Areas.** The Komavi displaced-persons concentration, approximately 10,000 West Papuans arrived February 2026; the outlying hills reported to hold OPM camps; the Kiunga river terminal; Daru; Port Moresby, holding thousands of internally displaced persons; and customary land, 97 percent of Papua New Guinea, community-held and largely unregistered.

**Structures.** Village housing and subsistence gardens; church and mission structures, the principal assembly points and information nodes of the Fly headwaters; the Komavi airstrip; the Kiunga wharf and runway; the Daru wharf.

**Capabilities.** Subsistence agriculture supports the resident population but not a sudden increment of 10,000; food, shelter, clothing, and medical capacity at Komavi is exhausted. Distribution of humanitarian supplies outside the capital region is a major problem.

**Organizations.** USAID with non-governmental and international organizations flowing into Port Moresby; the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, the Komavi population holding prima facie refugee status; the Catholic Church; Awin and Yonggom clan authorities; and OPM structures within the displaced population.

**People.** Awin and Yonggom clans with kin ties spanning the border, whose members cross for customary purposes and shelter West Papuan separatists; the displaced West Papuan population at Komavi; over 2,000 American citizens in Papua New Guinea, under a Department of State advisory to depart voluntarily; and traditional inhabitants of the Torres Strait.

**Events.** The National Liberation Army declaration of war and refugee movement of February 2026; the 11 February 2026 Smart Air killings and same-day Freeport Indonesia convoy attack; planting and harvest cycles; and church observances, which concentrate population.

# EVALUATE THE ADVERSARY

## 3.1 Indonesian doctrinal template — limited border offensive

Indonesian practice in a limited border offensive, assessed from the laydown in the Adversary Order of Battle, follows a recognisable sequence. Territorial structures — Korem, Kodim, and Koramil to sub-district level, approximately 4,800 personnel — establish population control and human-source coverage before any movement. Rotational border battalions under Kostrad Perbatasan, three totalling approximately 2,200 personnel, thicken the outpost line and secure departure areas. Kopassus conducts reconnaissance and direct action against identified camps ahead of conventional movement. Fixed-wing aviation from Sentani establishes local air superiority and Mi-35P from Timika support the ground scheme. Infantry battalions of Kodam XVII/Cenderawasih then advance on a few trail-bound axes to seize and clear a defined belt of terrain rather than hold ground indefinitely, with Brimob — approximately 2,600 across the Papua provinces at MODERATE confidence — following to screen the population. Throughout, Jakarta frames the operation as counter-insurgency within a claimed treaty gap.

## 3.2 Indonesian capability summary

The Indonesian Army fields approximately 300,000 active personnel across 13 military area commands, of which Kodam XVII/Cenderawasih oversees West Papua with an estimated 20,000 troops; six infantry battalions are reported at Manokwari, Jayapura, and Sorong, with additional battalions at Tanah Merah, Mappi, and Merauke. Reinforcement beyond West Papua is assessed to be limited, Indonesian forces being occupied with internal issues and Jakarta security.

The Indonesian Air Force operates over 100 combat-capable aircraft, including 26 F-16A A/B, 5 Su-27SK/SKM, 11 Su-30MK/MK2, 6 Mi-35P, 8 AH-64A, and 30 BAE Hawk; one squadron of Su-30 and F-16 A/B is assessed at Jayapura Air Base, with at least four Mi-35 at Timika.

Naval forces include three Marine Corps brigades, the nearest at Surabaya four days away by amphibious ship; 33 frigates and corvettes, of which 3 corvettes and 6 frigates are assessed at Manokwari; and over 100 armed fast boats.

## 3.3 OPM and NLA patterns of operation

OPM formed in 1965, received Libyan funding from 1969, and has since built an indigenous cadre arming disparate West Papuan groups. The National Liberation Army emerged in February 2026 as the armed wing following unification of those groups, declaring war on Indonesia to drive Indonesian forces from West Papua and shut down foreign resource extraction. The stated OPM objective is unification of West Papua with Papua New Guinea. OPM is not a designated foreign terrorist organization.

Five patterns recur: ambush of security-force convoys, including the killing of Major General I Gusti Putu Danny Karya Nugraha, head of the Papua intelligence agency, on 25 April 2021; attacks on civil aviation as the sole reliable access to the interior, the Susi Air burning of 7 February 2023 with the New Zealand pilot held until 21 September 2024 and the Smart Air killings of 11 February 2026; attacks on extractive-industry personnel, as against the Freeport Indonesia convoy on 11 February 2026; mass-casualty attacks on infrastructure workers, as at Nduga on 1 December 2018; and internationalisation of grievance through spokesman statements.

## 3.4 OPM and NLA capability summary

OPM is assessed at 2,500 to 3,000 committed members, at least 2,000 armed in small groups with small arms, rocket-propelled grenades, and light mortars. Numerous camps and caches are reported in the outlying hills east of the border, unconfirmed. Expected behaviour is avoidance of eradication by both Indonesia and Papua New Guinea and blending with the displaced population. The critical enabler is local sympathy and support from the peoples of Papua New Guinea.

# ADVERSARY COURSES OF ACTION

## 4.1 Indonesian COA 1 — Coercive demonstration and limited raiding (MOST LIKELY)

- **Purpose.** Compel Papua New Guinea to accept joint border security cooperation and renounce OPM sanctuary, without an acknowledged invasion and without unambiguously engaging the Australia–Papua New Guinea treaty.

- **Method.** Continued fortification of the outpost line by Kostrad Perbatasan; visible massing of Kodam XVII/Cenderawasih infantry at Tanah Merah and Merauke; combat air patrol from Sentani with deliberate airspace incursions; Kopassus raids of 24 to 72 hours against suspected camps within 15 miles of the border; diplomacy offering ASEAN sponsorship for cooperation.

- **End state.** Papua New Guinea accedes to joint border security operations; OPM sanctuary becomes untenable; outside involvement is deterred as disproportionate.

- **Indicators.** Drummed fuel and ammunition moving forward to Tanah Merah; Kopassus rotary-wing activity out of Timika above rotation norms; Sentani sortie rate touching the 24-to-30 surge band briefly only; no movement of the Surabaya Marine brigade.

- **Timeline.** Preparation continuous; demonstration D-day to D+7; raiding episodic to D+21; diplomacy beyond D+21.

## 4.2 Indonesian COA 2 — Ground offensive to seize a border security zone (MOST DANGEROUS)

- **Purpose.** Search for, isolate, and destroy OPM sanctuary camps by occupying a belt of Papua New Guinea territory, accepting armed conflict with any force attempting to repel the movement.

- **Method.** Local air superiority over the Komavi area by Su-30 and F-16 A/B from Sentani at surge rates; simultaneous heliborne insertion of Kopassus and one infantry battalion supported by Mi-35P from Timika; ground advance of two to three infantry battalions from the Tanah Merah axis to hold a security zone 15 to 20 miles deep east of the border, including Komavi; clearance of the outlying hills and Brimob screening of the displaced population.

- **End state.** OPM camps and caches east of the border destroyed; a security zone occupied and defended; Papua New Guinea returned to negotiation on Jakarta terms.

- **Indicators.** Sentani aircraft dispersing into the 14 hardened revetments and a sustained shift from 14 to 24 or more sorties per day; all six Mi-35P forward rather than the four assessed at Timika; amphibious shipping moving from Surabaya toward the Arafura Sea; medical and engineer support concentrating at Tanah Merah; the three Satgas Pamtas battalions consolidating to static frontage.

- **Timeline.** Preparation D-14 to D-3; air phase and heliborne insertion D-day to D+2; ground advance to D+5; clearance to D+21; consolidation or withdrawal thereafter. Earliest assessed readiness is not before mid-July 2026.

## 4.3 Indonesian COA 3 — Protracted deniable pressure and maritime encirclement (ALTERNATIVE)

- **Purpose.** Impose cumulative cost on Papua New Guinea and the OPM sanctuary indefinitely while staying below the threshold at which the Australia–Papua New Guinea treaty is unambiguously engaged.

- **Method.** No declared offensive. Deniable cross-border patrolling by small Kopassus and territorial-command elements justified as hot pursuit, exploiting the absence of any Indonesian assurance against hot pursuit in the 1986 treaty; proxy militia cultivated from cross-border Awin and Yonggom kin networks; sustained maritime pressure in the Arafura Sea and western Torres Strait approaches by armed fast boats, Manokwari-based combatants, and submarine presence against the shallow approaches to Daru and the Fly River mouth; deniable airspace and territorial-water violations; information operations exploiting Papua New Guinea sensitivities over sovereignty and the cost of the displaced population.

- **End state.** Papua New Guinea capacity and will to host the displaced population are exhausted over months; OPM sanctuary collapses without an invasion; no single attributable act triggers outside intervention.

- **Indicators.** Unattributed armed incidents east of the border without Indonesian force signature; Brimob growth in western border districts without infantry movement; increased fast-boat activity in the Arafura Sea and Fly approaches; Indonesian survey or fisheries activity inside Papua New Guinea waters; unchanged Sentani sortie rates.

- **Timeline.** Continuous, no discrete D-day; assessed effects horizon three to nine months.

## 4.4 OPM and NLA COA 1 — Dispersal and concealment among the displaced (MOST LIKELY)

- **Purpose.** Preserve force and sanctuary through the crisis, avoiding eradication by Indonesian or Papua New Guinea forces.

- **Method.** Dispersal into cells of squad size or smaller across the outlying hills; weapons cached separately from personnel; cadre intermingled with the 10,000 displaced persons at Komavi; reliance on Awin and Yonggom kin networks for food, shelter, and early warning; suspension of action east of the border while low-level operations continue to the west.

- **End state.** OPM survives with force structure intact and the Komavi sanctuary preserved; the West Papua question remains internationally visible.

- **Indicators.** Falling incident tempo east of the border; small groups moving from the Komavi settlement into the outlying hills; increased food purchase at Kiunga and in upper Fly villages; absence of the Morning Star flag in the displaced area; messaging emphasising refugee protection rather than armed action.

- **Timeline.** Immediate and continuous; adjusted within 24 to 72 hours of observed Indonesian movement toward the border.

## 4.5 OPM and NLA COA 2 — Deliberate escalation to foment a border war (MOST DANGEROUS)

- **Purpose.** Force international dialogue on West Papuan independence or unification by provoking open armed conflict between Papua New Guinea and Indonesia.

- **Method.** Cross-border attacks from Papua New Guinea territory against Indonesian outposts and patrols, timed and sited to guarantee retaliation into Papua New Guinea; attacks or hostage-taking against foreign nationals, civil aviation, or mine infrastructure to internationalise the crisis; armed elements positioned inside the displaced-persons concentration so that an Indonesian strike produces refugee casualties; spokesman statements framing any Indonesian response as genocide.

- **End state.** Indonesian forces cross the border in strength; Australia is drawn in under treaty obligation; the West Papua question reaches an international audience on terms unattainable by insurgency alone.

- **Indicators.** Resumption of attacks originating east of the border after a quiet period; attacks against foreign nationals or aviation in Western Province; messaging shifting from refugee protection to calls for international intervention; concentration rather than dispersal of armed elements near the displaced area.

- **Timeline.** Executable on 72 hours' notice; most likely triggered by Indonesian and Papua New Guinea convergence on joint border security arrangements, or by the onset of repatriation at Komavi.

## Annex A — Bilateral and Multilateral Instruments

### A.1 Agreement on Defense Cooperation (United States – Papua New Guinea), 2023

**Parties.** The United States and the Independent State of Papua New Guinea.

**Date.** Signed 2023.

**Operative provision, Article 5, as carried in the Strategic Context.** The Agreement commits the United States to protect and secure United States personnel in Papua New Guinea and thus provides the United States the ability to cooperate in the defence of Papua New Guinea territory from foreign invasion.

**Scope on its face.** Article 5 addresses two subjects. The first subject is the protection and security of United States personnel in Papua New Guinea. The second subject is cooperation in the defence of Papua New Guinea territory from foreign invasion. The geographic frame of both subjects is the territory of Papua New Guinea. The Agreement is bilateral; no third state is a party. The persons identified by the President are United States military personnel, contractors and citizens, and the physical property of those persons.

### A.2 Treaty of Mutual Respect, Cooperation and Friendship (Indonesia – Papua New Guinea), 27 October 1986

**Parties.** The Republic of Indonesia and the Independent State of Papua New Guinea.

**Date.** Signed 27 October 1986, following talks on rights and obligations along the shared border.

**Article 2, quoted.** The parties agreed to "avoid, reduce, and contain disputes or conflicts between their nations and settle any differences that may arise only by peaceful means."

**Article 7, quoted.** The parties promised that "they shall not threaten or use force against each other."

**Scope on its face.** A bilateral non-aggression instrument, binding on Indonesia and Papua New Guinea only, and a stated basis for a lasting structure of peace and cooperation between the parties.

**Matters left unresolved by the 1986 Treaty.**

1.  West Papuan refugees fleeing insurgent violence and crossing into Papua New Guinea. Not resolved by the Treaty.

2.  Joint security operations in the border regions. Papua New Guinea refused to agree to joint operations.

3.  Cross-border movement by Indonesian forces in response to insurgent operations mounted from Papua New Guinea territory, or in hot pursuit of OPM insurgents. Indonesia avoided categorical assurance against such crossing.

### A.3 Mutual defence treaty (Australia – Papua New Guinea), October 2025

**Parties.** Australia and the Independent State of Papua New Guinea.

**Date.** Signed October 2025.

**Operative content, close paraphrase.** The treaty secures the mutual defence and security interests of the two states and affirms that each will come to the aid of the other in defence of sovereignty.

**Scope on its face.** Bilateral. The subject is the defence of the sovereignty of the parties; the United States is not a party. The Regional Intelligence Summary and the Commander's Initial Planning Guidance both record Australia as near certain to honor the treaty and deploy forces in the defence of Papua New Guinea sovereignty.

### A.4 AUKUS, September 2021

**Parties.** Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States.

**Date.** Launched September 2021.

**Operative content, close paraphrase.** A trilateral security partnership in two pillars. Under Pillar I the United States is to provide Australia nuclear-powered submarines to replace the ageing Australian fleet, and Australia is to provide submarine basing access. Pillar II provides for collaboration across artificial intelligence and autonomy, quantum technologies, undersea robotics, hypersonic missiles, advanced cyber and electronic warfare, and for integration of the defence industrial bases of the three parties.

**Scope on its face.** The subject matter is capability development, technology sharing and industrial integration among the three parties. Papua New Guinea is not a party, although Papua New Guinea is, with Australia, a Commonwealth nation recognising Charles III as king.

### A.5 Compacts of Free Association (United States – FSM, RMI, Palau)

**Parties.** The United States and, respectively, the Federated States of Micronesia, the Republic of the Marshall Islands and the Republic of Palau.

**Operative content, close paraphrase.** The Compacts guide the relationships between the United States and the three Compact states, including United States obligations for the defence of those states. In return, the Compacts provide assured access in a contingency and authority to grant or deny access to the military forces of another nation.

**Scope on its face.** The instruments govern relations between the United States and each Compact state and cover the territory of those states. The third-nation access authority supports a strategic line of communication across the Pacific.

### A.6 Treaty of Amity and Cooperation in Southeast Asia, 1976

**Parties.** The ASEAN member states, together with acceding parties. Indonesia is a founding ASEAN member state.

**Date.** Concluded 1976. Papua New Guinea acceded as a non-voting observer by the 1987 ASEAN decision.

**Operative principles, close paraphrase.** The High Contracting Parties are guided by mutual respect for the independence, sovereignty and territorial integrity of all nations; the right of every state to lead a national existence free from external interference, subversion or coercion; non-interference in the internal affairs of one another; settlement of disputes by peaceful means; and renunciation of the threat or use of force.

**Scope on its face.** The instrument governs relations among the High Contracting Parties. Papua New Guinea participates as a non-voting observer following accession, not as an ASEAN member state.

**Associated status.** Papua New Guinea acceded in 1987 as the first non-ASEAN country to do so, and has applied for full membership. Since 1992, member states have routinely blocked that application.

### A.7 Torres Strait Treaty (Australia – Papua New Guinea), 1978

**Parties.** Australia and the Independent State of Papua New Guinea.

**Date.** Signed 1978.

**Operative content, close paraphrase.** The treaty delimits the maritime boundaries between the two states in the Torres Strait, establishes a Protected Zone, provides for free movement and traditional activities by the traditional inhabitants of that zone without passport or visa formality, and protects the traditional livelihood of those inhabitants and the marine environment.

**Scope on its face.** Bilateral, and limited to the Torres Strait and the Protected Zone. The subject matter is boundaries, traditional inhabitant movement, fisheries and environmental protection. The Torres Strait is 90 miles across at the closest point between New Guinea and Australia, with a controlling depth of approximately 12 metres and compulsory pilotage in the Prince of Wales Channel.

## Annex C — Designations and Their Legal Consequence

### C.1 Free Papua Movement

The United States has not designated the Free Papua Movement (OPM) as a foreign terrorist organization.

Designation as a foreign terrorist organization is made by the Secretary of State under section 219 of the Immigration and Nationality Act. A designation in force engages the material-support offences at 18 U.S.C. §§ 2339A and 2339B, immigration exclusion and removal provisions applicable to members and representatives of the entity, and blocking obligations on United States financial institutions holding funds of the entity. Absent a designation, those authorities are not engaged.

The National Liberation Army (NLA) is the armed wing of OPM, formed on unification of the armed insurgent groups of West Papua and declaring war against Indonesia in February 2026.

### C.2 Refugee status of the West Papuan population at Komavi

Papua New Guinea is a party to the 1951 Convention relating to the Status of Refugees and to the 1967 Protocol, subject to reservations. Those reservations qualify Convention articles bearing on wage-earning employment, public education, freedom of movement, naturalisation, and penalties for unlawful presence and expulsion (Articles 17(1), 21, 22(1), 26, 31, 32 and 34).

The approximately 10,000 West Papuans who crossed the international border in February 2026 and settled in the Komavi area hold prima facie refugee status, recognised on a group basis by reason of the circumstances of flight rather than by individual determination.

The principal consequence of Convention status is Article 33, under which no contracting state shall expel or return a refugee to the frontiers of territories where life or freedom would be threatened on account of race, religion, nationality, social group or political opinion. Convention obligations attach to the contracting state, which for the Komavi population is Papua New Guinea.

### C.3 Internally displaced persons

Persons displaced within the territory of their own state are not refugees within the meaning of the 1951 Convention. The reference framework for the displaced population in Port Moresby is the United Nations Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement, which are not a treaty.

## Annex D — Rules of Engagement and Use-of-Force Framework

### D.1 Standing baseline

CJCSI 3121.01B, Standing Rules of Engagement / Standing Rules for the Use of Force for US Forces, applies to all United States forces during all military operations and contingencies. The instrument establishes the policies governing the actions of United States commanders and gives implementation guidance on the inherent right of self-defence and the application of force for mission accomplishment.

### D.2 Self-defence

- **Inherent right and obligation.** Unit commanders always retain the right and obligation to exercise unit self-defence against a hostile act or demonstrated hostile intent.

- **Categories.** National, collective, unit and individual self-defence. Collective self-defence of designated non-United States forces, persons and property is exercised only as authorised by the President or the Secretary.

- **Triggers.** A hostile act is an attack or other use of force against United States forces or designated persons and property. Hostile intent is the threat of imminent use of force.

- **Limits.** Necessity and proportionality govern. Force is limited in intensity, duration and magnitude to that required to decisively counter the hostile act or intent and ensure continued protection.

- **Declaration of a hostile force.** Declaration authority is retained at the levels specified in the Standing Rules of Engagement.

### D.3 Supplemental measures and law of war

Supplemental measures are requested, approved and promulgated through the chain of command; measures not delegated remain with the approving authority; and such measures neither limit nor expand the inherent right of self-defence. Department of Defense policy separately requires compliance with the law of war in all armed conflicts and in all other military operations, on the principles of military necessity, humanity, distinction, proportionality and honour.

### D.4 Presidential authorization as stated

The Strategic Context and the Commander's Initial Planning Guidance record the President's public statement: that the United States would pursue peaceful settlement of all regional disputes and does not wish to take sides between Indonesia, OPM separatists and Papua New Guinea; that the United States will honor the 2023 Agreement on Defense Cooperation; and that protection of United States military personnel, contractors, citizens and property is a top priority. The President further emphasized that while the United States did not wish to get involved in the internal activities of either Indonesia or Papua New Guinea, the President "would authorize military force to protect U.S. personnel and non-combatants, contain the situation to the immediate area, and to de-escalate the conflict."

1\. (E) **SITUATION** (Ref. Regional Intelligence Summary)

1.  \(E\) Two of our Pacific partners, Indonesia and Papua New Guinea (PNG), are in a dispute over the armed insurgent group National Liberation Army (NLA) of the Free Papua Movement (OPM), who has simultaneously “declared war” on Indonesia while seeking sanctuary in the rugged western frontier of PNG, just across the porous and unsecure international border. The regional violence in West Papua has resulted in thousands of people seeking relative safety on the PNG side of the border. There are unconfirmed reports that large numbers of well-equipped insurgents crossed with them and have established numerous camps and weapons’ caches in the outlying hills on the PNG side. Indonesia has accused PNG of failing to secure its border and deny sanctuary to OPM and in response, has fortified its outposts along the West Papua–PNG border. They now have an estimated 20,000 troops throughout West Papua, supported by at least two squadrons of attack aircraft (helicopters and fixed-wing) and national intelligence agencies, in consultation with the PACOM J-2, have assessed that Indonesian forces are preparing for a limited offensive into PNG to eliminate OPM/NLA strongholds and camps. The U.S. Ambassador in Port Moresby reports that the PNG Prime Minister is convinced that an Indonesian invasion of Papuan territory is imminent. The Prime Minister is requesting immediate U.S. and Australian intervention to deter such an attack, or, if an invasion occurs, assistance in repelling the Indonesians.

2.  \(E\) Indonesia refuses to recognize any possibility of West Papuan independence or of West Papua joining PNG: this is a red line for them. Indonesia will also not allow OPM to find safe havens in PNG territory where they can continue carrying out a violent insurgency in West Papua. Indonesia desires that PNG cooperate with Indonesia in securing the international border. Not only will this render OPM largely impotent, but this enhances Indonesian position as the dominant power on New Guinea and a leading nation in the South Pacific. In return for PNG cooperation, Indonesia will likely sponsor PNG’s full membership in ASEAN. Likely strategic objectives of Indonesia:

> :

1.  \(E\) Coerce PNG cooperation through military power demonstration at the border.

2.  \(E\) Should coercion fail, conduct a limited ground offensive to search for, isolate, and destroy OPM sanctuary camps.

3.  \(E\) Conduct diplomacy and operations in the information environment to limit U.S. and Australian involvement in support of PNG.

3.  \(E\) The strategic Center of Gravity of Indonesia is their formidable diplomatic leverage as the world’s largest Muslim-majority democracy and as one of the most influential diplomatic players in Asia. This is due to its strict “active alignment” policy to avoid taking sides between China and the U.S. They can sustain this due to their geostrategic position as a bridge between the Indian and Pacific oceans; their resource abundance which provides them defense against sanctions and embargoes; and their governing of vital sea chokepoints such as Lombok, Sunda, and the Straits of Malacca through which 90% of global maritime traffic in the Pacific must pass. Their large global share of EV components makes them an almost indispensable partner to the U.S. and China.

4.  \(E\) Our President has made it clear that American citizens, military personnel currently in PNG, contractors, and their physical property will be protected. He further stated that the United States would pursue peaceful settlement of all regional disputes and does not wish to take sides in this dispute between Indonesia, OPM separatists, and PNG; however, the U.S. will honor the 2023 Agreement on Defense Cooperation with PNG. Finally, the President emphasized that while the U.S. did not wish to get involved in the internal activities of either Indonesia or PNG, he would authorize military force to protect U.S. personnel and non-combatants, contain the situation to the immediate area, and to de-escalate the conflict.

5.  \(E\) Indonesia’s immediate adversary is OPM and their objectives are clearly stated: West Papua region unification with PNG. To accomplish this, they will likely attempt to avoid both Indonesian and PNG attempts to eradicate their presence in the PNG Kamavi area and blend in with the thousands of IDPs. Their most dangerous course would be to foment a full scale war between PNG and Indonesia, forcing international dialogue on a free West Papua or unification with PNG.

6.  \(E\) PNG, on their own, cannot hope to repel an Indonesian invasion, however limited. They can, however, count on Australia’s obligation by treaty. It is this clash of arms that represents the most dangerous escalation of violence as Australia has formidable military capabilities and is an ally of both the U.K. and the U.S. PNG’s objective is to dissuade an Indonesian invasion altogether, by an allied show of force that includes Australia and possibly the U.S., and place the border and OPM issues back in the negotiating room.

2\. **(E) Strategic Environment.** The United States is allied or partnered with most nations of the South Pacific region and East Pacific islands, including Indonesia and PNG. (See Regional Intelligence Summary).

1.  \(E\) Outside of Indonesia and PNG, the most important actor is Australia, an ally of both the U.S. and PNG. In October 2025, Australia and PNG signed a mutual defense treaty that secured their mutual defence and security interests and affirmed that each would come to the other’s aid in defense of sovereignty.[^1] The Royal Australian Navy (RAN) deploys two amphibious assault ships, three AEGIS-capable destroyers, and several dozen frigates, submarines, and multirole vessels. The Royal Australian Army has one brigade (3rd BDE) on ready status for deployment with two additional active brigades (1st and 7th BDE). Australia maintains six reserve brigades available for call-up. It is near certainty that Australia will honor their defense treaty with fellow commonwealth nation PNG and deploy forces to assist PNG in the defense of their sovereignty.

2.  \(E\) In addition, <span class="mark">AUKUS (Australian, UK, US) is a trilateral security pact between Australia, the UK, and the US, launched in September 2021. This is significant since PNG is a Commonwealth nation that recognizes UK Charles III as king, along with Australia. Its core focus is countering China's influence in the Indo-Pacific region through two "pillars", Nuclear-Powered Submarines and Advanced Capabilities. While neither Indonesia or PNG wishes an armed conflict, PACOM’s involvement with the military forces of AUKUS provides a significant deterrent to keep the situation below the threshold of armed conflict.</span>

3.  \(E\) Other key partners include the East Pacific Islands (Oceana). Our agreements give the U.S. authority to grant or deny access to another nation’s military forces which allows the U.S. to maintain a clear strategic line of communication across the Pacific. PACOM will continue to maintain liaison with the various East Pacific Islands to keep out line of communication secure.

4.  \(E\) Finally, both ASEAN and the Pacific Islands Forum (PIF) provide additional political organizations familiar with PACOM that may be able to defuse the current crisis. PNG is a formal member of PIF and Indonesia is a member of ASEAN.

5.  \(E\) Strategic Environment / desired state. The strategic endstate is that Indonesia does not successfully invade PNG territory and that American citizens, military personnel currently in PNG, contractors, and their physical property will be protected. PNG, with international assistance, repatriates West Papuan refugees and begins expelling OMP separatists from PNG territory.

**3. (E) Operational environment (OE) / current state**. (See Country Fact Sheet) The geography and climate of New Guinea represent a formidable challenge. Infrastructure is minimal and roads are non-existent in remote areas: air and rivers is how the local travel. The humidity, heat, and tropical rainforest will ruin troops and equipment in short order, without the enemy ever firing a shot.

1.  \(E\) Likely operational objectives of the Indonesian armed forces:

    1.  \(E\) Establish forward bases along the international border with PNG.

    2.  \(E\) Establish local air superiority over the Komavi area.

    3.  \(E\) Isolate and destroy OPM camp locations.

2.  \(E\) There are two Operational Centers of Gravity:

    1.  \(E\) Indonesian Army. The Indonesian Army is quite capable and acclimated to the region. They are well supported by air and fires. They are vulnerable, however, in areas of sustainment, which require air and ground transport and air and ground-based bases along the way. In the air domain, Indonesia is particularly vulnerable to the advanced capabilities of the Australian and U.S. air forces.

    2.  \(E\) Local support to OMP. OMP cannot sustain their presence in PNG territory without wide sympathy and support from the peoples of PNG. OMP is vulnerable in this area: if support dries up, they will have to return to West Papua where their local support bases are.

3.  \(E\) Desired OE future state. Indonesian and PNG/Allies on their respective sides of a secure border. OMP finds no sanctuary in Komavi and is coerced into returning to West Papua.

## 2. (E) **Operational Problem**

1.  \(E\) Problem statement. The Komavi area is one of the remotest locations in all of Papua New Guinea. There is no infrastructure linking Komavi to the rest of PNG. The operational problem is overcoming the challenges of distance with sufficient force in a rapid manner to deter or repel an Indonesian invasion of the area, and then sustaining that force in a remote location for enough time for negotiations to restart between Indonesia and PNG. Finally, to assist the PNG military in a longer duration counterinsurgency, a small coalition footprint must be established, sustained, and maintained to provide the PNG Army training, equipment, and expertise in ejecting OMP separatists.

2.  \(E\) Commander’s initial analysis of the problem. It will take 7-10 days for sufficient naval forces to reach the area. While Lae and Port Moresby have sufficient infrastructure as APOD/SPODs, intertheater travel will be difficult. While coalition air forces can obtain air supremacy, the tropical jungle will minimize their tactical effect on either Indonesian ground troops and OPM insurgents. As well, collateral damage to PNG villages is counterproductive to U.S./PNG desired endstate. Infrastructure must be constructed in order to increase coalition flow of troops and equipment; this will require an air bridge to Komavi to rapidly deploy ground troops. Finally, the deterrence or defeat of an Indonesian invasion leaves the root cause unaddressed: OPM must be dealt with by the PNG and currently, they lack the capabilities and equipment to do this. Local sympathies also rest with the separatists as the Komavi peoples straddle both sides of the border. In order to deny OPM sanctuary in the future, sympathies and local support has to change. Our own operational COG is our ability to project forces to remote areas: a key strength relying on air capabilities, specialized forces, and logistics - to include construction capabilities. We are vulnerable, however, in the area of force protection: our troops and equipment simply are not well-acclimated to the region and there is little time in which to get them acclimated.

    2.  \(E\) PACOM military objectives are to protect military personnel and American citizens and their property in Papua New Guinea; prevent Indonesian armed forces from successfully invading PNG territory; and to assist PNG armed forces in denying sanctuary to OPM.

    3.  \(e\) PACOM operational objectives:

4.  \(E\) Directed Operational Concept. The overarching purpose of this operation is to keep/return Indonesian armed forces to their side of the international border, secure it, and then build PNG’s capacity and capability to deny OPM sanctuary, which is the root cause of the current crisis. The decisive operation is the rapid forward deployment of at least a brigade of ground troops, coordinated with PNG armed forces, to the defense of the Komavi area, supported by air. Obtaining and maintaining air superiority over the international border is decisive, as this will seriously degrade any Indonesian invasion as well as provide coalition forces protection and uninterrupted sustainment by air. Concurrent with this is the establishment of bases at Lae (Nadzab Airfield) and Dura to facilitate force flow and build critical logistics for sustainment and construction of infrastructure forward in Komvari. ## 4. (**E) CDRUSPACOM Initial Intent**

1.  \(E\) Purpose. To keep/return Indonesian armed forces to their side of the international border, secure it, and then build PNG’s capacity and capability to deny OPM sanctuary, which is the root cause of the current crisis.

2.  \(E\) Method. Establish air superiority over the border and Komvari area at earliest possible time. Deploy forward a credible ground force to defend Komvari from invasion while building base support and infrastructure to facilitate follow-on force flow. Conduct special operations activities to build PNG capacity and capability while managing operations in the information environment to control the narrative. I envision an Australian-PNG led ground operation supported by air, cyber, and space provided by JTF SOUTHERN SHIELD. However, U.S. ground forces must be postured to follow and support or follow and assume the defense mission as well as build and protect bases and infrastructure.

3.  \(E\) Endstate. Return to peaceful resolution of the border issues and negotiated solution to the OPM issue.

4.  \(E\) Risk. The current probability of achieving military objectives is questionable based on the following: the extreme difficulties presented by New Guinea geography and equatorial climate. Available forces are minimal and Indonesia has the strategic initiative. Finally, OPM has found sanctuary in PNG due largely to the local people’s sympathies. Mitigation of risk must include rapid formation of a regional coalition to force Indonesia into calculating the military cost of invading PNG; rapid obtaining air superiority over the contested area, and rapid construction of infrastructure to mitigate the effects of the harsh environment and climate.

Once Commander’s Estimate is reviewed and approved by CJCS/SECWAR/POTUS, we will rapidly transition the selected COA into a Level 4 CONPLAN. Ensure that a NEO branch plan is fully developed and ready to execute prior to N+7.

Host-nation facilities are Port Moresby General, Angau Memorial at Lae, Daru General, Kiunga District and Rumginae Rural, the last two with no surgical capability beyond basic procedures and no blood bank. No medical facility of any kind exists in the Komavi area.

- **Evacuation chain and distances.** Komavi area to Daru, 190 air miles, no roads at all; Daru to Port Moresby, 275 air miles; Port Moresby to Lae, 200 air miles, none with a direct road. Daru to Kiunga is 165 air miles or 510 river miles; Lae to Guam, 1,400 nautical miles. Aeromedical evacuation is coordinated by the theater patient movement requirements centre; no aeromedical evacuation crews or en-route care teams appear on the US Force List for Planning. Casualty evacuation in the operational area is limited to Papua New Guinea Defence Force leased light helicopters and mine operator contract aircraft, none medically configured.

- **Endemic disease, Western Province.** Malaria holoendemic, year-round transmission of *Plasmodium falciparum* and *Plasmodium vivax*, chloroquine resistance documented, annual parasite incidence in the Middle and North Fly districts approximately 180 cases per 1,000 population. Dengue endemic at Daru and Kiunga. Scrub typhus (*Orientia tsutsugamushi*) in grassland and secondary regrowth in the Fly headwaters. Melioidosis (*Burkholderia pseudomallei*) in soil and surface water, wet-season peak. Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis at high prevalence in the Daru area. Komavi ambient conditions: mean 27 degrees Celsius, 88 percent relative humidity sustained.

- **Preventive medicine.** No force health protection policy has been issued; existing theater guidance requires malaria chemoprophylaxis, permethrin-treated uniforms, bed nets and vector control. Preventive medicine detachment support is available at Okinawa and Guam; no preventive medicine unit appears on the US Force List for Planning. Immunisation currency across notice-to-move units is 61 percent for typhoid and 34 percent for Japanese encephalitis.

- **Blood.** Armed Services Blood Program support from Yokota and Okinawa; theater liquid group O holdings 620 units, frozen holdings at Guam 1,900. Daru and Kiunga hold no bank stock.

#### Ongoing actions

## 8. Engineer

#### Current posture

An engineering planning cell is open and base development data for Lae, Daru, Kiunga and the Komavi area is in compilation. No US engineer reconnaissance has been conducted since 2024. No engineer unit is alerted.

#### Data and holdings

- **Apportioned engineer force.** 84 EN BN, Hawaii, notice to move N+14; NMCB FIVE, Okinawa, notice to move N+7. The Papua New Guinea Defence Force engineer battalion at Lae holds explosive ordnance disposal capability. Australian engineer capability sits within 17th Sustainment Brigade.

- **Construction capacity.** 84 EN BN fields three construction companies with organic earthmoving, quarry and paving sections; sustained common excavation planned at 2,400 cubic metres per day dry and 900 under sustained rainfall. NMCB FIVE fields approximately 600 personnel and an 89-person air detachment deployable at 48 hours, with earthmoving planned at 1,800 cubic metres per day. A 32-person vertical platoon completes one pre-engineered building in nine working days.

- **Expeditionary airfield matting.** AM-2 matting at the Naval Construction Battalion Center detachment, Guam: 92,000 square feet; one complete expeditionary airfield package of 96,000 square feet at Port Hueneme. Planned installation rate is 9,000 square feet per day on a compacted subgrade of specified California bearing ratio, exclusive of subgrade preparation. Movement of matting stocks is by sealift or strategic airlift and is not currently scheduled.

- **Expeditionary airfield design criteria held by the planning cell.** Contingency airfield criteria applied by this staff are: C-17 semi-prepared assault strip, 1,065 m by 27 m at California bearing ratio 6 minimum; C-130 semi-prepared assault strip, 1,065 m by 18 m at California bearing ratio 6 minimum, reducible to 915 m for a single-ship emergency delivery at restricted gross weight; C-130 sustained-operations strip, 1,525 m by 27 m, surfaced. Rotary-wing landing zone, 50 m by 50 m cleared and level per aircraft, no bearing-ratio floor. STOL utility fixed-wing, 350 m unsurfaced. A grass or unsurfaced laterite runway loses an assessed 30 to 50 percent of dry-condition bearing ratio within 24 hours of heavy rainfall and is not restored without mechanical compaction.

- **Quarry and aggregate, Western Province.** Nearest tested hard-rock source to the Komavi area is the Ok Tedi crusher complex at Tabubil, linked to Kiunga by the mine-operated road, 85 miles; output is committed to mine operations. Alluvial gravel is available in the upper Fly and Ok Tedi river beds, quality untested. Laterite is present in the Komavi area, with no California bearing ratio testing conducted. The Daru coastal plain and Fly delta are alluvial silt and clay with no hard rock reported.

- **Water production.** NMCB FIVE holds two 3,000 gallon-per-hour reverse osmosis water purification units and four 1,500 gallon-per-hour tactical systems; 84 EN BN holds distribution and storage but no production above unit level. Surface water in the Fly headwaters is abundant year-round, with turbidity after rainfall regularly above 200 nephelometric turbidity units.

- **Other.** Unexploded ordnance from the 1942–1944 campaign remains a significant hazard nationwide, and off improved roads the ground is a sticky morass of mud for most of the year.

## 9. Staff Judge Advocate

#### Current posture

One operational law attorney is assigned to the joint planning group and one to the crisis action team. No supplemental rules of engagement have been requested or approved for this operation.

#### Data and holdings

- **Authorities.** Presidential direction, per the Strategic Context, is to protect United States military personnel, citizens, contractors and physical property in Papua New Guinea, contain the situation to the immediate area and de-escalate the conflict, while pursuing peaceful settlement and not taking sides. The 2023 Agreement on Defense Cooperation, Article 5, commits the United States to protect and secure United States personnel in Papua New Guinea and provides the ability to cooperate in the defence of Papua New Guinea territory from foreign invasion. Interpretation of Article 5 as applied to the planning requirement has been requested from the Joint Staff Office of Legal Counsel.

- **Agreements in place.** 2023 Agreement on Defense Cooperation, including status protections for United States personnel; Torres Strait Treaty of 1978; October 2025 mutual defence treaty between Australia and Papua New Guinea; 1986 Treaty of Mutual Respect, Cooperation and Friendship, Articles 2 and 7, under which refugee status, joint border security operations and assurance against hot pursuit remain unresolved. An acquisition and cross-servicing agreement is in force with Australia, none with Papua New Guinea; force posture arrangements govern access at RAAF Darwin and RAAF Tindal.

- **Rules of engagement.** The standing rules of engagement remain in force with no supplemental measures approved. A draft package is in preparation covering self-defence of United States persons and property, protection of designated persons, and convoy security. The OPM is not designated a foreign terrorist organization by the United States.

- **Claims.** Single-service claims responsibility is not assigned and no foreign claims commission is appointed. Customary land accounts for 97 percent of the country, community-held and largely unregistered.

- **Detention and displaced persons.** No detention facility exists in theater and no detainee operations capability appears on the US Force List for Planning. Papua New Guinea is a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention with reservations, and the approximately 10,000 West Papuans in the Komavi area hold prima facie refugee status.

#### Ongoing actions
