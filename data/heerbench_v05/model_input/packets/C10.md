## 1. Purpose

This document compiles the strategic guidance that frames United States military support to the Government of Japan following Super Typhoon KAGURA. It establishes why a disaster-relief mission in the southern Ryukyu Islands is a national priority, what national and theater objectives it serves, and the boundaries within which the joint force operates. It is a planning input; it does not direct execution. Execution direction is in the Execute Order chain (documents 07 through 09b).

## 2. National Security Strategy — Regional Excerpts (Synthetic)

**Overarching interest.** The United States maintains a vital national interest in a free, open, secure, and prosperous Indo-Pacific. The strength of the United States alliance network is the central asymmetric advantage in the region. Among those alliances, the United States–Japan alliance is described as the cornerstone of peace and security in the Indo-Pacific.

**Allied resilience as a strategic objective.** The Strategy directs that the United States help allies and partners build resilience to shocks — including natural disasters, which are increasing in frequency and severity in the Western Pacific — and that the United States be prepared to respond rapidly, at allied request, when a treaty ally suffers a catastrophe that exceeds its national response capacity. Demonstrated alliance reliability in a crisis is treated as a strategic deterrent in its own right: an alliance that performs under stress is more credible than one that performs only in rhetoric.

**Integration of the instruments of national power (DIME).**

- *Diplomatic.* The Department of State, through Embassy Tokyo, is the lead for the bilateral relationship. Any United States assistance flows from a Government of Japan request and is coordinated government-to-government. The United States affirms Japanese sovereignty over its own territory and its own response; the United States role is supporting.
- *Information.* The United States communicates that its assistance is requested, transparent, and in support of Japanese-led relief, and works to ensure that accurate information about relief reaches affected populations. The Strategy notes that adversaries and malign actors increasingly exploit the information environment during disasters to erode public trust.
- *Military.* The Department of Defense provides unique capabilities — heavy and medium airlift, sea-based logistics, engineering, and maritime-domain-awareness support — that complement, and do not substitute for, the affected nation’s own efforts and civilian relief mechanisms.
- *Economic.* Rapid restoration of essential services and supply chains in an allied economy serves United States and regional economic interests. The United States works through existing agreements so that assistance does not create new dependencies or liabilities.

## 3. National Defense Strategy — DoD-Specific Excerpts

**Campaigning and the value of allies.** The National Defense Strategy directs the Department to campaign daily to strengthen alliances and to be the partner of choice. Foreign Humanitarian Assistance and Disaster Relief, conducted at allied request, is identified as a high-return campaigning activity that reinforces interoperability and access while meeting a genuine humanitarian need.

**Force-allocation discipline.** The Strategy emphasizes that the joint force must meet competing global demands with finite forward forces. Commanders are directed to weigh the opportunity cost of committing forward-postured forces to any single contingency against standing deterrence and presence requirements, and to surface those trade-offs to higher headquarters rather than absorb them silently.

**Civil-military and interagency integration.** The Department supports, and does not lead, United States Government foreign disaster response. The United States Agency for International Development, Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance (USAID/BHA), is the lead federal agency for United States civilian assistance abroad. Military capabilities are committed to fill validated gaps that civilian and host-nation capacity cannot meet, and are transitioned back as soon as those gaps close.

## 4. National Military Strategy — Joint Force Direction

The National Military Strategy directs the joint force to be able to transition rapidly between competition activities and crisis response without ceding deterrence posture, and to integrate with allied command structures through established bilateral coordination mechanisms rather than improvising command relationships in a crisis. It directs commanders to protect the integrity of relief operations against criminal and malign exploitation while respecting that law enforcement in a sovereign ally’s territory is the ally’s responsibility.

## 5. Indo-Pacific Strategy — Regional Excerpts

The Indo-Pacific Strategy reaffirms the United States–Japan alliance as foundational and highlights the southern Ryukyu Islands as part of the strategically significant first-island-chain geography. It directs that United States activity in and around Japanese territory be conducted with full respect for Japanese sovereignty and host-nation lead, and that the United States deepen disaster-response interoperability with the Japan Self-Defense Forces, the Japan Coast Guard, and Japanese civil authorities. It explicitly cautions that United States activity in this geography be conducted so as to avoid generating unintended escalation signals, and confirms that humanitarian response is to be framed and conducted as humanitarian response.

## 6. Strategic Communication Themes

1.  United States assistance is provided at the request of the Government of Japan and in support of Japanese-led relief; Japan leads, the United States supports.
2.  The United States–Japan alliance delivers for the Japanese people in their hour of need; alliance reliability is real and demonstrated.
3.  Relief is transparent, accountable, and reaches affected populations regardless of island, district, or community; accurate information is itself a relief commodity.
4.  The United States respects Japanese sovereignty, including Japanese responsibility for law enforcement and public safety, and operates within it.

## 7. Cross-Reference to Planning Direction

This strategic context is implemented through the Secretary of Defense / Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff Execute Order (document 07), the USINDOPACOM Linked Execute Order (document 08), and the component Linked Execute Orders (documents 09a and 09b). ## 1. Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF)

Super Typhoon KAGURA (Japan Meteorological Agency designation T26-21; Joint Typhoon Warning Center 24W) struck the southern Ryukyu (Sakishima) Islands of Okinawa Prefecture, Japan, on 13–14 October 2026 at Category-5-equivalent intensity, then weakened northeastward along the Kuroshio Current after brushing eastern Taiwan on 14–15 October. Catastrophic wind, flood, and storm-surge damage to Miyakojima and Ishigaki has degraded power, water, communications, airfields, and ports; displaced thousands of residents into shelters; and overwhelmed the limited medical and logistics capacity of the outer islands. The Government of Japan (GoJ) is leading its national response and, on 15 October, requested United States Department of Defense unique capabilities — heavy and medium airlift, sea-based logistics, and maritime-domain-awareness support. USINDOPACOM has been directed to conduct mission analysis to form Joint Task Force ONYX RAIN (JTF-OR) in support of the GoJ.

J2 assesses with **high confidence** the physical facts of the disaster and the resulting humanitarian requirement. Concurrently, J2 has received a growing body of reporting indicating that **a transnational criminal smuggling and diversion network is beginning to exploit the relief environment**. The reporting is fragmentary and the network’s scope, organization, and intent are assessed with **low confidence** at this time. This paper presents the road to the crisis, the indicators received to date, and the intelligence gaps, so the staff can frame collection and analysis. It deliberately does not resolve those gaps; characterizing the criminal exploitation is analytic work for the staff and partners, not a conclusion handed down in this summary.

There is no state-conflict dimension to this crisis. United States forces do not operate in or around Taiwan. All law enforcement and maritime interdiction are Japanese responsibilities.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. The Road to the Crisis (genesis → landfall → collapse → criminal exploitation)

### 2.1 Long-term backdrop

The southern Ryukyu Islands are exposed, low-capacity island communities at the southwestern end of the first island chain. They depend almost entirely on sea and air resupply from Okinawa main island and the Japanese mainland for fuel, food, and medicine, and they sit astride a region of chronic, low-level transnational smuggling. The Bashi Channel and the Kuroshio corridor between Taiwan, the Yaeyamas, and the Philippines have for years carried a background flow of contraband — untaxed fuel and goods, fisheries violations, and occasional human smuggling — handled by loosely federated criminal brokers using fishing vessels and small coastal freighters under flags of convenience. Japanese authorities (the Japan Coast Guard, the National Police Agency and Okinawa Prefectural Police, Japan Customs, and the Japan Fisheries Agency) manage this background activity as a routine law-enforcement matter. It is this pre-existing criminal ecosystem that a disaster perturbs.

### 2.2 Storm genesis and intensification (08–12 October 2026)

A tropical disturbance formed east of the Philippine Sea on 08 October. Favorable sea-surface temperatures (about 29 degrees Celsius) and low vertical wind shear drove rapid intensification between 10 and 12 October, with the system reaching Category-5-equivalent strength (sustained winds about 140 knots) as it tracked west-northwest toward the Sakishima Islands. The Japan Meteorological Agency issued its highest-level warnings for the Miyako and Yaeyama regions on 12 October; Okinawa Prefecture and the municipalities ordered evacuations to designated shelters on Miyakojima, Ishigaki, and the smaller islands.

### 2.3 Landfall and physical effects (13–14 October 2026)

KAGURA passed directly over or immediately adjacent to Miyakojima and then Ishigaki on 13–14 October. Effects assessed with high confidence:

- **Wind and structural damage.** Widespread structural damage across Miyakojima and Ishigaki, with lesser damage to Yonaguni and the smaller Yaeyama islands. Power transmission and distribution networks were severely damaged on both principal islands.
- **Flood and storm surge.** Storm surge inundated low-lying districts of Ishigaki and the Hirara waterfront on Miyakojima; flash and riverine flooding compounded the surge.
- **Lifelines.** Widespread and sustained loss of commercial power; consequent loss of water treatment and pumping, wastewater handling, fuel dispensing, cold-chain medical storage, and most cellular and microwave communications.
- **Ports and airfields.** Hirara Port and Ishigaki Port were debris-fouled and surge-damaged; Miyako Airport and New Ishigaki Airport sustained terminal and navigation-aid damage; Shimojishima’s long runway was obstructed by debris. Initial airfield throughput is limited and weather-dependent.
- **Maritime-domain awareness.** Coastal surveillance radar and Automatic Identification System (AIS) shore receivers across the Miyako and Yaeyama clusters were knocked out or degraded by power loss and antenna damage, and several navigation aids and shore lights were extinguished or displaced.

### 2.4 Humanitarian collapse and displacement (14–16 October 2026)

By 16 October the GoJ and prefectural authorities report thousands of residents displaced into shelters on Miyakojima and Ishigaki, with significant unmet needs in potable water, power for medical facilities, fuel, and medical evacuation of acute cases from the outer islands to Okinawa main island and the mainland. Local medical and logistics capacity is overwhelmed. The GoJ has committed national means (the Japan Self-Defense Forces, the Japan Coast Guard, and civil agencies) and has requested United States support to fill validated gaps in airlift, sea-based logistics, and the degraded maritime picture.

### 2.5 Emergence of criminal exploitation (reported indicators, 14–16 October 2026)

In the same window, J2 — drawing on Japanese law-enforcement liaison, open-source reporting, and the maritime picture — has received indicators that the pre-existing criminal ecosystem is adapting to the disaster. These are presented as **indicators**, not as established findings:

- **Commodity-flow anomalies.** Japanese logistics and prefectural liaison have noted early, unquantified discrepancies between relief commodities dispatched to certain island distribution points and the quantities recorded as received, particularly for fuel, portable generators, and pharmaceuticals. Whether these reflect diversion, recording lag, storm chaos, or a combination is not yet established.
- **Anomalous small-craft activity.** With coastal radar and AIS degraded, the Japan Coast Guard and United States maritime patrol support have noted small-craft movements in the inter-island waters and toward the Hateruma and Yaeyama-bank approaches that do not correlate with known relief, fishing, or evacuation traffic, including vessels operating with AIS switched off or gapped.
- **Black-market reporting.** Open-source and liaison reporting references the early reappearance of black-market fuel and goods at elevated prices on the affected islands, consistent with the pre-existing smuggling ecosystem repurposing toward scarce relief commodities.
- **Shelter and displacement risk.** Protection actors (the International Organization for Migration and Japanese authorities) have flagged the generic, well-documented risk that displaced and sheltered populations — including vulnerable foreign nationals — are targets for exploitation and trafficking, and have noted unverified reports of unknown individuals approaching evacuees at reception points.
- **Information manipulation.** Japanese authorities and open-source monitors have observed locally circulating rumors about relief — that aid is contaminated, that distribution favors some districts or communities over others, and that foreign responders are taking fuel — of uncertain origin, some of which appears to be amplified rather than organic.

J2 does not at this time assess the degree to which these indicators are connected, directed by a single network, or merely the diffuse opportunism that follows any disaster. That characterization is a priority for collection and for staff analysis.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. Threat picture — what is known, suspected, and unknown

A fuller laydown is in document 03. In summary:

**Known (high confidence).** A pre-existing, loosely federated transnational smuggling ecosystem operates the Bashi–Sakishima–Kuroshio corridor using fishing vessels and small coastal freighters under flags of convenience, with shore-side fences and brokers on the affected islands and along the eastern Taiwan coast. Its peacetime capability is light and commercial in character: small craft, cash, encrypted messaging, knowledge of local waters, and corrupt or coerced facilitation. It has no military, cyber, or electronic-warfare capability and no demonstrated intent to threaten responders.

**Suspected (low-to-moderate confidence).** Elements of this ecosystem are repurposing toward diversion and resale of scarce relief commodities and are exploiting the degraded maritime picture to move goods inside or alongside relief traffic. One trans-shipment broker is the subject of thin, unconfirmed reporting of prior dealings with a sanctioned entity; **J2 treats this as an unresolved criminal-nexus reporting thread, not as evidence of state direction, and cautions against over-reading it.**

**Unknown (the gaps).** The network’s organization and leadership; the scale and routing of diversion; which nodes and vessels are involved; the degree of central coordination versus diffuse opportunism; and the network’s intent toward the relief effort and toward vulnerable populations.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4. Information environment snapshot

The information environment is contested in a manner typical of disasters, not by a peer adversary. Rumor and locally circulating disinformation about relief — contamination, favoritism, and foreigner-blaming narratives — are present and appear in part amplified. Their effect, if unchecked, is to suppress uptake of official aid (pushing residents toward black-market sources) and to complicate the credibility of Japanese and United States messaging. Cellular and social-media connectivity is intermittent in the disaster area but largely intact on Okinawa main island and nationally. Public information and counter-rumor activity in support of the GoJ’s messaging is an information task the staff should expect to address; the employment posture and authorities for United States information capabilities are in document 11, Annex C.

## 5. Intelligence gaps and confidence assessment

J2 distinguishes between confidence in the **physical/humanitarian picture** (high) and confidence in the **criminal picture** (low). The capabilities of the criminal ecosystem are reasonably well characterized at the peacetime baseline; its **intent and disaster-adapted behavior are the dominant unknowns** — the analytic weight sits on intent, not capability.

Priority intelligence gaps for the staff and partners to drive collection against:

1.  The relationship, if any, among the commodity-flow discrepancies, the anomalous small-craft activity, the black-market reporting, and the rumor activity — and whether they indicate one network or diffuse opportunism.
2.  The scale and routing of any relief-commodity diversion, and the specific nodes and vessels involved, fused from logistics throughput data and the maritime picture.
3.  The degree of restoration of the maritime-domain-awareness picture required to deconflict legitimate relief traffic from illicit movement.
4.  The nature and reach of trafficking risk to displaced and sheltered populations, including foreign nationals.
5.  The origin and amplification mechanism of the aid-related disinformation.

These gaps are framed for collection management and for the JIPOE (document 10). They are not resolved here.

## 6. Implications for mission analysis

The staff’s mission analysis should reason about — not be handed answers on — at least the following:

1.  How United States support best fills the validated airlift, sea-logistics, and maritime-awareness gaps while keeping the GoJ in the lead.
2.  How to protect the integrity of the relief commodity stream when distribution-versus-receipt discrepancies may indicate diversion — a logistics-and-intelligence fusion problem, not a single-staff problem.
3.  How United States forces deconflict their own movement and sensing with Japanese law enforcement, given that interdiction and arrest are Japanese functions and the United States does not conduct law enforcement in Japan.
4.  How the single forward amphibious force is balanced between this relief effort and standing regional presence requirements.
5.  How accurate information is delivered to affected populations in support of the GoJ to counter aid-suppressing rumor.
6.  How vulnerable populations in the evacuation and shelter system are protected, with Japanese and interagency actors in the lead.

## C10:D03 — Threat Network Laydown

# Threat Network Laydown — Operation ONYX RAIN

**Product Type:** USINDOPACOM J2 threat laydown (transnational criminal smuggling and diversion ecosystem) **Information cutoff:** 160400Z OCT 26 **Source basis:** Japan Coast Guard and National Police Agency law-enforcement liaison; Japan Customs and Japan Fisheries Agency reporting; United States maritime patrol observations; open-source maritime tracking and press **Companion graphic:** 02_Situational_Map.svg / 02_Situational_Map.png **Classification (synthetic):** UNCLASSIFIED // FOR EXERCISE / TRAINING USE ONLY (E)

## 1. Scope and a caution to the reader

This laydown characterizes the transnational criminal smuggling and diversion ecosystem in the Bashi Channel–Sakishima–Kuroshio corridor, both at its peacetime baseline (assessed with high confidence) and in terms of the disaster-adapted indicators received since landfall (assessed with low confidence). It is an intelligence input. It deliberately does not assert a finished, connected picture of who is diverting what, through which node, at what scale — that characterization is the fusion-and-analysis work of the staff and Japanese partners, supported by collection against the gaps listed in Section 7. Readers should resist the temptation to treat the reported indicators in Section 5 as an established network; they are reported, fragmentary, and unverified.

This is a **criminal** actor. There is no organized extremist actor, no military or paramilitary capability, and no state direction in this picture.

## 2. The corridor ecosystem — peacetime baseline (high confidence)

The waters linking eastern Taiwan, the Yaeyama and Miyako Islands, the Bashi Channel, and the northern Philippines have for years carried a persistent low-level flow of transnational smuggling, handled as a routine law-enforcement matter by Japanese authorities and their regional counterparts. The ecosystem is not a hierarchy; it is a loosely federated set of brokers, vessel operators, and shore-side fences who cooperate transaction-by-transaction. Japanese authorities track this background activity under the informal label of the “Kuroshio Line.”

Baseline commodities and activity: untaxed fuel and consumer goods, fisheries violations and trans-shipment of catch, occasional contraband, and intermittent human smuggling. Baseline volume is low and the actors are commercially, not ideologically, motivated.

## 3. Known geography of the corridor (baseline; anchored to real terrain)

The corridor’s known smuggling geography — the places where brokers, fences, and staging have historically operated — is anchored to real features. This is regional background, not an assertion about the current disaster:

## 4. Capabilities and tradecraft (baseline; high confidence on capability)

- **Platforms.** Coastal fishing vessels and small coastal freighters, frequently under flags of convenience; unregistered sampans and skiffs for inter-island and night movement. Speeds and ranges are commercial; no organic weapons of military significance.

- **Maritime concealment.** Routine use of Automatic Identification System (AIS) switching, gapping, and occasional spoofing; co-mingling with legitimate fishing and coastal traffic; exploitation of weather and night to avoid coastal radar.

- **Finance.** Cash and informal value-transfer (hawala-style) networks; minimal banking footprint.

- **Communications.** Commercial mobile and encrypted messaging applications.

- **Facilitation.** Reliance on local knowledge, opportunistic corruption or coercion of individual handlers, and front businesses (fuel brokers, fishing cooperatives, distributors).

The ecosystem has **no** cyber, electronic-warfare, space, or military capability, and no demonstrated intent or capability to threaten responder forces.

## 6. What the threat is — and is not

- It **is** an opportunistic, commercially motivated criminal ecosystem that a disaster incentivizes to scale up against scarce, high-value relief commodities and to exploit a degraded maritime picture and a vulnerable displaced population.

- It **is not** a military, paramilitary, extremist, or state-directed threat. It poses no assessed kinetic threat to responder forces. Countering it is a law-enforcement problem owned by Japanese authorities, not a targeting problem owned by the joint force.

## 7. Intelligence gaps and collection focus

These gaps are provided to inform collection management and the development of commander’s critical information requirements by the staff; the requirements themselves are not specified here.

1.  Connectivity among the commodity-flow anomalies, small-craft activity, black-market reporting, and rumor activity — one network or diffuse opportunism?

2.  Scale and routing of any relief-commodity diversion, and the specific nodes and vessels involved, fused from logistics throughput and the maritime picture.

3.  Restoration of maritime-domain awareness sufficient to separate legitimate relief traffic from illicit movement.

4.  Reach and nature of trafficking risk in the evacuation and shelter system.

5.  Origin and amplification of aid-related disinformation.

## 8. Pattern of life and indicators and warnings

Historic pattern: night and weather-masked small-craft movement; AIS manipulation timed to coastal-radar gaps; fences activated when commodity prices spike. Warning indicators the collection effort should watch: sustained AIS-dark small-craft tracks in relief sea lanes; widening commodity distribution-versus-receipt gaps; clustering of black-market activity near specific distribution nodes; organized approaches to shelters; coordinated rather than organic rumor surges. Cross-reference the JIPOE (document 10) for the integrated operational-environment picture.

## 3. United States standing presence requirements (the competing demand)

The forces apportioned above are the same forward-postured forces that hold standing United States Indo-Pacific presence and deterrence requirements. As of the apportionment date these standing requirements remain in effect and unfunded by any relief of mission:

- Routine forward presence and deterrence patrols across the theater, including the single forward amphibious ready group and Marine expeditionary unit as the theater’s sole afloat crisis-response force.

- Standing maritime-patrol coverage commitments that the Task Force 72 P-8A force already carries.

- Intratheater airlift demands that predate the disaster.

The relief mission and these standing requirements draw on one finite forward force pool. Reconciling the two is a higher-headquarters apportionment-and-allocation matter that the staff is expected to surface and quantify; it is not resolved in this order of battle.

## 1. Japan — national level

**Government and political dynamics.** Japan is a parliamentary constitutional democracy. National disaster response is led by the Cabinet, supported by the Cabinet Office Director-General for Disaster Management. For a catastrophe of this scale, the Government of Japan (GoJ) stands up a national Major Disaster Management Headquarters chaired at the Prime-Minister level, which coordinates national ministries, the affected prefecture, and the Japan Self-Defense Forces. Japanese disaster law gives prefectural governors and municipal mayors substantial authority over local response, sheltering, and evacuation, so Okinawa Prefecture and the municipalities of Miyakojima and Ishigaki are central civil actors, not merely recipients of national direction.

**Decision to request United States assistance.** Japan first commits its own national means and requests foreign assistance only when a disaster exceeds national capacity. The GoJ request for United States Department of Defense unique capabilities, transmitted government-to-government through diplomatic channels on 15 October, is the legal and political trigger for United States military involvement. The request is specific and bounded — heavy and medium airlift, sea-based logistics, and maritime-domain-awareness support — and it preserves Japanese lead throughout.

**Security forces (top level).** - *Japan Self-Defense Forces (JSDF).* For this crisis the JSDF stands up a Joint Task Force-Southwest (JTF-SW) under a Japan Ground Self-Defense Force lieutenant general, integrating ground, maritime, and air elements. The Japan Ground Self-Defense Force 15th Brigade (Naha) and Western Army provide ground relief forces; the Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force provides transport and escort vessels and Fleet Air Wing 5 (Naha); the Japan Air Self-Defense Force Southwestern Air Defense Force (Naha) provides airlift and air coordination. - *Japan Coast Guard (JCG).* The 11th Regional Coast Guard Headquarters (Naha) owns maritime search and rescue coordination, port and waterway safety, and all maritime law enforcement and interdiction in the operating area. - *National Police Agency (NPA) and Okinawa Prefectural Police.* These own land law enforcement, public order at shelters and distribution sites, and the criminal investigation of black-market diversion and trafficking, supported by Japan Customs (Okinawa Regional Customs) and the Japan Fisheries Agency on smuggling matters.

**Alignment posture.** Japan is a United States treaty ally under the 1960 Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security. United States–Japan disaster cooperation is well-precedented (Operation TOMODACHI, 2011). Japan’s posture in this crisis is fully aligned with the United States; the only friction in the relationship is the routine and proper one of a sovereign host nation insisting on leading its own response and retaining its own law-enforcement authority.

## 2. Okinawa Prefecture — the operating area

**Population and settlement.** Okinawa Prefecture comprises Okinawa main island (the population and logistics center, around 1.4 million people) and the outer Miyako and Yaeyama island groups (together on the order of a hundred thousand residents, concentrated on Miyakojima and Ishigaki). The outer islands depend almost entirely on sea and air links to Okinawa main island and the mainland for fuel, food, medicine, and goods; they have limited organic medical and logistics capacity, which a major storm rapidly overwhelms.

**Critical infrastructure and storm impact.** - *Power.* Island grids on Miyako and Ishigaki are largely self-contained, served by local thermal generation and increasingly by renewables; both took heavy transmission and distribution damage, leaving widespread outages and forcing reliance on portable generators — making generators and fuel high-demand, divertible commodities. - *Water and wastewater.* Desalination and pumping depend on grid and generator power; outages cascade into water and sanitation shortfalls. - *Fuel.* The outer islands hold limited fuel stocks and depend on resupply by coastal tanker into Hirara and Ishigaki; storm damage to port fuel-handling and to the grid makes marine diesel and aviation fuel both scarce and valuable. - *Communications.* Cellular, microwave backhaul, and inter-island fiber sustained damage; coverage is intermittent. Inter-island and island-to-mainland submarine cables that carry telecommunications are a dependency, with restoration governed by Japanese carriers. - *Air and sea ports.* Detailed in document 06; in summary, Shimojishima offers the most capable runway in the disaster area when reopened, New Ishigaki is the Yaeyama hub, and the principal ports (Hirara, Ishigaki) are debris- and surge-damaged.

**Critical-infrastructure interdependency.** The dependencies chain tightly: power loss disables water, fuel, cold-chain medical storage, and communications; port and airfield damage throttles the resupply that would relieve all of the above; and the scarcity of fuel, generators, and medicines creates exactly the high-value, divertible commodity stream that a criminal network can exploit. The planner should treat the commodity distribution system itself — what arrives, where it goes, and whether receipts match — as critical infrastructure to be protected, not merely as logistics.

## 3. United States presence and persons

**Basing and agreements.** United States Forces Japan is headquartered at Yokota Air Base; III Marine Expeditionary Force is headquartered at Camp Courtney on Okinawa main island, with Marine aviation at Marine Corps Air Station Futenma and Air Force forces at Kadena Air Base. United States–Japan cooperation operates under the Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security, the Status of Forces Agreement, and the Acquisition and Cross-Servicing Agreement (ACSA), which provides the standing mechanism for logistics cross-servicing between the two forces. These agreements mean the United States can support a relief effort from inside Japan quickly and lawfully, without negotiating access from scratch.

**United States and other foreign persons in the disaster area.** Beyond the concentrated United States military community on Okinawa main island (not in the disaster area), the outer Sakishima islands host a modest, dispersed foreign-national population: tourists (the Yaeyamas and Miyako are resort destinations), foreign English-language assistant teachers and other long-term residents, and a small number of United States and other foreign citizens. Numbers fluctuate seasonally and are not precisely known at the outset. Their presence is a civil-considerations factor the staff should account for; the United States Embassy and the Department of State own the welfare and any movement of United States citizens, in coordination with Japanese authorities.

## 4. Eastern Taiwan — regional context only

Eastern Taiwan (Hualien, Taitung, the Su’ao and Yilan area, and Lanyu/Orchid Island) lies within KAGURA’s regional impact band and along the smuggling corridor. It appears in this package solely as context: as a storm-affected coastline and as the southern shore where the criminal network is assessed to maintain a fence and trans-shipment connection. United States forces do not operate in or around Taiwan in this operation, and nothing in this package treats the criminal corridor as state-directed. Cross-strait criminal activity is treated as criminal activity.

## 3. Sea lines of communication and chokepoints

**Miyako Strait (Miyako Channel).** The wide passage between Okinawa main island and the Miyako Islands is the principal maritime line of communication from the Okinawa staging base to the disaster area. It is open water, not a constrained chokepoint, but the post-storm sea state and floating debris fields driven by the Kuroshio Current complicate small-craft and connector movement.

**Sakishima inter-island waters.** The waters between Miyako and the Yaeyamas, and among the Yaeyama islands themselves, are dotted with reef and shoal. Local pilotage knowledge matters; charted hazards are compounded by storm-displaced navigation buoys and damaged shore lights.

**Bashi Channel.** The channel between Lanyu (Orchid Island, Taiwan) and the Batanes (Y’ami Island, Philippines) links the South China Sea and the Philippine Sea and carries heavy commercial traffic. It is the southern leg of the regional smuggling corridor and a route for flag-of-convenience small freighters. United States forces do not operate in the Bashi Channel for this operation; it is relevant as background to the maritime-domain-awareness picture.

**Kuroshio Current.** The warm, northeast-setting Kuroshio runs past the Ryukyus at one to two meters per second. It shapes drift, the dispersal of storm debris, and the routing small craft can exploit, and it lends its name to the regional smuggling corridor.

## 4. Key terrain — ports and airfields

- **Naha (Okinawa main island).** Naha Airport (OKA/ROAH) is a joint civil and Japan Air Self-Defense Force field with long runways and full handling capacity; it and Kadena Air Base are the relief throughput and reception nodes. Naha port has commercial and ferry capacity intact.

- **Shimojishima Airport (SHI/ROMD).** Its 3,000-meter runway, adjacent to Miyakojima across the Irabu bridge, is the most capable airhead in the disaster area when reopened, and is the intended intermediate throughput node for fixed-wing relief flow into the Miyako cluster.

- **Miyako Airport (MMY/ROMY) and Hirara Port.** Both sustained storm damage — terminal and navigation-aid damage at the airport, debris and quay damage at the port. Initial throughput is limited and weather-dependent.

- **New Ishigaki Airport (ISG/ROIG) and Ishigaki Port.** The Yaeyama hub; the airport’s 2,000-meter runway supports medium airlift once cleared; the port’s quays are surge-damaged.

- **Yonaguni Airport (OGN/ROYN), Sonai and Kubura ports (Yonaguni).** Smaller facilities serving the westernmost island; lesser storm damage than Miyako and Ishigaki.

Japanese garrisons in the area — Japan Ground Self-Defense Force Camp Ishigaki and Camp Yonaguni — provide initial organic response capacity on those islands and are described in the friendly order of battle (document 04).

## 5. Climatology and the physical environment

The operation falls in the late Western Pacific typhoon season (mid-October). Super Typhoon KAGURA has passed northeast along the Kuroshio, but the environment remains degraded: residual heavy seas subsiding from sea state 4–5, saturated ground and continued localized flooding, episodic trailing rain bands reducing ceilings and visibility, and the possibility of a follow-on disturbance later in the season. Daylight is about eleven hours. Sea-surface temperatures remain warm (about 27 degrees Celsius), sustaining convective showers. Detailed sea-state, tide, and illumination data are in document 11, Annex D (Climatology and Astronomical Data).

## 6. Electromagnetic spectrum and the maritime-domain-awareness picture

The storm degraded the electromagnetic and sensing environment across the disaster area. Commercial power loss and physical antenna damage have reduced or knocked out coastal surveillance radar coverage and Automatic Identification System (AIS) shore-receiver coverage across the Miyako and Yaeyama clusters; several navigation aids and shore lights are off or displaced. Cellular and microwave backhaul are intermittent where towers and fiber are damaged. The practical effect is a degraded maritime-domain-awareness picture in exactly the waters where relief sea and air traffic is densest and where small craft operate. Restoring sensing coverage, and deconflicting the surge of legitimate relief traffic, are environmental facts the planner must account for; the maritime-domain-awareness support that United States maritime patrol aircraft can provide to the Japan Coast Guard is addressed in documents 04, 09b, and 11.

### 1. SITUATION

#### (a) General

On 13–14 October 2026, Super Typhoon KAGURA struck the southern Ryukyu (Sakishima) Islands of Okinawa Prefecture, Japan, at Category-5-equivalent intensity, causing catastrophic damage to Miyakojima and Ishigaki. Power, water, communications, airfields, and ports are severely degraded; thousands of residents are displaced; and the medical and logistics capacity of the outer islands is overwhelmed. The Government of Japan (GoJ) is leading its national response and, per reference (c), has requested United States Department of Defense unique capabilities — heavy and medium airlift, sea-based logistics, and maritime-domain-awareness (MDA) support — to fill validated gaps. The President has authorized, and the Secretary of Defense has directed, Department of Defense support to the GoJ-led relief effort under references (a), (b), (d), and (e).

#### (b) Threat / criminal activity

There is no state adversary and no armed-conflict dimension to this crisis. A transnational criminal smuggling and diversion ecosystem operates the regional maritime corridor and is assessed to be adapting to exploit the relief effort through commodity diversion, contraband movement under cover of degraded MDA, exploitation of displaced populations, and aid-related disinformation. **All law enforcement and maritime interdiction against this activity are the responsibility of the Japan Coast Guard (JCG) and the National Police Agency (NPA). United States forces do not conduct law enforcement in Japan.** The threat to United States forces is assessed as negligible; the principal risk the threat poses is to the integrity and reach of the relief effort.

#### (c) Friendly

CDR USINDOPACOM is the supported combatant commander for the Department of Defense effort, in support of the GoJ-led response. Commander, United States Forces Japan (COMUSFJ) is the senior United States operational coordinator with the Japanese Joint Staff through the United States–Japan Bilateral Coordination Mechanism. USAID/BHA, per reference (e), is the lead federal agency for the United States Government civilian response and will deploy a Disaster Assistance Response Team (DART). The Department of State, through Embassy Tokyo, leads government-to-government coordination and the welfare of United States citizens.

#### (d) Civil considerations

Thousands of Japanese residents are displaced into shelters on Miyakojima, Ishigaki, and the smaller islands, with acute needs in potable water, medical care and evacuation, power, and fuel. A dispersed and seasonally variable foreign-national population, including a small number of United States citizens (tourists and long-term residents), is present on the outer islands; their welfare and any movement are owned by the Department of State in coordination with Japanese authorities. Displaced and sheltered populations carry the well-documented disaster risk of exploitation and trafficking.

## Step 1 — Define the operational environment

**Joint operational area.** The Miyako and Yaeyama Island clusters and the surrounding waters, with the Okinawa main island intermediate staging base to the northeast and the Miyako Strait as the principal sea line of communication. **Area of interest.** The wider Bashi Channel–Kuroshio corridor (south to the Bashi Channel, west to eastern Taiwan as context only), through which the regional smuggling ecosystem operates and from which weather and maritime traffic flow.

**Defining characteristics.** A catastrophic-disaster environment: degraded power, water, communications, ports, and airfields; thousands displaced; a degraded maritime-domain-awareness (MDA) picture from storm damage to coastal radar and the Automatic Identification System (AIS); a permissive security environment with no state or armed adversary; and an opportunistic transnational criminal ecosystem adapting to the relief environment. The Government of Japan leads; the United States supports; Japanese authorities own law enforcement.

## Step 2 — Describe the impact of the operational environment (PMESII-PT)

- **Political (P).** Government of Japan in the lead through a national Major Disaster Management Headquarters; Okinawa Prefecture and the Miyakojima and Ishigaki municipalities as local civil authorities; the United States supporting under treaty. Politically straightforward and aligned; the standing sensitivity is host-nation lead and sovereignty.

- **Military (M).** Japan Self-Defense Forces Joint Task Force-Southwest, Japan Coast Guard (JCG) 11th Region, and Japanese police are the lead Japanese actors; United States JTF ONYX RAIN supports. No military threat.

- **Economic (E).** Outer-island economies depend on sea/air resupply; storm damage has created acute scarcity of fuel, generators, and medicines — the high-value, divertible commodity stream.

- **Social (S).** Thousands displaced into shelters; vulnerable populations (elderly, medically fragile, foreign nationals) concentrated; community trust susceptible to rumor.

- **Information (I).** Intermittent connectivity in the disaster area; locally circulating aid-related rumor (contamination, favoritism, foreigner-blaming), some apparently amplified; national and Okinawa-main-island connectivity intact.

- **Infrastructure (I).** Power, water, communications, ports, and airfields degraded; the relief distribution system itself is a critical infrastructure to be protected.

- **Physical environment (PE).** Late typhoon season; residual heavy seas subsiding; trailing rain bands; warm sea-surface temperatures; about eleven hours of daylight; debris fields set by the Kuroshio.

- **Time (T).** Life-saving needs (medical, water) are most acute in the first operational periods; airfield and port reopening is the pacing factor for relief throughput.

## Step 3 — Evaluate the threat

The threat is a transnational criminal smuggling and diversion ecosystem (see document 03), not a military adversary. Applying the capabilities-versus-intent distinction:

- **Capabilities (assessed with moderate-to-high confidence).** Small craft and small coastal freighters under flags of convenience; AIS manipulation; cash and informal value transfer; encrypted messaging; local knowledge; opportunistic facilitation and front businesses. No military, cyber, electronic-warfare, or space capability. No capability to threaten responder forces.

- **Intent and disaster-adapted behavior (assessed with low confidence — the dominant unknown).** Reporting indicates the ecosystem is repurposing toward scarce relief commodities and exploiting the degraded maritime picture, but the scale, routing, organization, and degree of central coordination are unknown. The analytic weight is on intent and behavior, not capability.

- **Center-of-gravity framing (intelligence view, offered for staff use).** If the activity is materially organized, its sources of strength are likely the broker/fence relationships and the concealment afforded by the degraded MDA picture and co-mingled relief traffic; its vulnerabilities are the same accountability and sensing the relief effort can restore. The staff will develop the operational implications.

## Step 4 — Threat courses of action (competing hypotheses; not yet confirmed)

J2 carries three plausible, non-exclusive hypotheses for how the criminal ecosystem is adapting. These are **hypotheses to drive collection**, not findings; the degree to which any, all, or none are occurring — and whether they reflect one network or diffuse opportunism — is unresolved.

**Hypothesis A — Commodity diversion at scale.** Brokers divert relief fuel, generators, and medicines from island distribution points for black-market resale. *Indicators:* widening distribution-versus-receipt discrepancies; clustering of black-market activity near specific distribution nodes; commodity prices spiking then easing as illicit supply arrives.

**Hypothesis B — Corridor exploitation under the MDA gap.** Operators move contraband (and diverted goods) inside or alongside relief sea and air traffic, exploiting degraded radar/AIS. *Indicators:* sustained AIS-dark small-craft tracks in relief sea lanes; small-craft movements toward the Hateruma/Yaeyama-bank approaches uncorrelated with relief, fishing, or evacuation; trans-shipment activity along the Bashi leg.

**Hypothesis C — Exploitation of displaced populations.** Facilitators target shelters and reception points for trafficking of vulnerable evacuees. *Indicators:* organized approaches to evacuees; unaccounted movement of vulnerable persons out of the shelter system; recruiter activity reported by protection actors.

J2 does not at this time rank these as most-likely / most-dangerous, because the reporting is insufficient to do so responsibly. The most-dangerous outcome for the **mission** is that any of the above materially suppresses aid uptake or undermines confidence in the relief; the most-dangerous for **vulnerable persons** is Hypothesis C.

## Confidence assessment

High confidence in the physical and humanitarian picture and in the threat’s peacetime capabilities. Low confidence in the threat’s current scale, organization, intent, and the connectivity of the indicators. The single-source criminal-nexus thread (possible prior dealing with a sanctioned entity) is very-low-confidence and is treated as a criminal matter, not state direction.

## Implications for JIPOE consumers (the mission-analysis staff)

The staff should: drive collection against the gaps above; design logistics-and-intelligence fusion to characterize possible diversion; plan MDA support to the JCG that recovers the maritime picture and feeds Japanese law enforcement without the United States conducting law enforcement; account for the apportionment tension on the single forward amphibious force; plan accurate-information delivery in support of the Government of Japan; and surface protection of vulnerable populations with Japanese and interagency actors in the lead. The operational answers are the staff’s to develop.

## Annex A — Operational Law and Authorities

**Legal basis for United States involvement.** United States support is provided at the request of the Government of Japan (diplomatic note, 15 October 2026) under the Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security between the United States and Japan (1960). Department of Defense foreign-disaster assistance is provided under Title 10, United States Code, Section 404, following the Secretary of State’s foreign-disaster determination and designation of the United States Agency for International Development, Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance (USAID/BHA), as lead federal agency. Logistics cross-servicing with the Japan Self-Defense Forces is conducted under the United States–Japan Acquisition and Cross-Servicing Agreement (ACSA). The Status of Forces Agreement governs the status of United States forces in Japan.

**Law-enforcement boundary (authority and condition — not a prohibition of capability).** Law enforcement, criminal investigation, search, seizure, arrest, and maritime interdiction within Japanese territory and waters are exercised by the Japan Coast Guard (JCG) and the National Police Agency (NPA) / Okinawa Prefectural Police, supported by Japan Customs and the Japan Fisheries Agency. United States forces do not exercise these functions. This is a matter of authority and host-nation sovereignty, not of capability: where United States sensing or information is relevant to a Japanese law-enforcement matter, it is provided to Japanese authorities (maritime-domain-awareness support to the JCG, conditioned on a JCG request and Department of State concurrence; observations referred through the Joint Task Force). United States forces retain the inherent right of self-defense at all times.

**Rules for the use of force.** The Standing Rules for the Use of Force (CJCS Instruction 3121.01-series) apply for force protection and individual self-defense. The environment is permissive and humanitarian; no mission-specific escalation of force is directed. Use-of-force questions arising from contact with criminal activity are resolved by referral to Japanese authorities, not by United States enforcement action.

**Protection-of-civilians and trafficking-in-persons obligations.** United States forces comply with United States anti-trafficking policy and support, in a supporting role, Japanese and interagency efforts to protect vulnerable displaced populations from exploitation.

## Annex B — Cyber

United States cyber activity in this operation is **defensive support to mission systems only**: protection of the relief command-and-control, common-operating-picture, and logistics networks, and of the maritime-domain-awareness data exchange with the JCG, against criminal or opportunistic interference. United States Cyber Command provides cybersecurity support in a supporting role. No offensive cyber operations are authorized or required. Coordination of any cyber matter affecting Japanese networks runs through the Bilateral Coordination Mechanism and Japanese authorities.

## Annex C — Information, Public Affairs, and Military Information Support (employment posture)

Information capabilities integral to a disaster response are **employed**, with stated authorities and conditions — not stripped out.

- **Public Affairs.** The Department of State / Embassy Tokyo leads United States public communication; Department of Defense public affairs supports. Themes per document 01, Section 6: Japan leads and the United States supports; the alliance delivers; relief is transparent and reaches all communities; accurate information is itself relief.

- **Military Information Support Operations (MISO).** MISO capabilities are employed in support of the Government of Japan’s public-information effort and Embassy Tokyo messaging, with Chief-of-Mission approval, to deliver accurate, timely relief information to affected populations and to counter aid-related rumor and disinformation that suppresses aid uptake. MISO is conducted in support of, and consistent with, Japanese government messaging; it is not employed against Japanese audiences independently of the Government of Japan and Chief of Mission.

- **Electromagnetic-spectrum operations.** Spectrum operations support maritime-domain awareness, navigation in the degraded environment, and direction-finding support to the JCG (for example, against vessels manipulating the Automatic Identification System), conducted within standing authorities and host-nation coordination. There is no blanket restriction on these capabilities; where a constraint applies, it is stated as an authority and a condition.

- **Counter-disinformation.** The information staff, with Embassy Tokyo and Japanese authorities, monitors and counters aid-related rumor through accurate public information; attribution of disinformation is an intelligence and law-enforcement matter handled with Japanese partners.
