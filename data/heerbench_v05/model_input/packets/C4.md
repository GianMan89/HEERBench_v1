# **OPERATION SEA TIGER — STRATEGIC CONTEXT**

**HEADQUARTERS, UNITED STATES PACIFIC COMMAND (USPACOM), J5**

**DTG: 260600Z JUN 2026**

**1. PURPOSE.** This paper orients the Joint Planning Group to the strategic and operational setting for Operation SEA TIGER prior to mission analysis. It summarizes the situation; it does not substitute for the Regional Intelligence Summary, the Joint Intelligence Preparation of the Operational Environment (JIPOE), or the Commander's Initial Planning Guidance (CIPG).

**2. STRATEGIC SETTING.** The People's Republic of China (PRC) has moved from coercion to force in a bid to compel the unification of Taiwan (the Republic of China) with the mainland. Following a sustained gray-zone campaign — an expanded Air Defense Identification Zone over the South and East China Seas, a distributed denial-of-service attack against the Taipei exchange, a naval clash east of the Matsu Islands, and a fabricated terrorist-incident pretext — PRC forces seized the Quemoy (Kinmen) island group on or about 10 June 2026 and, on 18 June 2026, secured the entire Matsu island group by multi-brigade amphibious assault within twenty-four hours. On 13 June 2026 the PRC announced it would reunite Taiwan with the mainland by force if necessary. Taiwan has formally requested United States military assistance under the Taiwan Relations Act (Public Law 96-8). The President has directed Commander, USPACOM (CDR USPACOM) to develop a range of options to de-escalate the crisis and restore the status quo.

**3. THE ADVERSARY IN BRIEF.** The PRC is a peer competitor with interior lines, a higher speed of concentration, land-based short- and medium-range missiles able to range Okinawa and Guam, a robust cyberspace and electronic-warfare capability, and the ability to open new theaters in space and cyberspace. The People's Liberation Army Navy (PLAN) Eastern Sea Fleet, based at Ningbo, has begun preparing an amphibious force for a division-sized assault. PRC weaknesses are equally consequential: no experience in joint operations below the operational level, weakness in anti-submarine warfare and underway replenishment, scarce amphibious lift that requires an undamaged port, a centralized command vulnerable to decapitation, and no international support.

**4. THE OPERATIONAL PROBLEM.** The operational problem is two-fold: first, how to quickly deny the Taiwan Strait and its approaches to the PLAN while United States forces continue to build up in the region; and second, how to obtain and maintain control of the East and South China Seas without provoking the PRC toward escalation and general war. The defining tension is time and distance: the bulk of United States forces require seven to ten days to arrive in theater, while Taiwan can repel a large-scale invasion unaided for no more than approximately fifteen days. The speed of the United States build-up is assessed to be the decisive factor in PRC decision-making.

**5. NATIONAL AIMS.** The national strategic objectives are the diplomatic resolution of cross-strait issues while protecting the security, social, and economic systems of Taiwan; the prevention of escalation with the PRC through a flexible-response option; and the restoration of long-range regional stability in the Western Pacific. The desired endstate is the political stability and physical security of Taiwan, with conditions established for an amicable and freely-chosen resolution of cross-strait issues, the Quemoy and Matsu islands returned to Taiwan control, and freedom of navigation restored in the Strait.

**6. WHY IT MATTERS.** Taiwan is the world's leading producer of advanced semiconductors and a central node in the global technology economy. A protracted or stalemated conflict over Taiwan threatens a catastrophic disruption of the global economy — a consequence the PRC also fears, and which bears directly on the assessed PRC strategic center of gravity, its economic interdependence with Taiwan and the wider world. The friendly strategic center of gravity is, in turn, the cohesion of United States partners across the Pacific region.

**7. CONSTRAINTS ON ACTION.** Action must remain tied to the single issue of Taiwan's defense and must avoid provoking general war with a nuclear-armed peer. The President retains the authority to deploy United States forces into Taiwan or to conduct operations from Taiwan. No combined Taiwan–United States rules of engagement exist at this time. Coalition contributions carry national caveats that bound the range of feasible options. The safe evacuation of American citizens from Taiwan is a precondition to any military option**.**

**8. THE ASK.** CDR USPACOM will submit an initial requirements and options proposal and an OPREP-1 report to the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff not later than 260400Z June 2026, and will lead the development of a Commander's Estimate recommending a course of action, termination criteria, and post-conflict considerations. There is no single right answer in mission analysis; the commander decides, grounded in doctrine and the facts of the situation**.**

> This summary provides the Strategic intelligence background and escalation history for the mission-analysis staff. Assessments are stated at confidence; attribution of the PRC intentions is a cross-source assessment, not an open-source finding.

**1. (E) Political-Strategic Activities.**

1.  \(E\) Concerned with the U.S. publicly announced “Pivot to Asia” strategy, the People’s Republic of China (PRC) has increased its actions in the South China Sea, a self-proclaimed “Core National Interest.” Since early 2025, these actions have included a more aggressive strategy towards re-claiming Taiwan (ROC) as a province of the PRC. This more forceful approach is partially in response to the Taiwanese political rhetoric of certain political parties seeking Taiwanese independence and admission to the United Nations.

2.  \(E\) The PRC has declared that 2027 would be a year of decision regarding Taiwan unification and has since stepped up missile tests, ordered multiple amphibious landing exercises, and increased its naval presence - to include new carrier strike groups - in the Taiwan Straits and eastern approaches. It has deployed multiple fast-attack diesel submarines on so-called routine patrols far beyond the second island chain. In addition, the PRC Government approached the Governments of the Republic of the Philippines and of Japan regarding a long sought after diplomatic solution to the claims over disputed islands (Spratleys and Senkakus). The PRC has also offered to assist in curbing North Korean nuclear ambitions as well as assist Vietnam in their decades-long disputes over Mekong water rights in Indochina. American and Taiwanese leadership assessed that these PRC initiatives were part of an effort to categorize Taiwan’s ambition for formal admission to the UN as deliberately provocative and regionally destabilizing while the PRC’s diplomatic efforts garner international plaudits. By offering favorable settlements to Japan, the Philippines, and Vietnam, the PRC can work directly towards assimilation of Taiwan without interference from other regional actors.

3.  \(E\) The continuing war between Russia and Ukraine and the fractious response from the international community, including the U.S./NATO, has also entered into PRC strategic calculations: without military support from Japan, Korea, or the Philippines, the U.S. would likely not risk armed conflict on China’s doorstep over Taiwan, a historic province of mainland China. However, the current U.S. administration has shown a willingness towards overt unilateral military interventions, most recently in Venezuela, Iran and (potentially) in Cuba.

**2. (E) Strategic Setting (East China Sea Fleet Activities).**

1.  \(E\) The East China Sea Fleet of the People's Liberation Army Navy (PLAN) maintains routine patrols across the East China Sea, encompassing the Taiwan Strait and adjacent waters, to assert maritime sovereignty, safeguard fishing interests, and monitor foreign naval movements. These operations typically involve formations of surface combatants, submarines, and support vessels conducting high-seas transits, anti-submarine warfare drills, and escort duties for civilian maritime traffic. In December 2025, assessments indicated roughly 100 PLAN vessels operating across the East China Sea and adjacent regions amid broader winter training activities, underscoring the fleet's persistent regional presence.

2.  \(E\) Presence missions emphasize normalizing Chinese naval activity beyond coastal limits, including transits near Japan's southwest islands and the Philippine Sea. For instance, the *Liaoning* carrier strike group completed a six-day patrol near Japan's southwestern region in December 2025 before returning to East China Sea bases, demonstrating operational reach with escort destroyers and frigates. Such patrols, conducted year-round, integrate with air patrols and often coincide with responses to perceived foreign incursions, though they are framed by Chinese authorities as defensive maritime security measures.

3.  \(E\) In the vicinity of the disputed Diaoyu/Senkaku Islands, routine PLAN patrols complement China Coast Guard activities, focusing on territorial sea enforcement and deterrence against Japanese patrols. Overall, the fleet's patrols average multiple sorties weekly in key areas like the Taiwan Strait, enhancing combat readiness and signaling resolve amid regional tensions.

4.  \(E\) In December 2025, the fleet's aircraft carrier *Fujian* conducted its first Taiwan Strait transit post-commissioning, accompanied by escorts, heightening concerns over potential invasion rehearsals amid U.S. and allied freedom-of-navigation operations. These operations, while not resulting in direct clashes, have normalized large-scale naval encirclements, with Taiwanese tracking data indicating over 1,000 air incursions into its ADIZ in 2024.

5.  \(E\) In Taiwan-related scenarios, the East Sea Fleet, operating under the Eastern Theater Command (ETC), is positioned as the lead naval element for enforcing maritime quarantines or blockades across the Taiwan Strait, leveraging its proximity—approximately 130-180 kilometers from Fujian Province bases—to rapidly deploy surface action groups comprising destroyers, frigates, and submarines. For instance, in modeled limited quarantine operations, up to five such groups, totaling nearly 30 warships, would encircle Taiwan from standoff distances to monitor traffic, deter foreign intervention, and support intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) missions, while aircraft carrier groups like those with the *Shandong* conduct sorties east of the island. These tactics align with gray-zone strategies to coerce compliance without full escalation, drawing on the fleet's integration with coast guard and maritime militia assets for enforcement.

6.  \(E\) For higher-intensity invasion or amphibious assault scenarios, the fleet's capabilities center on achieving sea and air superiority in the strait, with destroyers and submarines tasked to neutralize Taiwanese naval forces and counter U.S. or allied responses, potentially involving cruise missile strikes from Kilo-class submarines and Sovremenny-class destroyers observed in regional drills. Recent transits demonstrate operational readiness for projecting power into contested areas east of Taiwan, including threats to ports like Hualien via long-range bombers and submarine-launched missiles. However, empirical assessments highlight persistent challenges, including the strait's turbulent currents, limited landing sites on Taiwan's east coast, and the need for massive amphibious lift—potentially augmented by civilian ferries—which could expose the fleet to attrition from anti-ship missiles and submarines. War games indicate the fleet's expanding reach, such as strikes on Taiwan's eastern defenses, but underscore vulnerabilities to interdiction beyond initial A2/AD zones.

**3. (E) Escalation Arc (preceding 90 days).**

1.  \(E\) Since May, 2020 the PRC has maintained a compelling narrative to counter the “Pivot to Asia” strategy of the United States. The PRC posits that its “One China” policy is in the interests of the international community in order to maintain regional stability and ensure freedom of navigation and maritime trade. According to the PRC narrative, its “One China” policy and maintenance of regional order in the South China Sea is akin to India providing the same maritime service to the international community in the Indian Ocean or NATO in the North Atlantic. This narrative has caused more than a few members of the international community to look towards China as the inevitable power that ultimately matters in East Asia rather than the U.S.

2.  \(E\) Recently, “incidents” vis-à-vis China and Taiwan have increased. On November 1, 2025 the Taiwanese Stock Markets International Exchange was the subject of an unexpected Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack and the RPI satellite communications systems suffered sporadic and unexplained outages. Although no one claimed responsibility for this DDoS attack some cyber security industry experts strongly suspected the Chinese PLA as the originators. Members of the Taiwanese Government privately viewed it as a coercive attempt by the PRC to force them back into the “One China” discussions. Adding to the economic uncertainty was a news story originating from an Indian investigative news network that reported a future PRC policy requiring all goods imported and exported by Taiwan to pass PRC customs. This was seen by many U.S. politicians, led by Senator Lindsey Graham (R-South Carolina), as a subtle warning by the world’s second largest economy. This would significantly increase political pressure on the Trump administration and possibly force the U.S. into a decision on the depth of future support for Taiwan because of the potential risk of disrupting the U.S. economy.

3.  \(E\) Also in November, the PRC announced a greatly expanded Air Defense Identification Zone (ADIZ) in the South and East China Seas. While regional opposition was vociferous and very public, the United States avoided provocative language and instead simply ignored the ADIZ with overflights of unarmed military aircraft. American commercial carriers, however, voluntarily complied with the ADIZ rather than risk disruption of passenger and carrier service. In early December, the PRC expanded this ADIZ into the western Pacific Ocean, again with little public comment from the United States Government. Almost immediately the Republic of Korea expanded their KADIZ and Japan announced increased defense expenditures, including the unveiling of the JS *Izumo*, as new light carrier, capable of launching/recovering F-35B (STVOL) 5th generation aircraft. The JS *Izumo* will be joined by another light carrier, JS *Kaga,* in 2027. Both ships displace roughly 25,000 tons and look remarkably similar to a USN LHD. The U.S. has also quietly deployed several P8 Poseidon Maritime Patrol and Reconnaissance Aircraft to Iwakuni Air Base in Japan. On November 12, Taiwan returned to the two-party unification talks after some PRC assurances that no further actions would be taken along the economic, military, or informational lines of effort. These talks were viewed by the U. S. as a de-escalatory step by the PRC.

4.  \(E\) On May 28, a PLAN Luyang II-class guided missile destroyer *Changchung* and a Jiangwei II-class frigate attempted to interdict the Taiwan guided missile destroyer ROCS *MA KONG* 22 NM east of the Matsu Islands. Gunfire was exchanged and the Taiwan task group withdrew with damage to MA KONG. A senior PLA (N) representative in a World News live interview condemned the Taiwanese for instigating the action and stated that the PRC would “use force, if necessary, to ensure freedom of navigation.” A press release from the PRC Presidential spokesman emphasized the affair as “entirely Chinese with a Chinese solution.”

5.  \(E\) On June 1, 2026 two USMC F-18Cs from Kadena, Japan, en route to Singapore conducted an emergency landing at Tainan Airport. The PRC immediately protested what it considered a blatant U.S. military provocation and “test” of Chinese resolve. The next day, the PRC Government ordered home two PLAN Frigates from anti-piracy and NEO operations vicinity Yemen.On June 8, 2026 an explosion of unknown origin ripped through the construction site of the future Wind Lion Plaza on Quemoy (Kinmen) Island (24°27'N 118°24'E). Quemoy Island (also called Kinmen), administered by the Taiwan government since 1949, is part of the PRC-Taiwan disputed Matsu Island group and only 10 km from Xiamen, a large city on mainland China. In recent years, the Taiwan government has taken advantage of the diplomatic thaw between the PRC and Taiwan by withdrawing the majority of the Taiwanese Army garrison from Quemoy and by developing the island as a future destination for Chinese and other international tourists. The multi-billion dollar Wind Lion Plaza, inspired by the Taiwan Land Development Corporation as the economic centerpiece, employs several hundred Chinese nationals. In addition to the millions of dollars in damages caused by the explosion, eight Chinese nationals were killed and over 20 critically injured. While the PRC government accused Taiwan of lax and irresponsible security measures, unidentified heavily armed groups seized control of Kinmen Airport and Kinmen Seaport in the early hours of June 10. Within 24 hours, several unidentified battalion-sized military groups had arrived by helicopter, “secured” the local government house, and disarmed the small garrison. The PRC, claiming that it had “indisputable evidence of terrorist activity” announced that it would honor a request for assistance in “securing” Quemoy Island while promising to hold a popular referendum on possibly returning the island and the rest of the Matsu group to PRC control: Quemoy Island, once a part of China, should once again be part of China. PRC television showed throngs of cheering crowds, allegedly the population of Quemoy, rejoicing at the arrival of Chinese security forces.

6.  \(E\) The Government of Taiwan immediately and forcefully protested this “unprovoked military adventure” and requested assistance from the United Nations, ASEAN, and the Governments of the Philippines, Japan, Australia, and the United States. The United States and Australia, while condemning the PRC, sought and received a UN agreement to monitor a new series of two party talks between the PRC and Taiwan but failed to secure a UNSCR condemning the occupation, due to the PRC’s veto and Russian abstention. The Governments of the Philippines and of Japan remained noncommittal, publicly preferring to allow the UN and ASEAN to determine an “appropriate course of action that maintains Asian stability.” As all parties sought a diplomatic solution to the crisis, UN-led two-party talks between China and Taiwan resumed on June 14.

7.  \(E\) On June 18, U.S. NSA imagery revealed a significant buildup of PRC land forces in the vicinity of Xiamen, on the mainland opposite the Matsu Islands. The Government of Taiwan protested this “unprovoked attempt to threaten a democratic, free-market nation” and immediately increased its naval presence in and around Matsu with two guided missile destroyers. A low level exchange of liaison officers occurred between the United States and Taiwan which was immediately exploited by the PRC’s central news organization: “*The Renegade Province Prepares for War with Imperialist Support*.” After an attempt at direct negotiations with the PRC President, the POTUS directed the Secretary of War and the CDRUSPACOM to prepare to execute Noncombatant Evacuation Operation (NEO) of U.S. citizens on the Matsu Islands and possibly Taiwan.

8.  \(E\) On June 13, 2026, the PRC announced that it had decided, “after considerable patience in the face of unconscionable provocation and with near universal international support” to reunite Taiwan with the mainland—by force if necessary. Only then would stability return to the region. In the early morning hours of Sunday, June 21 (Hong Kong Time), a missile and rocket barrages were reported landing on Taiwanese garrison locations on several other islands of the Matsu island group. This was followed by pre-dawn multi-brigade sized amphibious assaults by the PRC Army and Navy with support by their Air Force. With Quemoy already secured, there was little fighting and within 24 hours, all the Matsu Islands were secured by PLA Naval and Army forces. Taiwan immediately requested formal U. S. military assistance for what it described as, “the last true chance for democracy in China.” In response, at 0535 EST 26 June 2026, POTUS directed Commander, U. S. Pacific Command to issue a planning order to its component commands to develop a range of options, including military, which could be employed to deescalate the situation and to seek a return to the status quo.

**4. (E) Strategic Environment (Relationships, Alliances, Frictions, Tensions).**

1.  **(E) Adversaries and Competitors - Summary.** North Korea continues to disregard United Nations sanctions by developing, and threatening to use intercontinental ballistic missiles and nuclear weapons that will threaten the U.S. Homeland. China has fundamentally altered the physical and political landscape in the South China Sea through large scale land reclamation and by militarizing these reclaimed features. Beijing continues to press Japan in the East China Sea, is stepping up diplomatic, economic, and now military pressure against Taiwan, and is methodically trying to supplant U.S. influence with our friends and allies in the region. Furthermore, China is rapidly building a modern, capable military that appears to far exceed its stated defensive purpose or potential regional needs. China’s military modernization is focused on defeating the U.S. in Asia by countering U.S. asymmetric advantages. China’s military modernization cannot be understated, especially when we consider the Communist regime’s lack of transparency and apparent strategy. China is committed to developing a hypersonic glide weapon and advanced cyber and anti-satellite capabilities that present direct threats to the Homeland. China’s near term strategy is focused on building up combat power and positional advantage to be able to restrict freedom of navigation and overflight while asserting de facto sovereignty over disputed maritime features and spaces in the region. Russia, while consumed by its war in Ukraine, is once again exercising its conventional forces and nuclear strike capabilities in the Pacific, which also threaten the Homeland. Transnational terrorists have set their sights on the Indo-Asia-Pacific by supporting and encouraging attacks in Indonesia, Bangladesh, Philippines, and Malaysia while recruiting and fund-raising there and elsewhere. Drug trafficking, human smuggling, piracy, weapons proliferation, natural disasters – as well as illegal, unreported, and unregulated fishing – further challenge regional peace and prosperity.

    1.  \(E\) The U.S.-China relationship remains complex. While Chinese actions and provocations create tension in the region, there have also been opportunities for cooperation. USPACOM’s approach to China has been to cooperate where we can to collectively address our shared security challenges, but remain ready to confront its provocative actions where we must.

    2.  \(E\) China is a significant military and economic power: a costly war between the US and China would have an enormously negative impact on the global economy. While competitors, the U.S. and China are economically dependent upon each other and cooperate on a number of international issues such as nuclear arms control. Continued viability of the PRC economy and the trade between that economy and that of the United States and its allies is critical to international political and market stability. The current crisis has increased tensions between China and the U.S. to the point of armed conflict.

    3.  \(E\) As a permanent member of the UN Security Council, China can veto any proposed UNSCR. Diplomatically, however, the PRC continues to be viewed by the international community with suspicion, even by those not aligned with the West, due to their use of transactional (and often predatory) relationships. Thus, the seizure of Quemoy and Matsu islands were negatively viewed by the international community who perceive China as the aggressor.

    4.  \(E\) Mainland China and Taiwan are economically interdependent and the economic success of Taiwan invariably leads to significant economic benefits of Mainland China. The PRC views unification as inevitable but would prefer a unification similar to the peaceful absorption of Hong Kong by China in 1997.

    5.  \(E\) China is a peer competitor of the U.S. with a strong, well-equipped, well-led, and well-trained military, and is capable of denying U.S. access to Taiwan and the approaches to Taiwan. Long range fires can reach as far as Guam and U.S. allies such as Japan, Korea, and the Philippines are all within long-range missile and drone range. Unlike other regional conflicts that the U.S. has been involved in, the PRC has the capability to open new theaters across the globe, in cyberspace, and in space.

    6.  \(E\) PRC policy is to garner international recognition of Chinese pre-eminence in the Western Pacific and international acceptance of the immediate reunification of Mainland China with Taiwan. Strategically, the PRC seeks to isolate Taiwan from its Western Pacific neighbors; force the Taiwan regime to recognize and adopt political reunification with the Mainland China; increase international concern over economic disruption to facilitate quick resolution; and color the international perception of U.S. military assistance as a colonial and racist power on the wrong side of history.

    7.  \(E\) Likely strategic objectives of the PRC:

        1.  \(E\) Intimidate Taiwan leadership by demonstrating the ability to control their economic lifeline.

        2.  \(E\) Deter the Taiwan President and other political factions from pursuing pro-independence policies.

        3.  \(E\) Limit U.S. and international support for Taiwan.

    8.  \(E\) While the PRC is politically prepared to withstand sanctions and embargoes, it lacks strategic depth in natural resources and has few international friends that would risk the ire of the West to provide these critical resources. Their strengths are in their military - especially “at home”, their superior use of Information, and the solidarity of support from their people.

2.  **(E) Allies and Partners - Summary.** USPACOM can count on a number of staunch allies, resolute partners, and multilateral organizations and agreements in the region to confront PRC aggression against Taiwan.

    1.  \(E\) **(Ally)** Australia: The U.S.-Australia alliance anchors peace and stability in the region. Australia plays a leading role in regional security, capacity-building efforts and addressing disaster response. Cooperative activities under Enhanced Air Cooperation, another force posture initiative, formally commenced in February 2017 with the deployment of F-22 aircraft to northern Australia. The U.S. and Australia are increasing collaboration in counter-terrorism, space, cyber, integrated air missile defense, and regional capacity building. Australia has procured high-tech U.S. platforms that will further increase interoperability. These include the F-35A Lightning II, P-8 Poseidon, C-17 Globemaster III, EA-18G Growler, Global Hawk UAVs, and MH-60R helicopters. (See para. 1.b.xiii. AUKUS) The Royal Australian Navy (RAN) is modern and interoperable with the USN, deploying two amphibious assault ships, three AEGIS-capable destroyers, and several dozen frigates, submarines, and multirole vessels.

    2.  \(E\) **(Ally)** Japan: The U.S.-Japan alliance remains the cornerstone for peace and stability in the Indo-Asia-Pacific region. Under the Defense Policy Review Initiative (DPRI), the U.S. began transferring III MEF from Okinawa to Camp Blaz on Guam in order to reduce U.S. presence on Okinawa to less than 50%. Aggressive activities by the PRC, however, may force a review of this policy. Japan continues to support USPACOM activities to maintain freedom of navigation in the South China Sea, and remains concerned about Chinese activities in the East China Sea. The Japanese Maritime Self-Defense Force remains one of the most capable navies in the world, with 2 DDH helicopter carriers, 8 Aegis-capable guided missile destroyers, 23 diesel-electric submarines, and an additional 40+ destroyers, frigates, and destroyer escorts of various classes. With the recent addition of the F-35B to light multirole carriers JS *Kaga (CVM-184)* and JS *Izumo (CVM-183)*, the JMSDF presents a formidable maritime challenge to PLAN activities in the East China Sea and the northern approaches to the Strait of Taiwan.

    3.  \(E\) **(Ally)** Republic of Korea (ROK): The U.S.-ROK alliance remains ironclad. In response to the evolving threat posed by North Korea, the U.S. and the ROK made an Alliance decision to maintain the deployment of the Terminal High Altitude Area Defense (THAAD) system in the ROK. North Korea’s provocative actions, and its refusal to engage in authentic and credible negotiations on denuclearization, compelled our Alliance to take defensive measures. The ROK Navy is a substantial maritime force, interoperable with the USN and regional allies. Deploying over 20 diesel electric submarines, the ROK Navy also can employ four AEGIS-capable guided missile destroyers, an additional 27 surface combatants (destroyers and frigates), and one of the almost capable amphibious task forces in the Pacific.

    4.  \(E\) **(Ally)** The Philippines: The U.S.-Philippine alliance remains resolute. Since 2016, the Philippine administration has fully supported the Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement. Project development at various Philippine bases have improved interoperability and built partner capacity of the Armed Forces of the Philippines (AFP) in Maritime Security, Maritime Domain Awareness, and HA/DR capabilities. We remain committed to supporting the AFP to counter-terrorism not only in the Southern Philippines, but in the tri-border area in Sulu and Celebes Seas. At the request of several Philippine administrations, Special Operation Command Pacific (SOCPAC) continues to provide counter-terrorism support and assistance. Additionally, the 41st iteration of Exercise BALIKATAN held from APR- MAY 2026 demonstrates the commitment both Armed Forces have towards maintaining U.S.-Filipino security.

    5.  \(E\) **(Ally)** Thailand: The longstanding U.S.-Thailand alliance is supported by deep bilateral military-to-military ties that go back to our 1950 Agreement Respecting Military Assistance between the Government of the United States of America and Government of Thailand.

    6.  \(E\) **(Partner)** Singapore: A key strategic partner in Southeast Asia, we depend on Singapore for its insights on regional dynamics and its support to U.S. security priorities. Singapore has been a major security cooperation partner for over a decade and provides us invaluable access including hosting of Littoral Combat Ships, Maritime Patrol and Reconnaissance Aircraft, and the Seventh Fleet’s Logistics Force headquarters. Recently, our partnership expanded into new areas including cyber security and counter-proliferation. We conduct dozens of military exercises with Singapore each year and Singaporean military officers regularly attend U.S. professional military education. This combination of forward deployed forces, logistics, and deep training relationships contributes to readiness, builds deeper ties and allows the U.S. to promote maritime security and stability with regional partners.

    7.  \(E\) **(Partner)** India: Since 2016, India has emerged as a Major Defense Partner to the U.S. This declaration is unique to India and places it on the same level as many of our closest allies for the purposes of defense trade and technology sharing. We signed the Logistics Exchange Memorandum of Agreement (LEMOA) after more than a decade of negotiation to further deepen our military-to-military relationship and serve as a force multiplier during exercises and real world HA/DR operations.

    8.  \(E\) **(Partner)** Indonesia: Indonesia plays an essential role in the security architecture of the region. We maintain a robust defense relationship particularly in maritime security. Indonesia desires to play a larger role in international economic and security issues. Indonesia continues to build and exercise in strategic maritime border areas to bolster its defense capabilities, and has concerns with Chinese activities in the vicinity of the Natuna Islands.

    9.  \(E\) **(Partner)** Malaysia: Our close security ties with Malaysia are based on our Comprehensive Partnership. We continue to assist Malaysia in building an amphibious force to address non-traditional threats in and around their territorial waters. Malaysia has reached a trilateral agreement with the Philippines and Indonesia for improving the maritime security environment in the Sulu and Celebes Seas. Malaysia also has an on-going dispute with China with respect to the Luconia Shoals, which China also claims.

    10. \(E\) **(Partner)** Mongolia: Mongolia endures as a small yet strong partner in Northeast Asia and continues to demonstrate staunch support for U.S. regional and global policy objectives – especially those linked to the Global Peace Operations Initiative. The government engages with the U.S. and other countries as part of their “Third Neighbor” policy.

    11. \(E\) **(Partner)** Vietnam: Vietnam continues to expand cooperation with the U.S. at a moderate, but steady pace. USPACOM provides support for Vietnam's modernization and capacity building, focusing on maritime security, peacekeeping, and disaster response. The U.S. has transferred maritime security vessels including maintenance and training packages to Vietnam's Coast Guard, which has built their capacity for maritime domain awareness.

    12. \(E\) Oceania: Maintaining strategic influence in Oceania is becoming ever more important to U.S. national security. The provisions included in the Compacts of Free Association with the Federated States of Micronesia, the Republic of the Marshall Islands, and the Republic of Palau are important mechanisms that guide the relationships, including U.S. obligations for their defense. In return, these agreements provide assured access to the three Compact Nations in a contingency situation. They also give the U.S. authority to grant or deny access to another nation’s military forces which allows the U.S. to maintain a clear strategic line of communication across the Pacific.

    13. \(E\) AUKUS: <span class="mark">AUKUS is a trilateral security pact between Australia, the UK, and the US, launched in September 2021. Its core focus is countering China's influence in the Indo-Pacific region through two "pillars", Nuclear-Powered Submarines and Advanced Capabilities. The U.S. has agreed to provide Australia Nuclear powered submarines to replace its aging fleet and Australia will provide submarine basing access in the near future. Pillar II has the allies collaborate on sharing military technology and expertise across six key technological areas: artificial intelligence and autonomy, quantum technologies, undersea robotics, hypersonic missiles, advanced cyber, and electronic warfare.The pact is designed to foster deep integration of the three nations' defense industrial bases.</span>

# TAIWAN RELATIONS ACT PUBLIC LAW 96-8 96TH CONGRESS

January 1, 1979

##### An Act

To help maintain peace, security, and stability in the Western Pacific and to promote the foreign policy of the United States by authorizing the continuation of commercial, cultural, and other relations between the people of the United States and the people on Taiwan, and for other purposes.

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled,

##### Short Title

SECTION 1. This Act may be cited as the “Taiwan Relations Act”.

##### Findings and Declaration of Policy

Section. 2.

1.  The President- having terminated governmental relations between the United States and the governing authorities on Taiwan recognized by the United States as the Republic of China prior to January 1, 1979, the Congress finds that the enactment of this Act is necessary–

    1.  to help maintain peace, security, and stability in the Western Pacific; and

    2.  to promote the foreign policy of the United States by authorizing the continuation of commercial, cultural, and other relations between the people of the United States and the people on Taiwan.

2.  It is the policy of the United States–

    1.  to preserve and promote extensive, close, and friendly commercial, cultural, and other relations between the people of the United States and the people on Taiwan, as well as the people on the China mainland and all other peoples of the Western Pacific area;

    2.  to declare that peace and stability in the area are in the political, security, and economic interests of the United States, and are matters of international concern;

    3.  to make clear that the United States decision to establish diplomatic relations with the People’s Republic of China rests upon the expectation that the future of Taiwan will be determined by peaceful means;

    4.  to consider any effort to determine the future of Taiwan by other than peaceful means, including by boycotts or embargoes, a threat to the peace and security of the Western Pacific area and of grave concern to the United States;

    5.  to provide Taiwan with arms of a defensive character; and

    6.  to maintain the capacity of the United States to resist any resort to force or other forms of coercion that would jeopardize the security, or the social or economic system, of the people on Taiwan.

3.  Nothing contained in this Act shall contravene the interest of the United States in human rights, especially with respect to the human rights of all the approximately eighteen million inhabitants of Taiwan. The preservation and enhancement of the human rights of all the people on Taiwan are hereby reaffirmed as objectives of the United States.

##### Implementation of United States Policy with Regard to Taiwan

Section. 3.

1.  In furtherance of the policy set forth in section 2 of this Act, the United States will make available to Taiwan such defense articles and defense services in such quantity as may be necessary to enable Taiwan to maintain a sufficient self-defense capability.

2.  The President and the Congress shall determine the nature and quantity of such defense articles and services based solely upon their judgment of the needs of Taiwan, in accordance with procedures established by law. Such determination of Taiwan’s defense needs shall include review by United States military authorities in connection with recommendations to the President and the Congress.

3.  The President is directed to inform the Congress promptly of any threat to the security or the social or economic system of the people on Taiwan and any danger to the interests of the United States arising therefrom. The President and the Congress shall determine, in accordance with constitutional processes, appropriate action by the United States in response to any such danger.

##### Application of Laws; International Agreements

Section. 4.

1.  The absence of diplomatic relations or recognition shall not affect the application of the laws of the United States with respect to Taiwan, and the laws of the United States shall apply with respect to Taiwan in the manner that the laws of the United States applied with respect to Taiwan prior to January 1, 1979.

2.  The application of subsection (a) of this section shall include, but shall not be limited to, the following:

    1.  Whenever the laws of the United States refer or relate to foreign countries, nations, states, governments, or similar entities, such terms shall include and such laws shall apply with such respect to Taiwan.

    2.  Whenever authorized by or pursuant to the laws of the United States to conduct or carry out programs, transactions, or other relations with respect to foreign countries, nations, states, governments, or similar entities, the President or any agency of the United States Government is authorized to conduct and carry out, in accordance with section 6 of this Act, such programs, transactions, and other relations with respect to Taiwan (including, but not limited to, the performance of services for the United States through contracts with commercial entities on Taiwan), in accordance with the applicable laws of the United States.

        1.  The absence of diplomatic relations and recognition with respect to Taiwan shall not abrogate, infringe, modify, deny, or otherwise affect in any way any rights or obligations (including but not limited to those involving contracts, debts, or property interests of any kind) under the laws of the United States heretofore or hereafter acquired by or with respect to Taiwan.

        2.  For all purposes under the laws of the United States, including actions in any court in the United States, recognition of the People’s Republic of China shall not affect in any way the ownership of or other rights or interests in properties, tangible and intangible, and other things of value, owned or held on or prior to December 31, 1978, or thereafter acquired or earned by the governing authorities on Taiwan.

    3.  Whenever the application of the laws of the United States depends upon the law that is or was applicable on Taiwan or compliance therewith, the law applied by the people on Taiwan shall be considered the applicable law for that purpose.

    4.  Nothing in this Act, nor the facts of the President’s action in extending diplomatic recognition to the People’s Republic of China, the absence of diplomatic relations between the people on Taiwan and the United States, or the lack of recognition by the United States, and attendant circumstances thereto, shall be construed in any administrative or judicial proceeding as a basis for any United States Government agency, commission, or department to make a finding of fact or determination of law, under the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 and the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Act of 1978, to deny an export license application or to revoke an existing export license for nuclear exports to Taiwan.

    5.  For purposes of the Immigration and Nationality Act, Taiwan may be treated in the manner specified in the first sentence of section 202(b) of that Act.

    6.  The capacity of Taiwan to sue and be sued in courts in the United States, in accordance with the laws of the United States, shall not be abrogated, infringed, modified, denied, or otherwise affected in any way by the absence of diplomatic relations or recognition.

    7.  No requirement, whether expressed or implied, under the laws of the United States with respect to maintenance of diplomatic relations or recognition shall be applicable with respect to Taiwan.

3.  For all purposes, including actions in any court in the United States, the Congress approves the continuation in force of all treaties and other international agreements, including multilateral conventions, entered into by the United States and the governing authorities on Taiwan recognized by the United States as the Republic of China prior to January 1, 1979, and in force between them on December 31, 1978, unless and until terminated in accordance with law.

4.  Nothing in this Act may be construed as a basis for supporting the exclusion or expulsion of Taiwan from continued membership in any international financial institution or any other international organization.

##### Overseas Private Investment Corporation

Section. 5.

1.  During the three-year period beginning on the date of enactment of this Act, the \$1,000 per capita income restriction in insurance, clause (2) of the second undesignated paragraph of section 231 of the reinsurance, Foreign Assistance Act of 1961 shall not restrict the activities of the Overseas Private Investment Corporation in determining whether to provide any insurance, reinsurance, loans, or guaranties with respect to investment projects on Taiwan.

2.  Except as provided in subsection (a) of this section, in issuing insurance, reinsurance, loans, or guaranties with respect to investment projects on Taiwan, the Overseas Private Insurance Corporation shall apply the same criteria as those applicable in other parts of the world.

##### The American Institute of Taiwan

Section. 6.

1.  Programs, transactions, and other relations conducted or carried out by the President or any agency of the United States Government with respect to Taiwan shall, in the manner and to the extent directed by the President, be conducted and carried out by or through–

    1.  The American Institute in Taiwan, a nonprofit corporation incorporated under the laws of the District of Columbia, or

    2.  such comparable successor nongovermental entity as the President may designate, (hereafter in this Act referred to as the “Institute”).

2.  Whenever the President or any agency of the United States Government is authorized or required by or pursuant to the laws of the United States to enter into, perform, enforce, or have in force an agreement or transaction relative to Taiwan, such agreement or transaction shall be entered into, performed, and enforced, in the manner and to the extent directed by the President, by or through the Institute.

3.  To the extent that any law, rule, regulation, or ordinance of the District of Columbia, or of any State or political subdivision thereof in which the Institute is incorporated or doing business, impedes or otherwise interferes with the performance of the functions of the Institute pursuant to this Act; such law, rule, regulation, or ordinance shall be deemed to be preempted by this Act.

##### Services by the Institute to United States Citizens on Taiwan

Section. 7.

1.  The Institute may authorize any of its employees on Taiwan–

    1.  to administer to or take from any person an oath, affirmation, affidavit, or deposition, and to perform any notarial act which any notary public is required or authorized by law to perform within the United States;

    2.  To act as provisional conservator of the personal estates of deceased United States citizens; and

    3.  to assist and protect the interests of United States persons by performing other acts such as are authorized to be performed outside the United States for consular purposes by such laws of the United States as the President may specify.

2.  Acts performed by authorized employees of the Institute under this section shall be valid, and of like force and effect within the United States, as if performed by any other person authorized under the laws of the United States to perform such acts.

##### Tax Exempt Status of the Institute

SECTION. 8.

1.  The Institute, its property, and its income are exempt from all taxation now or hereafter imposed by the United States (except to the extent that section 11(a)(3) of this Act requires the imposition of taxes imposed under chapter 21 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, relating to the Federal Insurance Contributions Act) or by State or local taxing authority of the United States.

2.  For purposes of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, the Institute shall be treated as an organization described in sections 170(b)(1)(A), 170(c), 2055(a), 2106(a)(2)(A),, 2522(a), and 2522(b).

##### FURNISHING PROPERTY AND SERVICES TO AND OBTAINING SERVICES FROM THE INSTITUTE

Section. 9.

1.  Any agency of the United States Government is authorized to sell, loan, or lease property (including interests therein) to, and to perform administrative and technical support functions and services for the operations of, the Institute upon such terms and conditions as the President may direct. Reimbursements to agencies under this subsection shall be credited to the current applicable appropriation of the agency concerned.

2.  Any agency of the United States Government is authorized to acquire and accept services from the Institute upon such terms and conditions as the President may direct. Whenever the President determines it to be in furtherance of the purposes of this Act, the procurement of services by such agencies from the Institute may be effected without regard to such laws of the United States normally applicable to the acquisition of services by such agencies as the President may specify by Executive order.

3.  Any agency of the United States Government making funds available to the Institute in accordance with this Act shall make arrangements with the Institute for the Comptroller General of the United States to have access to the; books and records of the Institute and the opportunity to audit the operations of the Institute.

##### Taiwan Instrumentality

Section. 10.

1.  Whenever the President or any agency of the United States Government is authorized or required by or pursuant to the laws of the United States to render or provide to or to receive or accept from Taiwan, any performance, communication, assurance, undertaking, or other action, such action shall, in the manner and to the. extent directed by the President, be rendered or Provided to, or received or accepted from, an instrumentality established by Taiwan which the President determines has the necessary authority under the laws applied by the people on Taiwan to provide assurances and take other actions on behalf of Taiwan in accordance with this Act.

2.  The President is requested to extend to the instrumentality established by Taiwan the same number of offices and complement of personnel as were previously operated in the United States by the governing authorities on Taiwan recognized as the Republic of China prior to January 1, 1979.

3.  Upon the granting by Taiwan of comparable privileges and immunities with respect to the Institute and its appropriate personnel, the President is authorized to extend with respect to the Taiwan instrumentality and its appropriate; personnel, such privileges and immunities (subject to appropriate conditions and obligations) as may be necessary for the effective performance of their functions.

##### Separation of Government Personnel for Employment with the Institute

Section. 11.

1.  Under such terms and conditions as the President may direct, any agency of the United States Government may separate from Government service for a specified period any officer or employee of that agency who accepts employment with the Institute.

2.  An officer or employee separated by an agency under paragraph (1) of this subsection for employment with the Institute shall be entitled upon termination of such employment to reemployment or reinstatement with such agency (or a successor agency) in an appropriate position with the attendant rights, privileges, and benefits with the officer or employee would have had or acquired had he or she not been so separated, subject to such time period and other conditions as the President may prescribe.

3.  An officer or employee entitled to reemployment or reinstatement rights under paragraph (2) of this subsection shall, while continuously employed by the Institute with no break in continuity of service, continue to participate in any benefit program in which such officer or employee was participating prior to employment by the Institute, including programs for compensation for job-related death, injury, or illness; programs for health and life insurance; programs for annual, sick, and other statutory leave; and programs for retirement under any system established by the laws of the United States; except that employment with the Institute shall be the basis for participation in such programs only to the extent that employee deductions and employer contributions, as required, in payment for such participation for the period of employment with the Institute, are currently deposited in the program’s or system’s fund or depository. Death or retirement of any such officer or employee during approved service with the Institute and prior to reemployment or reinstatement shall be considered a death in or retirement from Government service for purposes of any employee or survivor benefits acquired by reason of service with an agency of the United States Government.

4.  Any officer or employee of an agency of the United States Government who entered into service with the Institute on approved leave of absence without pay prior to the enactment of this Act shall receive the benefits of this section for the period of such service.

<!-- -->

1.  Any agency of the United States Government employing alien personnel on Taiwan may transfer such personnel, with accrued allowances, benefits, and rights, to the Institute without a break in service for purposes of retirement and other benefits, including continued participation in any system established by the laws of the United States for the retirement of employees in which the alien was participating prior to the transfer to the Institute, except that employment with the Institute shall be creditable for retirement purposes only to the extent that employee deductions and employer contributions.. as required, in payment for such participation for the period of employment with the Institute, are currently deposited in the system’ s fund or depository.

2.  Employees of the Institute shall not be employees of the United States and, in representing the Institute, shall be exempt from section 207 of title 18, United States Code.

    1.  For purposes of sections 911 and 913 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, amounts paid by the Institute to its employees shall not be treated as earned income. Amounts received by employees of the Institute shall not be included in gross income, and shall be exempt from taxation, to the extent that they are equivalent to amounts received by civilian officers and employees of the Government of the United States as allowances and benefits which are exempt from taxation under section 912 of such Code.

    2.  Except to the extent required by subsection (a)(3) of this section, service performed in the employ of the Institute shall not constitute employment for purposes of chapter 21 of such Code and title II of the Social Security Act.

##### Reporting Requirement

Section. 12.

1.  The Secretary of State shall transmit to the Congress the text of any agreement to which the Institute is a party. However, any such agreement the immediate public disclosure of which would, in the opinion of the President, be prejudicial to the national security of the United States shall not be so transmitted to the Congress but shall be transmitted to the Committee on Foreign Relations of the Senate and the Committee on Foreign Affairs of the House of Representatives under an appropriate injunction of secrecy to be removed only upon due notice from the President.

2.  For purposes of subsection (a), the term “agreement” includes-

    1.  any agreement entered into between the Institute and the governing authorities on Taiwan or the instrumentality established by Taiwan; and

    2.  any agreement entered into between the Institute and an agency of the United States Government.

3.  Agreements and transactions made or to be made by or through the Institute shall be subject to the same congressional notification, review, and approval requirements and procedures as if such agreements and transactions were made by or through the agency of the United States Government on behalf of which the Institute is acting.

4.  During the two-year period beginning on the effective date of this Act, the Secretary of State shall transmit to the Speaker of the House and Senate House of Representatives and the Committee on Foreign Relations of Foreign Relations the Senate, every six months, a report describing and reviewing economic relations between the United States and Taiwan, noting any interference with normal commercial relations.

##### RULES AND REGULATIONS

SECTION. 13.

The President is authorized to prescribe such rules and regulations as he may deem appropriate to carry out the purposes of this Act. During the three-year period beginning on the effective date speaker of this Act, such rules and regulations shall be transmitted promptly to the Speaker of the House of Representatives and to the Committee on Foreign Relations of the Senate. Such action shall not, however, relieve the Institute of the responsibilities placed upon it by this Act.’

##### Congressional Oversight

Section. 14.

1.  The Committee on Foreign Affairs of the House of Representatives, the Committee on Foreign Relations of the Senate, and other appropriate committees of the Congress shall monitor-

    1.  the implementation of the provisions of this Act;

    2.  the operation and procedures of the Institute;

    3.  the legal and technical aspects of the continuing relationship between the United States and Taiwan; and

    4.  the implementation of the policies of the United States concerning security and cooperation in East Asia.

2.  Such committees shall report, as appropriate, to their respective Houses on the results of their monitoring.

##### Definitions

Section. 15. For purposes of this Act-

1.  the term “laws of the United States” includes any statute, rule, regulation, ordinance, order, or judicial rule of decision of the United States or any political subdivision thereof; and

2.  the term “Taiwan” includes, as the context may require, the islands of Taiwan and the Pescadores, the people on those islands, corporations and other entities and associations created or organized under the laws applied on those islands, and the governing authorities on Taiwan recognized by the United States as the Republic of China prior to January 1, 1979, and any successor governing authorities (including political subdivisions, agencies, and instrumentalities thereof).

##### Authorization of Appropriations

Section. 16.

In addition to funds otherwise available to carry out the provisions of this Act, there are authorized to be appropriated to the Secretary of State for the fiscal year 1980 such funds as may be necessary to carry out such provisions. Such funds are authorized to remain available until expended.

##### Severability of Provisions

Section. 17.

If any provision of this Act or the application thereof to any person or circumstance is held invalid, the remainder of the Act and the application of such provision to any other person or circumstance shall not be affected thereby.

##### Effective Date

Section. 18.

This Act shall be effective as of January 1, 1979. Approved April 10, 1979.

        1.  **(E) Option 1: Deny, Delay, Build.** Deny the Taiwan Strait through use of air, submarines, and cyberspace operations. Delay direct military action, and Build forces and the coalition. This option seeks to avoid a direct military engagement with the military forces of PRC and counts on cooler heads in PRC political leadership to de-escalate the situation in the face of a robust, rapid build-up of US forces in the region. Initial priority of US maritime forces is protection and assurance of commerce in the South China Sea.

        2.  **(E) Option 2: Immediate Blockade**. Deny the Taiwan Strait through use of air, submarines, and cyberspace operations. Concurrently impose a maritime blockade on PRC maritime traffic based on their violations of UNCLOS and illegal seizure of the islands of Quemoy and Matsu. This, combined with forward deployed amphibious landing group, imposed economic sanctions, and diplomatic isolation, forces PRC leadership to stand down military action against Taiwan and shift back to competition in the diplomatic arena. An additional CSG will be needed for obtaining and maintaining control of the South China Sea.

        3.  **(E) Option 3. Defend in Depth.** Present a defense of Taiwan, with coalition partners, that forces PLAN leadership to avoid a general attack due to imposed costs. Deny the Taiwan Strait through use of air, submarines, and cyberspace operations. Initial priority of US maritime forces is the neutralization of the PLAN should they sortie from Mainland China. Secondary priority is the protection and assurance of commerce in the South China Sea. Reliance on air power and AMD to destroy PRC long range fires and drones, if used. Offensive action is a last resort.

        4.  **(E) Option 4. Pre-emptive Strike.** As the PRC assumes that US forces will not take action until forces have built up, engage immediately with available air and maritime assets against critically vulnerable infrastructure and platforms such as missile batteries, PLAN carrier groups, and landing craft. Absorb the response and continue to build up forces. As Admiral Nimitz proved at Midway (June 1942), in maritime warfare, all you have to hit is the one or two targets that actually matter. This option likely devolves into a long-range missile/drone war that defeats the purposes of PRC, which was unification of Taiwan with Mainland China.

5.  \(E\) Evaluate the proposed Termination Criteria and recommend modification.

    1.  Taiwan defended from PRC attempt to forcibly unify Taiwan with Mainland China. PRC military and para-military forces withdrawn from Quemoy and Matsu and both islands returned to Taiwan sovereignty. Readiness postures of PRC and Taiwan military forces returned to pre-conflict levels. PRC amphibious forces returned to garrison. PRC offensive air, sea, and submarine patrols terminated. Freedom of navigation assured in the straits. Freedom of air and sea commerce assured.

6.  \(E\) Evaluate proposed Theater Post-Conflict Considerations and recommend modification.

    1.  Outside independent security force-in-place to prevent near-future rupture in ceasefire and to separate forces. Reconciliation and territorial disputes back in the diplomatic arena. Long-term HA assistance, if required. Economic and infrastructure restoration established.

## 4. (**E) Commander’s Initial Intent**

1.  \(E\) Purpose. To protect the territory, peoples, and trade access of Taiwan, and to enable the free and unencumbered political self-determination of its inhabitants.

2.  \(E\) Method. Demonstrate resolve through a robust joint/combined force, maritime presence, overflights, and force build-up. Establish ALOCs and SLOCs. Employ C4ISR assets to determine PLAN submarine locations, PLAN movements and dispositions, and PRC information, cyber, and space activities in the JOA. After a forward defense to deter or disrupt any cross-strait attack, I expect that we will need to retain enough capability to lead a counter offensive effort to Quemoy and Matsu islands.

3.  \(E\) Endstate. Political stability and physical security of Taiwan ensured and conditions established for amicable and freely-chosen resolution of cross-straits political issues.

4.  \(E\) Risk. The current probability of achieving military objectives is questionable based on PRC proximity to the straits and the current relative force disparity between USPACOM and PRC maritime and air forces in the region. Mitigation of risk must include rapid formation of a regional coalition to force the PRC into calculating the political cost of carrying out a cross-straits attack on Taiwan; rapid deployment of air, maritime, ground, and cyber forces to the region which can impose significant military cost on a cross-strait attack; and robust amphibious forces as a signal of counter-offensive capability at a time/place of our choosing.

5.  \(E\) The J5 leads the JPG into development of a Commander’s Estimate that includes designation of OPTs for evaluation of Options and recommendation of one Option as the desired COA; recommended termination criteria; and recommended theater post-conflict considerations. Once Commander’s Estimate is reviewed and approved by CJCS/SECWAR/POTUS, we will rapidly transition the selected COA into a Level 4 CONPLAN. Selected OPTs develop the unselected options into Level 2/3 (with TPFDD-L) branch plans.

# **OPERATION SEA TIGER — RUNNING ESTIMATES**

**UNITED STATES PACIFIC COMMAND STAFF**

**DTG: 260500Z JUN 2026**

PURPOSE. These running estimates state current status, capabilities, and shortfalls across the joint functions to inform mission analysis. They are status products that feed the Commander's Estimate; they are not a course-of-action comparison and do not recommend an option.

**1. COMMAND AND CONTROL (J6 / J3).**

> \- Status. USPACOM is the supported command. Commander, Seventh Fleet (C7F) is designated Commander, Joint Task Force–Western Pacific and Commander, Combined Task Force. USS Blue Ridge (LCC-19) is available at N as the afloat command platform. A USPACOM-developed Multinational Force Standing Operating Procedure exists.
>
> \- Shortfalls. No combined Taiwan–United States rules of engagement exist; supplemental rules of engagement must be requested. Coalition integration will be executed largely by deconfliction rather than full integration. Positioning, navigation, and timing and satellite communications are assessed at risk from People's Republic of China (PRC) cyberspace and counter-space activity.

**2. INTELLIGENCE (J2).**

> \- Status. The Joint Intelligence Operations Center and Joint Intelligence Center Pacific provide centralized analysis. Maritime patrol (P-8A at Kadena; MQ-4C at Andersen) and national systems support indications and warning. Coalition intelligence support is provided by India, Indonesia, Thailand, and Malaysia.
>
> \- Priority gaps. Locations and readiness of PLAN submarines; indicators of a PLAN Eastern Sea Fleet amphibious sortie from Ningbo; PRC missile and drone readiness against Okinawa, Guam, and coalition bases; PRC intent regarding escalation and cyberspace/space effects; the distinction between consolidation of the seized islands and preparation for a cross-strait assault on Taiwan proper. A historically poor track record in assessing PRC strategic intent argues for a capability-based focus.

**3. FIRES (J3 / Component).**

> \- Status. United States air, surface, subsurface, and space-enabled fires hold a range advantage and can range PRC targets within the Joint Operations Area and on the mainland. B-52 (Guam) and carrier air are available at N; F-35A at Kadena at N.
>
> \- Shortfalls. Precision-guided-munition stocks are assessed inadequate for a protracted campaign. PRC integrated air defenses, missile-armed combatants, and land-based missile forces contest the fires problem. Low-collateral-damage guidance constrains targeting.

**4. MOVEMENT AND MANEUVER (J3 / J5).**

> \- Status. Carrier and amphibious forces provide maritime and air maneuver advantage. The 31st Marine Expeditionary Unit (Okinawa) and the Amphibious Ready Group are available at N+1.
>
> \- Binding constraint. Time and distance dominate: the bulk of United States forces require seven to ten days to close, while Taiwan can hold unaided for no more than approximately fifteen days. Confined waters in the Strait place United States submarines at elevated risk; the Luzon (Bashi) Strait and the sea lines east of Taiwan favor United States surface, air, and subsurface operations.

**5. PROTECTION (J3 / J34).**

> \- Status. Air and missile defense includes ballistic-missile-defense-capable cruisers (Shiloh, Cape St. George) and allied and Taiwanese air-defense systems. Taiwan air and missile defense is on alert.
>
> \- Shortfalls. Forward bases at Okinawa, Guam, the Philippines, and Singapore are high-value targets for PRC missile, drone, submarine, and cyberspace attack. Mine-countermeasures capacity and detection against conventionally-powered submarines are assessed inadequate. American citizens in Taiwan require evacuation before military options are exercised.

**6. SUSTAINMENT (J4).**

> \- Status. Combat logistics force, expeditionary fast transports (Saipan, N+2), the hospital ship Mercy (N+14), and construction battalions are available on the notice-to-move schedule. Singapore ship-repair-facility support is to be coordinated. A forward operating base at Clark Air Base (Philippines) is directed for strategic lift.
>
> \- Binding constraint. Long lines of communication, few operational aerial ports of debarkation, and contested sea ports of debarkation make throughput — not stockholding — the limiting factor. Sustainment over distance is the principal feasibility driver.

**7. INFORMATION (J39 / J3).**

> \- Status. Extensive information and cyberspace capability exists (Fleet Cyber Command / Tenth Fleet, Cyber Group One, Fleet Information Warfare Center Pacific). The PRC narrative frames Taiwan as the renegade-province disrupter and the United States as a weak, colonial actor.
>
> \- Requirement. Sustained messaging to hold the international perception of the PRC as the aggressor and to preserve coalition cohesion, integrated with electromagnetic-warfare geolocation of jamming and cyberspace effects, under retained-authority caveats.

**8. CIVIL-MILITARY / HOST-NATION (J9 / J5).**

> \- Status. Taiwan has a population of twenty-three million and is one of the most digitally connected societies in the world; its economy is central to the global technology supply chain. Fourteen percent of the population is of mainland-Chinese origin, presenting an infiltration and influence risk.
>
> \- Requirement. Noncombatant evacuation of American citizens; coordination with Taiwan authorities and coalition partners; low-collateral-damage discipline consistent with the humanitarian and civil-military guidance in the Warning Order.
