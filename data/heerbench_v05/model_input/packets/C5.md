## C5:D01 — Strategic Context

UNCLASSIFIED // FOR EXERCISE / TRAINING USE ONLY (E)

# **STRATEGIC CONTEXT (GULF GUARDIAN)**

**PRODUCT TYPE: USSOUTHCOM J5 strategic context note.**

UNCLASSIFIED // FOR EXERCISE / TRAINING USE ONLY (E)

**1. (E) THE ROAD TO CRISIS.**

\(E\) For the past year the United States has pursued the democratic reform of Cuba by economic pressure: a sustained oil blockade that denies the island the fuel it cannot produce, and the relisting of Cuba as a State Sponsor of Terrorism, which has severed it from what remained of the international financial system. The pressure has not moved the regime; it has broken the country. Fuel imports have fallen by roughly two thirds, the national electrical grid now fails for sixteen hours a day or more, water and sanitation depend on power the grid cannot supply, and the ration system no longer reaches the provinces. The Communist Party of Cuba and the Revolutionary Armed Forces remain in control, but they preside over a population in extremity.

**2. (E) THE EXODUS.**

\(E\) As in 1980 and again in 1994, Cubans have turned to the sea. Departures from the beaches around Havana, Matanzas, and Cardenas have risen to a level not seen since the balsero crisis, most of them in unseaworthy craft that the Florida Current carries northeast into the Straits. Because the United States blockade already governs what crosses those waters, the sea approaches are effectively under United States control; the exodus is therefore a problem of scale, of the safety of life at sea, and of what is to be done with those who are taken from the water, rather than a contest with a Cuban fleet.

**3. (E) THE TURN TO ATROCITY.**

\(E\) The regime has decreed that illegal departure will be punished by imprisonment or, for those who resist, by lethal force, and it has carried that decree out. National Revolutionary Police killed fourteen people at the coastal town of Carbonera; a Cuban naval vessel rammed and sank a boat carrying twenty-five migrants off the coast of Havana; soldiers fired on a crowd at the landward gate of the United States Naval Base at Guantanamo Bay, killing five; and the Cuban diaspora has published photographs of mass killings carried out with armed drones against civilians in the northern coastal towns. Cuba denies the killings and has throttled internet access across the island, but the evidence continues to reach the world.

**4. (E) THE FAILED UNITED NATIONS ROUTE AND THE NO-FLY ZONE.**

\(E\) The President sought a United Nations Security Council resolution authorizing a United Nations-led force to protect Cuban civilians. The Russian Federation and the People's Republic of China vetoed it, and both now describe the atrocity reporting as imperialist propaganda intended to manufacture a pretext for invasion. Acting without a Security Council mandate, the President declared a no-fly zone over the Cuban coast and stated that any further killings would be dealt with forcefully. The United States thus acts on a civilian-protection rationale under customary international law, over the objection of two great powers, and the legitimacy of that case is now itself a strategic center of gravity.

**5. (E) UNITED STATES INTERESTS AND AIMS.**

\(E\) The United States seeks to stop the killing of Cuban civilians, to prevent further loss of life at sea, to enforce the no-fly zone and the blockade, to avoid a wider armed conflict with the Cuban state, to sustain the legitimacy of the civilian-protection effort against the adversary narrative, and to preserve for national decision the graver options of acting ashore, whether to protect civilians directly or to enable a change of regime.

**6. (E) THE DISPOSITION PROBLEM AND ITS POLITICS.**

\(E\) What becomes of those taken from the water is a strategic question in its own right. No one will be returned to Cuba. Processing will begin outside the continental United States, at Guantanamo Bay and in Puerto Rico, and the question of whether to bring Cubans onward to processing sites in the continental United States, as was done during the Mariel influx, is a national decision that divides domestic opinion sharply in both directions: bringing them ashore would satisfy the Cuban American community and much moderate opinion while drawing strong opposition from constituencies favoring immigration restriction, and declining to do so would reverse those reactions. The commander must plan for the reception and processing that either course requires while leaving the decision itself to national authority.

**7. (E) THE SHADOW OF THE GRAVER OPTIONS.**

\(E\) The direction to prepare, on order, plans for operations ashore and for regime change hangs over the whole effort. It is planning only, gated on a decision by the National Command Authority, and it must be held apart, in fact and in perception, from the protective operation that begins now, lest it hand Havana, Moscow, and Beijing the invasion narrative they are seeking.

**1. (E) BOTTOM LINE. A United States oil blockade and the relisting of Cuba as a State Sponsor of Terrorism have collapsed the Cuban economy and driven a mass seaborne exodus. The regime is killing its own civilians ashore and along the coast, including by armed drone, and has sunk a migrant boat off Havana. Cuba cannot contest the Straits, the declared no-fly zone, or United States forces with the negligible naval and air power it retains; the danger lies in continued repression ashore, coastal incidents, armed drones of unconfirmed origin, and the risk of miscalculation. Whether the regime is escalating deliberately or reacting to its own collapse is unresolved.**

**2. (E) ECONOMIC COLLAPSE. Fuel imports have fallen roughly two thirds under the blockade. The national grid fails for sixteen hours a day or more; water, sanitation, medical care, and food distribution have degraded accordingly. The Communist Party of Cuba and the Revolutionary Armed Forces retain control, but popular discontent is broad, if leaderless.**

**3. (E) THE MARITIME EXODUS. Departures from the northern coast around Havana, Matanzas, and Cardenas have risen to a level not seen since the 1994 balsero crisis, most in unseaworthy craft. The Florida Current sets northeast at two to four knots, carrying craft off track and making detection and timely rescue difficult. Because the blockade already governs maritime movement, the exodus is a problem of scale and of safety of life at sea rather than a contest with a Cuban fleet.**

**4. (E) LETHAL REPRESSION AND ATROCITIES.**

\(E\) The regime decreed that illegal departure would be punished by imprisonment or lethal force and has acted on it. National Revolutionary Police killed fourteen people at Carbonera. A Cuban naval vessel rammed and sank a boat carrying twenty-five migrants off the coast of Havana. Soldiers fired on a crowd at the landward gate of the United States Naval Base at Guantanamo Bay, killing five. The Cuban diaspora has published photographs of mass killings carried out with armed drones against civilians in the northern coastal towns.

\(E\) Cuba denies the killings and has sharply restricted internet access to suppress the evidence; imagery and testimony nonetheless continue to reach the outside world through the diaspora.

**5. (E) ADVERSARY MILITARY CAPABILITY. The Revolutionary Armed Forces are a fuel-starved legacy force. Cuba has no navy able to operate in the open Straits and no armed air force able to contest United States air power. Its maritime capability is limited to a small number of coastal patrol craft operating near its own ports, one of which sank the boat off Havana. Its air-defense and fighter inventory is largely non-operational. The one capability of concern is the armed drones employed against civilians; their type, numbers, and origin are not established.**

**6. (E) ADVERSARY INTENT. The regime's overriding aim is assessed to be survival: to seal the coast and halt the exodus, to deter organized opposition through terror, and to shift blame for Cuban suffering onto the United States. Whether it intends to escalate deliberately, by driving the flow toward the United States or provoking an incident, or is simply reacting to collapse, is unresolved and is a priority intelligence question.**

**7. (E) INFORMATION ENVIRONMENT AND GREAT-POWER DIMENSION. Cuban messaging blames the United States blockade for civilian suffering and frames United States presence as aggression. After the United States sought a United Nations resolution authorizing a protection force, the Russian Federation and the People's Republic of China vetoed it and now characterize the atrocity reporting as imperialist propaganda intended to justify invasion. Whether Russian or Chinese support extends beyond diplomacy to material or military assistance, including the armed drones, is not established.**

**8. (E) THREATS TO GUANTANAMO BAY. The killing of civilians at the landward gate demonstrates the willingness of Cuban forces to use lethal force at the perimeter. The base is an isolated enclave behind a Cuban military screen; further perimeter incidents, harassment, or attempts to exploit the crisis for propaganda are assessed as likely.**

**9. (E) KEY INTELLIGENCE GAPS / PRIORITY REQUIREMENTS. The provenance and inventory of the Cuban armed drones and coastal craft; whether the regime intends deliberate escalation; projected flow rates, embarkation points, and craft types; the intentions of the Russian Federation and the People's Republic of China, and any material support to Cuba; the true scale and locations of the atrocities ashore; and the status of threats to Guantanamo Bay.**

## C5:D05 — Country Fact Sheet

UNCLASSIFIED // FOR EXERCISE / TRAINING USE ONLY (E)

# **COUNTRY FACT SHEET — REPUBLIC OF CUBA (GULF GUARDIAN)**

**PRODUCT TYPE: USSOUTHCOM J2 country fact sheet.**

UNCLASSIFIED // FOR EXERCISE / TRAINING USE ONLY (E)

**1. (E) GEOGRAPHY.**

\(E\) Cuba is the largest island in the Caribbean, roughly 1,250 kilometers long and astride the approaches to the Gulf of Mexico. Its northern coast faces the Straits of Florida; the nearest point of Cuba lies approximately ninety nautical miles from Key West. Principal northern coastal population centers and embarkation points include Havana, Matanzas, Cardenas, and the smaller town of Carbonera; Mariel lies just west of Havana. The southeastern coast holds the bay of Guantanamo, within which the United States Naval Base is an enclave on otherwise Cuban territory.

**2. (E) PEOPLE AND GOVERNMENT.**

\(E\) Cuba's population is approximately eleven million. The state is a one-party socialist republic controlled by the Communist Party of Cuba; executive authority rests with the President and the Council of State, and coercive power with the Revolutionary Armed Forces (FAR) and the Ministry of the Interior (MININT). Public order and border control are exercised by the National Revolutionary Police (PNR) and the Border Guard (Tropas Guardafronteras), both subordinate to MININT.

**3. (E) ECONOMY AND INFRASTRUCTURE.**

\(E\) The economy has collapsed under the oil blockade and the loss of financial access. Cuba produces little of its own fuel and depends on imports it can no longer obtain in quantity; the resulting shortfall has crippled electrical generation, with rolling blackouts of sixteen hours a day or more, and has broken the distribution of food and water. Medical care, sanitation, and refrigeration have degraded accordingly. The humanitarian condition of the population is severe and worsening.

**4. (E) MILITARY OVERVIEW.**

\(E\) The Revolutionary Armed Forces are a legacy force built on aging Soviet-era equipment and hollowed by the fuel and economic crisis. Cuba has no navy capable of operating in the open Straits and no armed air force able to contest United States air power; its maritime capability is limited to a small number of coastal patrol craft operating close to its own ports, one of which sank the migrant boat off Havana. Cuban security forces have employed armed drones against civilians in the northern coastal towns; the origin of those drones, whether indigenous, obtained on the grey market, or externally supplied, is not established and is a priority intelligence question. The instruments of internal repression are the PNR, MININT, and the Border Guard.

**5. (E) INFORMATION ENVIRONMENT.**

\(E\) The government controls the media and has sharply restricted internet access to suppress evidence of the killings; despite this, imagery and testimony continue to leave the island through the diaspora. Cuban official messaging blames the United States blockade for the population's suffering and frames any United States presence as aggression against Cuban sovereignty.

**6. (E) GUANTANAMO BAY.**

\(E\) The United States Naval Base at Guantanamo Bay is a long-standing United States enclave on the southeastern coast, surrounded by Cuban territory and a Cuban military screen. It has served in past contingencies (notably 1994) as a migrant safe-haven and processing site and retains a Migrant Operations Center for that purpose. It is presently both a potential reception site and an installation under threat, following the killing of civilians at its landward gate.

**7. (E) EXTERNAL RELATIONS.**

\(E\) Cuba retains political alignment with the Russian Federation, the People's Republic of China, and Venezuela. Russia and China have shielded Cuba diplomatically, vetoing the proposed United Nations protection force and characterizing United States action as an imperialist pretext for invasion. Whether that support extends to material or military assistance is not established.

## C5:D06 — Critical Geography

UNCLASSIFIED // FOR EXERCISE / TRAINING USE ONLY (E)

# **CRITICAL GEOGRAPHY (GULF GUARDIAN)**

**PRODUCT TYPE: USSOUTHCOM J2/J3 critical-geography note.**

UNCLASSIFIED // FOR EXERCISE / TRAINING USE ONLY (E)

**1. (E) THE STRAITS OF FLORIDA.**

\(E\) The Straits of Florida separate Cuba from the Florida Keys by roughly ninety nautical miles at the narrowest. The Florida Current sets northeast through the strait at two to four knots, carrying small, overloaded, low-freeboard craft off their intended tracks and toward United States waters; the same current makes drifting craft hard to detect and shortens the time available to reach people in distress. Sea state and afternoon thunderstorms in the spring season add to the hazard to small craft.

**2. (E) EMBARKATION AND THE COASTAL TOWNS.**

\(E\) The bulk of departures originate from the northern coast around Havana, Matanzas, and Cardenas, with the smaller town of Carbonera among the launch points; Mariel, west of Havana, was the principal embarkation port in the 1980 exodus. These same towns are where the regime's lethal repression, including the armed-drone attacks, has occurred, so the embarkation coast and the atrocity coast are the same ground.

**3. (E) THE INTERCEPTION AREA.**

\(E\) The operational area for interception and rescue is the international waters of the Straits and the Cuban coastal approaches. Operations are to be kept south of and well clear of United States territorial waters and the Florida Keys, so the effort remains a maritime operation conducted abroad rather than a domestic law-enforcement problem. This places the interception belt across the middle of the strait and along the Cuban side, athwart the current that carries the craft.

**4. (E) THE NO-FLY ZONE.**

\(E\) The declared no-fly zone covers the Cuban coastal approaches and extends over a belt of Cuban sovereign airspace along the northern coast, where the drone killings have occurred. Enforcing it places United States aircraft over and adjacent to Cuban territory, a deliberate step onto sovereign ground short of a landing, and requires basing and tanking sufficient to hold the zone continuously.

**5. (E) GUANTANAMO BAY.**

\(E\) Guantanamo Bay is an isolated United States enclave on the southeastern coast, backed by a Cuban military screen and reachable only by air and sea. It holds a Migrant Operations Center usable for reception and processing and is simultaneously an installation whose landward perimeter has already been the scene of lethal violence. It must be treated as both a place of safety and a point of vulnerability.

**6. (E) PUERTO RICO AND RECEPTION BASING.**

\(E\) Puerto Rico offers United States territory outside the continental United States for reception and processing, with port and airfield infrastructure (including former Naval Station Roosevelt Roads) and reachable by sea and air lift. Together with Guantanamo Bay it forms the outside-the-continental-United-States processing base the operation requires; the distances involved make strategic and intratheater lift a limiting factor.

**7. (E) DISTANCES AND LINES.**

\(E\) Key planning distances: Cuba to Key West approximately ninety nautical miles; Havana to the Florida Keys a comparable distance across the current; Guantanamo Bay is on the far southeastern coast, roughly the length of the island from Havana; Puerto Rico lies well to the east of Cuba. The strait's width, the current, and the dispersal of the flow across multiple embarkation points make persistent, wide-area maritime domain awareness the hardest geographic problem.

**1. (E) THE OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT. The operational environment is the Straits of Florida and the northern Cuban littoral, bounded to the north by United States territorial waters and the Florida Keys, which the operation deliberately holds clear of, and anchored in the southeast by the United States enclave at Guantanamo Bay. It is shaped by the oil blockade, which controls maritime movement and has caused the humanitarian collapse; by the Florida Current, which carries small craft northeast off their tracks; by the concentration of the exodus and of the regime's atrocities along the same northern coast; and by an information environment in which Cuban denial, restricted internet, and Russian and Chinese messaging contest the legitimacy of United States action.**

**2. (E) ADVERSARY CENTER OF GRAVITY. The Cuban regime's strategic center of gravity is assessed to be regime survival through coercive control of the population and the coast. Its critical capabilities rest on the security services (MININT, the National Revolutionary Police, the Border Guard) and on its information narrative; its critical requirements are the ability to seal the coast and to sustain internal control. Its critical vulnerabilities are the economic, food, and energy collapse, its eroding legitimacy, its dependence on visible repression that generates the very evidence it seeks to deny, and its inability to contest the sea or the air.**

**3. (E) FRIENDLY LEGITIMACY AS A CENTER OF GRAVITY. Because the United States acts to protect civilians without a United Nations mandate and over a Russian and Chinese veto, the legitimacy of the civilian-protection case is itself a friendly center of gravity. Sustaining it, and denying the adversary the invasion narrative, is a condition for coalition, partner, and domestic support.**

**4. (E) ADVERSARY COURSES OF ACTION.**

\(E\) Most likely: the regime continues lethal repression ashore and along the coast, employs armed drones against civilians, conducts occasional coastal patrol-craft interceptions near its ports, sustains its denial-and-blame information campaign, and probes the Guantanamo perimeter, while avoiding any deliberate clash with United States forces it cannot win.

\(E\) Most dangerous: the regime escalates deliberately, by driving or weaponizing the flow to create a mass-casualty event at sea, by a provocation at Guantanamo Bay, or, enabled by undisclosed external support, by fielding more capable armed drones or reviving elements of its air defense to contest the no-fly zone.

**5. (E) KEY TERRAIN AND AVENUES. The embarkation beaches around Havana, Matanzas, and Cardenas; the mid-strait drift lines set by the Florida Current; the Cuban coastal-approach and northern-coast airspace of the no-fly zone; the Guantanamo Bay fenceline; and alternate seaborne vectors through the Windward Passage and toward partner littorals.**

**6. (E) INFORMATION AND GREAT-POWER DIMENSION. The contest over whether this is civilian protection or an invasion pretext is central. Russia and China shield Cuba diplomatically; whether they provide material support, including the armed drones, is unresolved and is a decisive intelligence question.**

**7. (E) INTELLIGENCE GAPS AND PRIORITY REQUIREMENTS. The provenance and inventory of the armed drones and coastal craft; whether the regime intends deliberate escalation; projected flow rates and embarkation points; the intentions of Russia and China and any material support; the true scale of the atrocities; and threats to Guantanamo Bay.**

**1. (E) LEGAL BASIS FOR THE OPERATION. The maritime interception, the blockade, and the no-fly zone are conducted in international waters and adjacent Cuban airspace under the customary international law of the sea and the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, and under the President's constitutional authority for the protection of life and the conduct of foreign relations. The operation therefore proceeds without a Security Council mandate, on a civilian-protection rationale, and the legitimacy of that rationale must be actively sustained.**

**2. (E) USE OF FORCE. Forces operate under the Standing Rules of Engagement (CJCSI 3121.01 series). Supplemental measures are authorized for enforcement of the no-fly zone and the protection of civilians from attacks in progress, for unit and national self-defense, and for the minimum force necessary to compel compliance with the blockade. **3. (E) DISPOSITION OF INTERCEPTED PERSONS. No intercepted Cuban will be returned to Cuba; the customary prohibition on returning persons to persecution, non-refoulement, is honored as a matter of policy. **4. (E) DOMESTIC-LAW CONSIDERATIONS. The operation is kept in international waters and the Cuban approaches, south of and clear of United States territorial waters and the Florida Keys, so that it remains a military operation conducted abroad and does not trigger the domestic law-enforcement, border, and Posse Comitatus considerations that would attach to operations in United States waters or territory. The Coast Guard operates under its own authorities in support of the maritime interception.**

**1. (E) SITUATION (Ref. Regional Intelligence Summary).**

\(E\) Strategic Environment. A sustained United States oil blockade and the relisting of Cuba as a State Sponsor of Terrorism, both intended to compel democratic reform, have instead collapsed the island's fuel, power, and food distribution while leaving the regime in place. Fuel imports have fallen sharply, the national grid now suffers blackouts of sixteen hours or more, and food no longer moves reliably to the provinces. The Communist Party of Cuba and the Revolutionary Armed Forces retain control of the state and its security services, and the discontent that has followed remains broad but leaderless. Seaborne departures have risen to a level not seen since the 1994 balsero crisis. Because the blockade already governs what crosses the Straits, the maritime approaches are largely under our control; the problem the flow presents is therefore one of scale and of the safety of life at sea, not one of contesting a Cuban fleet.

\(E\) The Turn to Atrocity. What began as a migration emergency has become something graver. The Cuban diaspora has published photographs of mass killings in the northern coastal towns, carried out with armed drones against civilians gathering to leave, and has pressed the President to act. The Republic of Cuba denies the killings and has throttled internet access across the island, yet the evidence continues to reach the outside world. The President sought a United Nations Security Council resolution authorizing a United Nations-led force to protect Cuban civilians; the Russian Federation and the People's Republic of China vetoed it, and both now characterize the reporting as imperialist propaganda meant to manufacture a pretext for invasion. The President has responded by declaring a no-fly zone over the Cuban coast and stating that any further killings will be dealt with forcefully.

\(E\) Adversary Actions. The regime decreed that illegal departure would be punished by imprisonment or, for those who resist, by lethal force, and it has carried that decree out. Cuban National Revolutionary Police killed fourteen people at the coastal town of Carbonera; a Cuban naval vessel rammed and sank a boat carrying twenty-five migrants off the coast of Havana; Cuban soldiers fired on a crowd at the landward gate of the United States Naval Base at Guantanamo Bay, killing five; and armed drones have been used to kill civilians in the northern coastal towns. The regime's public line holds the United States blockade responsible for Cuban suffering and casts any United States presence as aggression against Cuban sovereignty.

\(E\) Likely Regime Objectives. I assess the regime's overriding aim to be its own survival, pursued by sealing the coast to halt an exodus that is both a humanitarian embarrassment and a visible signal of state failure, by deterring organized opposition through terror, and by shifting the blame for Cuban suffering onto the United States. Whether the regime intends to escalate deliberately, by driving the flow toward us or provoking an incident, or is reacting in panic to its own collapse, is not resolved, and I want that treated as a question to be answered rather than an assumption to be made.

\(E\) Adversary Capability. Cuba cannot contest the open sea, the airspace of the Straits, or United States forces: the Revolutionary Armed Forces are a fuel-starved legacy force (see Adversary Order of Battle) with no capacity to sortie against the fleet and no ability to defend against our air power. What it retains is a limited coastal reach: a handful of patrol craft operating close to its own ports, one of which sank the boat off Havana, and a number of armed drones it has turned against its own civilians ashore. The provenance of those drones and of any external support is not established, and I am making it a priority intelligence requirement; I will not assume it, and planners should be alert to the possibility of a foreign hand even as Moscow and Beijing confine themselves, for now, to diplomatic cover. The realistic danger is not a Cuban fleet or air action against us but continued lethal repression of civilians ashore and along the coast, an incident at the Guantanamo perimeter, and the escalation risk that our own enforcement of the no-fly zone carries.

\(E\) Partners. Jamaica, the Dominican Republic, Colombia, and Mexico, wary of onward migration to their own shores, and the United Kingdom, with its standing Caribbean patrol presence, have offered to assist. At least two regional partners are prepared to receive Cubans who cannot safely return until conditions on the island change, and the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees is prepared to supervise reception and processing. The terms, the consent, and the capacity each will provide remain to be arranged.

\(E\) Operational Environment. The decisive terrain is the Straits of Florida, roughly ninety nautical miles wide, where the Florida Current sets northeast at two to four knots and carries small, overloaded, hard-to-detect craft off their tracks. I intend to work the Cuban coastal approaches and the international waters of the Straits, and to keep our surface and interception operations south of and well clear of United States territorial waters and the Florida Keys, so that the maritime effort remains an operation conducted abroad and does not become a domestic law-enforcement problem in our own waters. The no-fly zone the President has declared extends over the Cuban coastal approaches and over a belt of Cuban sovereign airspace along the northern coast, where the drone killings are occurring; enforcing it will place our aircraft over and adjacent to Cuban territory, and I want the escalation that implies understood and planned for. Guantanamo Bay is an isolated United States enclave on a hostile shore, backed by a Cuban military screen, and it holds a Migrant Operations Center used to shelter and process migrants in earlier contingencies.

\(E\) Desired National End State. The blockade is enforced and the no-fly zone is upheld; the killing of civilians ashore and at sea is stopped; the seaborne exodus is intercepted and rescued without further preventable loss of life at sea; no one is returned to Cuba; intercepted migrants are processed outside the continental United States first, at Guantanamo Bay and in Puerto Rico, and, on national decision, moved onward to processing sites in the United States, with willing third countries and the United Nations available to share the burden; Guantanamo Bay remains secure; a wider war with the Cuban state is avoided; and options to act ashore, whether to protect civilians or to enable regime change, are preserved for national decision without being committed.

**2. (E) OPERATIONAL PROBLEM.**

\(E\) Problem Statement. The problem is how USSOUTHCOM, in support of the Department of Homeland Security and the United States Coast Guard and with willing regional partners, prevents further loss of Cuban life in the Straits of Florida, cares for interdicted migrants lawfully, and protects Guantanamo Bay, while avoiding armed conflict with the Cuban state and preparing regime-change options on order.

\(E\) Commander's Initial Analysis. This is a crisis-action problem in which time is the dominant factor: civilians are being killed ashore and along the coast now, people are drowning at sea now, and the flow is expected to grow, so credible interception, rescue, and no-fly-zone enforcement capacity must be on station at once. Several features will shape every option. First, this is a Maritime Interception Operation with an air-protection mission layered onto it, and the Navy leads it. We interdict, that is, deny passage to, the arms and oil the blockade forbids; we intercept, that is, detect, board, search, and take from the water, the people attempting the crossing; and we now also enforce a no-fly zone to stop the killing of civilians from the air. Those are distinct tasks and I want them spoken of with precision. The Coast Guard is integral to the interception and to the handling of those we bring aboard, and the Department of Homeland Security remains the lead for their immigration disposition; but the operation itself is a Navy-led military operation, not a law-enforcement action, and I do not intend to conduct the maritime piece inside United States waters, where it would become one. Second, a word on terms. I will refer to those we take from the water as migrants. That is a deliberate choice: the label keeps their disposition a matter of policy and does not, of itself, confer a legal status we are in no position to adjudicate from the deck of a cutter. Planners should nonetheless understand that many are in fact refugees fleeing violence, that the longer they remain displaced the more that status will assert itself, and that the distinction bears directly on where and how they may lawfully be sent; treat it as a matter for analysis. Third, the President has already used force in declaring the no-fly zone and warning that further killings will be met forcefully, and I read my authority accordingly: I am authorized now to enforce the no-fly zone and to use force to stop atrocities in progress, including engaging drones or coastal craft caught in the act of attacking civilians. That is deliberate, limited force to protect non-combatants; it is not a general war with Cuba, and it is not license for the graver steps. Fourth, feasibility will turn less on combat power than on throughput: whether the combined interception, rescue, and reception capacity, afloat, at the Guantanamo Migrant Operations Center, and at partner facilities under United Nations supervision, can keep pace with the flow. No one we take from the water will be returned to Cuba; on that point I want no ambiguity, and the customary prohibition on returning people to persecution is therefore honored as a matter of policy. The planning problem is not whether to repatriate but where to process and shelter those we intercept, and what that requires of us. Disposition begins outside the continental United States, at Guantanamo Bay and in Puerto Rico, and I am treating the establishment of that reception and processing capacity as a requirement, because it will define our basing, our lift, and our sustainment. Whether we then move Cubans onward to processing sites in the continental United States, as was done during the Mariel influx, is a national decision with sharp domestic political consequences in both directions, and I want it framed as a decision, not pre-empted. Fifth, we are acting without a Security Council mandate, over a Russian and Chinese veto, and against an adversary information campaign that calls our evidence a pretext for invasion; the legitimacy of the civilian-protection case is therefore a center of gravity in its own right, and nothing we do should hand Havana, Moscow, or Beijing the invasion narrative they are seeking. Sixth, and gravest, the hardest decision this crisis may force on me is ashore: whether to put forces on Cuban soil to protect civilians or as the leading edge of the regime-change option I have been directed to prepare. That decision carries the full weight of sovereignty and national commitment; I want mission analysis to frame it honestly, to identify the indicators and decision points that would bring it before me, and to keep it distinct, in fact and in perception, from the operation we begin now. Guantanamo Bay must be treated as both a place of safety and an installation under threat.

**3. (E) OPERATIONAL APPROACH.**

\(E\) Objectives. The military objectives I am pursuing are stated as end-conditions rather than tasks: the blockade is enforced and the no-fly zone is upheld; the killing of civilians ashore and at sea is stopped; further preventable loss of life at sea is prevented; intercepted migrants are protected and lawfully processed, none returned to Cuba, with reception and processing established outside the continental United States; a wider armed conflict with the Cuban state is avoided and Guantanamo Bay remains secure; the legitimacy of the civilian-protection effort is preserved; and the options to act ashore are available for national decision without being committed.

\(E\) Assumptions for Initial Planning. Plan on the assumption that the oil blockade and the relisting remain in effect and the flow continues or accelerates; that reception and processing capacity can be established at Guantanamo Bay and in Puerto Rico, with willing partners and the United Nations available to share the burden once terms are agreed; that Cuba cannot contest the no-fly zone or the fleet with the negligible naval and air forces it possesses, though coastal incidents, drone attacks, and miscalculation remain likely; that Russia and China will confine themselves to diplomatic and information measures rather than material intervention, a judgment to be watched closely; and that operations ashore and regime change remain planning tasks only pending a decision by the National Command Authority. Confirm or refute each as mission analysis develops.

\(E\) Available Forces. Joint Task Force-Southern Spear, built on U.S. Naval Forces Southern Command and the U.S. Fourth Fleet, is the executor. Forces available for planning include Carrier Strike Group 12 (Gerald R. Ford), whose air wing is the instrument of no-fly-zone enforcement; the Iwo Jima Amphibious Ready Group with the 22d Marine Expeditionary Unit, which is the instrument of any decision to act ashore; Air Forces South and its counter-air and counter-unmanned-aircraft capability; Special Operations Command South; United States Coast Guard cutters and aviation with the Maritime Security Response Team, the workhorses of interception and rescue; and Department of Justice elements. The Nimitz Carrier Strike Group is present in theater for the annual round-South-America exercise, apportionable, and the natural source of additional combat power for the no-fly zone or for operations ashore. See the Coalition, Friendly, and Interagency Order of Battle.

\(E\) Operational Concept. My concept is a Navy-led Maritime Interception Operation with a layered air-protection mission: establish persistent maritime domain awareness and an interception belt in the international waters of the Straits and along the Cuban approaches; detect, board, search, and rescue; enforce the arms and oil blockade; enforce the no-fly zone over the Cuban coast and its sovereign-airspace belt, defeating drones or aircraft attacking civilians; build afloat, Guantanamo, and partner reception throughput under United Nations supervision sufficient for the flow; protect Guantanamo Bay; win the argument that this is protection and not invasion; and hold the options ashore and the regime-change planning on order and closely compartmented. Save lives, act lawfully, keep the maritime effort south of United States waters, and give the regime and its patrons no pretext.

\(E\) Directed Tasks to USSOUTHCOM. Begin maritime interception operations and at-sea rescue on order. Enforce the arms and oil blockade. Enforce the declared no-fly zone over the Cuban coast, and use force to stop attacks on civilians in progress, within the President's declared authority. In support of the Department of Homeland Security and the United States Coast Guard, establish and operate reception and processing capacity for intercepted migrants outside the continental United States, at Guantanamo Bay and in Puerto Rico, sufficient for the flow, and identify the basing, lift, and sustainment it requires; return no one to Cuba; and prepare options, for national decision, for onward movement to processing sites in the continental United States and for burden-sharing with willing third countries and the United Nations. Protect the United States Naval Base at Guantanamo Bay. Conduct the information effort to sustain the legitimacy of the civilian-protection case and to counter the invasion narrative. Be prepared, on order, to conduct operations ashore to protect civilians; this is a planning task only. On order, develop plans to initiate regime change in Cuba, setting the conditions for a transition of power; this is a planning task only, and execution requires a decision by the National Command Authority. Coordinate with the Department of State on diplomacy, third-country processing, and United Nations arrangements, and with the Department of Justice on law-enforcement contingencies. Conduct mission analysis, develop courses of action, and submit the mission-analysis brief, the Commander's Estimate, and requests for forces, authorities, and rules of engagement.

\(E\) Course-of-Action Development. Develop and compare distinct, executable courses of action. Evaluate each for suitability against the objectives above, for feasibility against the balance of the flow and our interception and reception throughput, and for acceptability against the requirement to protect civilians and enforce the no-fly zone while avoiding a wider war, to keep the maritime effort clear of United States waters, and to handle the people we intercept lawfully. Address in each the rules for the use of force for no-fly-zone enforcement and the protection of civilians, for self-defense, and for compelling compliance with the blockade; the disposition and processing of intercepted persons; the protection of Guantanamo Bay; the information effort; and the indicators that would bring the decision to act ashore before me.

\(E\) Proposed Termination Criteria. Evaluate and recommend modification: the killing of civilians stopped and the no-fly zone uncontested; preventable deaths at sea halted; the flow managed to a sustainable level; intercepted migrants safe and lawfully processed outside the continental United States and, as nationally decided, onward, with none returned to Cuba; no wider armed conflict with the Cuban state and Guantanamo Bay secure; and the options ashore developed and preserved without commitment. Translate these into measurable, observable criteria in the Commander's Estimate.

\(E\) Proposed Post-Crisis Considerations. Evaluate the transition of the effort to the Department of Homeland Security and the interagency for durable disposition, to include onward movement to the continental United States and resettlement; the sustainment of partner and United Nations arrangements; the protracted-displacement problem of Cubans who cannot go home until conditions change; and the enduring information and legitimacy contest.

**4. (E) COMMANDER'S INITIAL INTENT.**

\(E\) Purpose. To stop the killing of Cuban civilians ashore and at sea and the preventable deaths of migrants in the water; to protect our people and installation at Guantanamo Bay; to enforce the President's no-fly zone and blockade; to keep a protective operation from becoming either a wider war with Cuba or a law-enforcement problem in our own waters; and to preserve for national decision the graver options ashore.

\(E\) Method. Place Navy-led interception, rescue, and no-fly-zone enforcement capacity on station quickly, with the Coast Guard integral to the maritime effort and the carrier air wing and Air Forces South enforcing the zone; use force to stop atrocities in progress within the President's declared authority; enforce the blockade; establish afloat, Guantanamo, and Puerto Rico reception and processing capacity, with partner and United Nations support, to receive and care for those we take from the water, returning no one to Cuba; protect Guantanamo Bay; sustain the legitimacy of the civilian-protection case and counter the invasion narrative; act with restraint toward Cuban forces beyond what protection requires; keep the maritime effort south of United States waters; and hold the options ashore and the regime-change planning on order and compartmented.

\(E\) End State. The killing stopped and the no-fly zone upheld; preventable deaths at sea ended; intercepted migrants safe, lawfully handled, and processed outside the continental United States, none returned to Cuba; no wider war with Cuba and Guantanamo Bay secure; the legitimacy of the effort intact; partners and the United Nations sharing the burden; and the options ashore preserved without commitment.

\(E\) Risk. The gravest risks are that our interception and reception capacity is outpaced by the flow and people drown whom we might have saved; that enforcing the no-fly zone over Cuban sovereign airspace, or an incident at the Guantanamo perimeter, escalates toward the wider war or the commitment ashore before either is chosen; that Russia and China move from diplomatic cover to material support; that the decision on where to send those we intercept, above all whether to bring Cubans to the continental United States, divides domestic opinion sharply whichever way it goes, satisfying the Cuban American community and much moderate opinion if we bring them ashore and drawing strong opposition from constituencies that favor immigration restriction, and reversing those reactions if we do not; and that the adversary information campaign, or a misstep of ours, delegitimizes the effort and turns it into the invasion narrative they are selling. I will accept moderate risk to force to place rescue capacity and no-fly-zone enforcement where they protect life, and moderate risk to tempo for the safety of migrants and civilians; I will not accept an unforced escalation into a wider war with the Cuban state, an operation ashore absent a decision by the National Command Authority, or the unlawful or inhumane handling of the people we intercept.

\(E\) The J5 leads the Joint Planning Group in developing the Commander's Estimate, to include the restated mission; the specified, implied, and essential tasks; the commander's critical information requirements, to include the provenance of the drones and the intentions of Russia and China; the assumptions; the analysis of friendly and adversary centers of gravity, to include the legitimacy of the civilian-protection case; the decision points and named areas of interest, to include the decision to act ashore; the risk assessment; and the development, comparison, and recommendation of a course of action with refined termination criteria and transition considerations. I will review the mission-analysis brief and decide the course of action on the timeline I have directed. There is no single right answer in mission analysis; bring me your best analysis and I will decide.

**1. (E) J1 PERSONNEL. Joint manning for the task force and the reception/processing sites is being sourced; the binding personnel demand is the manning of reception and processing at Guantanamo Bay and Puerto Rico and of afloat reception aboard the amphibious force. Interpreters and consular/processing personnel are a shortfall to be filled by request for forces and interagency support.**

**2. (E) J2 INTELLIGENCE. Persistent, wide-area maritime domain awareness across a ninety-nautical-mile strait, against small night-departing craft, is the hardest collection problem. Priority requirements are the provenance and inventory of the Cuban armed drones and coastal craft, the regime's intent (deliberate escalation versus collapse), flow projections and embarkation points, the intentions of Russia and China, and the scale of the atrocities. Collection is being weighted to the northern coast and the interception belt.**

**3. (E) J3 OPERATIONS. The concept is a Navy-led maritime interception operation with a layered no-fly-zone enforcement mission: an interception belt in the international waters of the Straits and the Cuban approaches; defensive counter-air and counter-unmanned-aircraft operations over the coastal approaches and the declared sovereign-airspace belt; blockade enforcement; and force protection of Guantanamo Bay. Three distinct use-of-force regimes must be kept straight: self-defense and defense of civilians under the Standing Rules of Engagement; no-fly-zone enforcement under the supplemental measures; and the minimum force to compel blockade compliance.**

**4. (E) J4 LOGISTICS. Logistics is the pacing function. Establishing and sustaining reception and processing capacity outside the continental United States, at Guantanamo Bay and in Puerto Rico, drives the requirement: berthing and life support for large numbers, medical throughput, potable water, and the strategic and intratheater lift to move people and materiel across the theater. The feasibility of the whole operation turns on whether interception, rescue, and reception throughput can keep pace with the flow. Basing, lift, and sustainment requirements are to be identified and submitted as requests for forces.**

**5. (E) J5 PLANS. Mission analysis is in progress; the base operation is the interception, blockade, and no-fly-zone enforcement, with compartmented branches for operations ashore to protect civilians and for regime change, each gated on a National Command Authority decision. The decision to commit forces ashore is the decisive decision for which indicators and decision points are being developed.**

**6. (E) J6 COMMUNICATIONS. Command and control for a dispersed maritime and air operation, coalition and interagency interoperability, and assured communications with the reception sites are being established. The information environment requires a robust capability to document and rapidly release evidence in support of the legitimacy of the civilian-protection case.**

**7. (E) SURGEON / MEDICAL. Mass at-sea rescue of people from unseaworthy craft in the Florida Current will generate a mass-casualty and public-health demand: hypothermia, near-drowning, dehydration, and the care of vulnerable populations. Afloat medical aboard the amphibious force, and medical capacity at Guantanamo Bay and Puerto Rico, are limiting factors being sized against projected flow.**

**8. (E) STAFF JUDGE ADVOCATE. Legal support covers the use-of-force regimes, the no-fly-zone authority within Cuban airspace, the disposition of interdicted persons (no return to Cuba; non-refoulement as policy; OCONUS processing; the national decision on onward movement), and the maintenance of the operation clear of United States territorial waters to avoid domestic-law entanglement.**

**9. (E) POLITICAL ADVISOR. The political estimate weighs the great-power dimension (the Russian and Chinese veto and information campaign, and the risk of material support to Cuba), the diplomacy of partner consent and third-country and United Nations reception, and the sharp domestic division over the disposition of Cubans, above all the contested question of onward movement to the continental United States, which cuts strongly in both directions.**

**10. (E) INFORMATION OPERATIONS. The information effort must sustain the legitimacy of the civilian-protection case, document and expose the atrocities the regime denies, counter the adversary invasion narrative, and protect essential elements of friendly information, including no-fly-zone gaps and the existence of the compartmented branches.**
