## 1. Purpose

This document compiles the strategic guidance relevant to USEUCOM planning for Operation SILENT FATHOM. It is a synthetic compilation of national- and department-level guidance excerpts, abridged and arranged to inform mission analysis. It is not itself a directive; directive authority for this operation flows through the CJCS and USEUCOM EXORDs (documents 07–08). The strategic posture throughout is competition below the threshold of armed conflict, conducted in a NATO Allied and coalition authorities environment.

## 2. National Security Strategy — High North / Russia Excerpts (Synthetic)

### 2.1 Overarching national interests

US national strategy treats a stable, rules-based High North and the integrity of the transatlantic alliance as enduring interests. Three lines recur in the guidance: deterrence of Russian aggression and coercion along NATO's northern flank; reassurance of Allies and preservation of Alliance cohesion; and the protection of the critical infrastructure — increasingly including undersea cables and energy interconnectors — on which allied economies, governance, and military command-and-control depend. The High North is characterized as a region of growing strategic competition where commercial, scientific, and military activity overlap and where gray-zone, sub-threshold actions are the most likely form of confrontation.

### 2.2 Strategic priorities in the region

- **Deterrence of Russia.** Guidance directs the United States to deny Russia the ability to coerce Allies or to degrade allied infrastructure through deniable, sub-threshold means, while avoiding actions that hand Moscow a pretext for escalation.
- **Allied reassurance and NATO cohesion.** The credibility of the Alliance rests on visible solidarity with exposed Allies. The recent accession of Finland and Sweden expands NATO's northern reach but also lengthens the frontage to be reassured and defended.
- **Protection of critical (incl. undersea) infrastructure.** Subsea cables and interconnectors are named as a category of critical infrastructure whose disruption produces strategic, not merely commercial, effect; their protection is a shared Alliance responsibility.
- **Freedom of navigation and the rule of law at sea.** The United States upholds UNCLOS navigational freedoms and the lawful regime governing submarine cables, even where that regime affords only limited enforcement authority against damage in international waters.

### 2.3 Integration of the instruments of national power (DIME)

- **Diplomatic.** The Department of State leads. The United States works through the North Atlantic Council to support the Article 4 consultations requested by the Kingdom of Norway, coordinates a common Allied position on attribution and response, and engages bilaterally with Norway, the United Kingdom, the Nordic Allies, and Iceland. Embassy Oslo (Chief of Mission, Hon. Patricia Donnelly) is the in-country focal point.
- **Information.** US and Allied messaging affirms Norwegian sovereignty and Alliance unity, frames CUI protection as defensive and lawful, and contests the Russian attribution-denial narrative ("trawler/anchor accident," "NATO false flag") without making public attribution claims ahead of NCA and NATO coordination.
- **Military.** USEUCOM is the supported combatant command. The military instrument supports a State-led, NATO-framed political effort: surveillance and protection of CUI, escort of cable-repair operations, detection and shadowing of suspect vessels, enabling of attribution, PNT-resilient operations, and calibrated deterrent signaling — all below the armed-conflict threshold.
- **Economic.** Treasury (OFAC) prepares contingency measures against entities implicated in seabed sabotage and shadow-fleet operations; the economic instrument is held in reserve pending attribution confidence and Allied alignment.

## 3. National Defense Strategy — DoW-Specific Excerpts (Synthetic)

### 3.1 Integrated deterrence and the competition continuum

The NDS frames Russia as an acute threat and directs integrated deterrence across domains, instruments, theaters, and allies. It explicitly recognizes the competition continuum: adversaries pursue objectives through coercion and deniable action calibrated to stay below the level that would trigger a collective-defense response. Seabed warfare against undersea infrastructure is treated as a characteristic gray-zone method — high in strategic effect, low in attributability.

### 3.2 Force allocation priorities and competing demands

The NDS and Global Force Management process establish that High North contingencies are resourced against standing global demand. USEUCOM holds a finite pool of high-demand, low-density enablers — ocean surveillance, undersea ISR, maritime patrol aircraft, and space/PNT support — for which the seabed-forensics, domain-awareness, and counter-EW tasks of SILENT FATHOM compete directly. Planners are directed to justify any allocated capability against opportunity cost across the theater.

### 3.3 NDS direction relevant to this operation

- Deter further Russian sub-threshold action against allied critical infrastructure without crossing into armed conflict.
- Strengthen Alliance interoperability and reassure Allies through visible, lawful presence.
- Treat the protection of critical undersea infrastructure as a combined civil-military problem requiring interagency (DOJ/FBI, DHS/CISA) and multi-CCMD (USSPACECOM, USCYBERCOM) integration.
- Operate within statutory and treaty limits, recognizing that the binding constraints in this problem are as much legal-jurisdictional as military.

## 4. National Military Strategy — Joint Force Direction (Synthetic)

The NMS directs the Joint Force to compete effectively in the gray zone and to be able to respond to crises on NATO's northern flank without ceding escalation control. For SILENT FATHOM this manifests as: (a) a preference for maritime and undersea options that develop the domain-awareness and attribution picture while signaling resolve; (b) integration with NATO MARCOM's Critical Undersea Infrastructure Coordination Cell and the Norwegian Joint Headquarters; and (c) deliberate escalation management against an adversary employing deniable means — calibrated to deter repetition without manufacturing the confrontation that overt force would risk.

## 5. Regional Strategy Excerpts — US High North Guidance (Synthetic)

### 5.1 The High North framework

US Arctic / High North strategy describes a region transitioning from low tension to active competition, where Russia maintains a concentrated military presence on the Kola Peninsula and seeks to normalize a posture of control over the approaches. It directs the United States to sustain awareness across the maritime and undersea domains, to operate in concert with Arctic Allies, and to uphold the freedom of the seas and the lawful undersea-cable regime, while recognizing the operational friction imposed by the polar environment.

### 5.2 Allied and partner positions (convergent intent, divergent thresholds)

- **Norway (host/lead).** Most directly affected; has requested Allied support and invoked Article 4 consultations. Norwegian sovereignty over Finnmark, Troms, and the Svalbard archipelago is central; the Svalbard Treaty's demilitarization nuance constrains the posture acceptable around the archipelago.
- **United Kingdom.** Forward-leaning on seabed warfare; contributes a multi-role ocean surveillance capability and a frigate; shares the assessment that CUI protection is an Alliance priority.
- **Finland and Sweden (NATO members).** Newly inside the Alliance; raise both the value and the frontage of the northern flank; supportive of a measured, lawful response.
- **Iceland and Denmark.** Exposed along the trans-Atlantic cable routes; support reassurance and CUI protection within national caveats.
- **Divergence on thresholds.** Allies converge on the need to deter and to develop attribution but differ on the evidentiary threshold for public attribution and on the level of response that competition (vice Article 5) permits — a friction the consultative process must manage.

### 5.3 Adversary framing

- **Russia.** Frames any allied investigation or escort as provocation; advances a deniability narrative attributing cable and interconnector damage to accident or to a "NATO false flag"; retains a concentrated Northern Fleet and a deniable deep-sea capability on the Kola Peninsula, but is assessed to prefer continued sub-threshold pressure to overt confrontation.

## 6. Ends, Ways, and Means

- **Ends.** Further sabotage of CUI deterred or defeated; attribution developed to a confidence sufficient to support a coordinated NATO political response; CUI restoration enabled and prioritized by military dependency; Norwegian sovereignty and Allied unity reassured; escalation controlled below the Article 5 / armed-conflict threshold.
- **Ways.** Surveillance and protection of CUI; escort of cable-repair operations; detection, tracking, and shadowing of suspect vessels; ISR and seabed/acoustic forensics in support of attribution; PNT-resilient operations and counter-EW; calibrated deterrent signaling — all integrated with NATO, Norway, and the interagency, and all below armed conflict.
- **Means.** USEUCOM forces with NAVEUR-6F as supported maritime commander, employed forward through Task Force 65 / CTF-67; Allied contributions through SNMG1, Norway, and the United Kingdom; supporting CCMDs (USSPACECOM for PNT/space domain awareness, USCYBERCOM for attribution support); and interagency partners (DoS, DOJ/FBI, DHS/CISA).

## 7. Strategic Communication Themes

1. **Allied solidarity and Norwegian sovereignty are unconditional.** US action supports Norway as host/lead and the Alliance through the consultative process.
2. **CUI protection is lawful and defensive.** US and Allied presence upholds freedom of navigation and the protection of civilian and shared infrastructure; it is not escalation.
3. **Attribution will be deliberate and evidence-based.** The United States will not make public attribution claims ahead of NCA and NATO coordination, and will contest, not amplify, adversary deniability narratives.
4. **Escalation is the saboteur's choice, not the Alliance's.** Posture is measured and proportionate; further action against allied infrastructure will be attributed and answered, but the Alliance is not seeking conflict.
5. **Resilience is a shared enterprise.** Protection of undersea cables and interconnectors is a combined civil-military, allied, and interagency responsibility.

## 1. Executive Summary

Between 05 and 13 JAN 26, three subsea telecom/data cables and one HVDC power interconnector in the Norwegian and Barents Seas suffered near-simultaneous damage, coincident with sustained GPS/PNT jamming and AIS spoofing degrading navigation across Finnmark and the Svalbard approaches. A Russian-flagged GUGI-analog hydrographic/survey vessel (GS Pomor) and two shadow-fleet merchants (MV Arktika Star, MV Kola Trader) operated in or near the cable corridors during the window with extended, unexplained AIS gaps.

J2 assesses **with high confidence** that Russia possesses the deniable seabed and deep-submergence capability to conduct the observed damage, resident in the Northern Fleet's Main Directorate for Deep-Sea Research (GUGI-analog) on the Kola Peninsula. J2 assesses **with low confidence** the **intent** behind the incidents and holds **low confidence on conclusive attribution** — specifically whether the damage was directed by the Russian state, executed by a deniable proxy or shadow-fleet vessel acting at state behest, or in any individual case the product of an accident (anchor drag, trawl gear) of the kind that genuinely occurs in these waters. This capability-known / intent-and-attribution-uncertain split is the central analytic problem and motivates the collection priorities at §8.

The window closed with GS Ladoga, a second GUGI-analog survey vessel, departing the Kola area on a track toward the Bear Island Gap corridor — an indicator carried forward as a decision-point trigger (`13_CCIRs.md`, `15_Event_Template_NAI_Matrix.xlsx`). Norway has requested Article 4 consultations; STARTEX for US mission analysis is 140600Z JAN 26.

## 2. The Nine-Day Escalation Arc (05–13 JAN 26)

- **05–06 JAN.** Onset of intermittent GPS/PNT degradation and AIS anomalies over Finnmark and the Svalbard approaches, initially assessed as routine Kola-based EW activity within the observed seasonal pattern. GS Pomor departs the Kola area; AIS shows a normal coastal track before the first of several gaps.
- **07 JAN — Cut Site A.** SVL-2, one of the twin Svalbard Link fibers (Longyearbyen–Harstad), is **severed** near the Svalbard approaches (Cut Site A, vicinity 76.5N 014.0E). Loss of SVL-2 degrades, but does not sever, connectivity from Longyearbyen and the Svalbard Satellite Station; SVL-1 carries the residual load. GS Pomor's AIS shows an extended gap bracketing the fault timeframe in the broader Svalbard approaches.
- **09 JAN — Cut Site B.** The trans-Atlantic data cable Boreal Connect (Norway–Iceland–North America) registers a **partial fault** in the Norwegian Sea (Cut Site B, vicinity 67.5N 002.0E). The shadow-fleet bulk carrier MV Kola Trader is the nearest tracked commercial vessel to the corridor in the preceding days, with an AIS gap of several hours over the relevant segment.
- **11 JAN — Nordkapp Data Link.** The coastal fiber along Finnmark (NDL) registers **intermittent faults**, coincident with a renewed and stronger episode of GPS/PNT jamming along the Finnmark coast. The simultaneity complicates fault localization and is itself assessed as a possible masking effect.
- **12 JAN — Arctic Bridge HVDC.** A segment of the Arctic Bridge HVDC power interconnector in the Norwegian Sea experiences a **protective trip**; operator preliminary assessment is consistent with suspected anchor-drag damage. The crude tanker MV Arktika Star transited the general area with an AIS gap in the preceding 24 hours; an anchor-drag mechanism is consistent with both a deliberate act and a genuine accident, and the two cannot yet be distinguished from the available data.
- **13 JAN.** GS Pomor recovered to the Kola area. GS Ladoga (second GUGI-analog survey vessel) departs on a northwesterly track assessed as consistent with transit toward the Bear Island Gap corridor. Jamming continues at reduced intensity. Norway requests Article 4 consultations.

## 3. Adversary Strategic Posture

### 3.1 Russian Northern Fleet and the GUGI-analog directorate

The Northern Fleet (HQ Severomorsk; Commander ADM Viktor Sorokin, RuN) maintains the concentration of surface, subsurface, and EW capability on the Kola Peninsula from which all observed activity is most plausibly staged. The Main Directorate for Deep-Sea Research (GUGI-analog) provides a deniable seabed and deep-submergence survey capability whose hydrographic vessels (GS Pomor, GS Ladoga) and special-mission host submarine (B-90 Vologda) are assessed capable of locating, surveying, and interfering with seabed infrastructure at the depths involved. See `03_Adversary_OOB.md` for unit detail.

**Capabilities — high confidence.** Russia has the platforms, the seabed knowledge, the EW assets, and the demonstrated doctrine to conduct deniable CUI interference across the Norwegian and Barents Seas.

**Intent — low confidence.** Whether the campaign is intended to (a) coerce Norway and the Alliance through demonstrated reach over critical infrastructure, (b) condition the operating environment for future leverage, or (c) probe Allied response thresholds, is unresolved. The deliberate use of deniable means is itself consistent with an intent to act below the level that would compel a collective-defense response.

### 3.2 Shadow-fleet and deniable maritime activity

MV Arktika Star (crude tanker) and MV Kola Trader (bulk carrier), both flags of convenience, exhibit the dark-activity profile (extended AIS gaps near the corridors, loitering inconsistent with their declared voyages) associated with Russia's shadow fleet. Their proximity to Cut Sites and to the HVDC corridor during the relevant windows is suggestive but, given the genuine density of commercial and fishing traffic and the prevalence of accidental anchor/trawl cable faults, is not by itself conclusive of a deliberate act or of state direction.

### 3.3 EW / PNT jamming from the Kola Peninsula

Northern Fleet EW units on the Kola Peninsula (Tobol- / Murmansk-BN-analog systems) are the assessed source of the GPS/PNT jamming and AIS-spoofing footprint observed over Finnmark and the Svalbard approaches. The jamming pattern correlated in time with the 11 JAN cable faults; J2 cannot yet determine whether the correlation reflects deliberate masking of a seabed act or the independent continuation of a standing EW posture. The footprint affects friendly as well as civil receivers, and degraded PNT is itself a constraint on the maritime and air operations that would conduct protection and forensics.

### 3.4 Subsurface activity and pattern of life

The least-observed element of the picture is the subsurface. The special-mission host submarine B-90 Vologda is assessed capable of deploying and recovering the deep-submergence systems that would localize and interfere with seabed cables; its location during the window is not established from available reporting. Northern Fleet pattern-of-life since 2024 shows a steady normalization of GUGI-analog survey activity along the cable corridors and recurring, seasonally-patterned EW episodes — a baseline against which the January incidents stand out chiefly for their clustering and near-simultaneity rather than for any single anomalous act.

## 4. Regional State and Actor Posture

- **Norway (host/lead).** Owns the affected infrastructure and the surrounding sovereignty equities (Finnmark, Troms, Svalbard); has invoked Article 4 and requested Allied support; the Norwegian Joint Headquarters (FOH, Reitan/Bodø) and E-tjenesten (Vardø ground station) are the principal liaison nodes.
- **United Kingdom.** Contributing seabed-warfare and surface capability; aligned on the CUI-protection priority.
- **Finland, Sweden, Iceland, Denmark.** Supportive Allies with exposure along the northern and trans-Atlantic routes; converge on reassurance and attribution but differ on response thresholds.
- **Commercial stakeholders.** Subsea-cable operators, the HVDC interconnector operator, and a contracted cable-repair ship shape the restoration timeline and the operating picture; they are not adversaries but their schedules and fault data are central to both protection and forensics.

## 5. Civil and Critical-Infrastructure Overlay

The affected cables and interconnector carry far more than civilian internet traffic. SVL and Boreal Connect transport allied/civil space-ground links from the Svalbard Satellite Station and a portion of allied communications and financial-clearing data; the redundancy lost when one of a redundant pair is severed raises the consequence of any further cut. The full dependency picture is developed in `05_Country_Fact_Sheet.md`, `11_Annexes.md`, and the OSINT bundle (`16_OSINT_Bundle.md`). The polar-night environment — near-continuous darkness, sea state 4–6, icing, an intermittent ice edge in the northern Barents — degrades surface, air, and repair operations and narrows the windows in which protection and forensics can occur.

## 6. Information Environment Snapshot

Russian state media and a coordinated inauthentic social-media network are advancing an attribution-denial narrative: that the damage is the result of a "trawler/anchor accident," or alternatively that any allied investigation is a "NATO false flag" pretext for militarizing the High North. The narrative exploits the genuine ambiguity of cable faults in heavily-trafficked waters. Attribution of the influence activity itself is moderate confidence; its effect is to raise the evidentiary bar the Alliance must clear before any public attribution.

## 7. Intelligence Gaps and Confidence Assessment

J2 holds **high confidence on Russian capability** and **low confidence on intent and on conclusive attribution** — the central capabilities-vs-intent split. Principal gaps:

1. **Attribution — direction vs. deniable proxy vs. accident.** No single reporting stream resolves whether the damage was state-directed, executed by a deniable proxy/shadow-fleet vessel, or in any case accidental. Resolution requires fusion of vessel tracks, AIS-gap correlation, EW geolocation, and seabed/acoustic forensics. (Drives PIR-2.)
2. **Next-target indication.** Whether GS Ladoga is positioning to target the Bear Island Gap corridor, and whether further sabotage is likely within the next 14 days and against which corridor. (Drives PIR-1 / PIR-3 and the decision-point trigger.)
3. **Subsurface picture.** The location and activity of the special-mission host submarine B-90 Vologda and any deployed seabed payloads — the least-observed and highest-value element of the adversary picture.
4. **Jamming characterization.** The full extent, pattern, and intent of the GPS/PNT jamming, and its effect on friendly maritime and air operations. (Drives PIR-4 and the PNT/SPACECOM seam.)
5. **CUI dependency and restoration.** The precise military-communications dependency on each affected cable and the realistic restoration timeline given the cable-repair ship's availability and the weather.

## 8. Implications for the Operational Approach

- The **capability-known / intent-and-attribution-uncertain** problem means the operation must develop attribution as an objective in its own right, not assume it; protection and forensics are mutually reinforcing collection.
- The **GS Ladoga transit** creates a time-sensitive indication-and-warning problem against the Bear Island Gap corridor that must be wired to a decision and a CCIR.
- The **deniability of the means** — shadow-fleet vessels, accident-plausible mechanisms, EW masking — is the adversary's principal advantage and the analyst's principal obstacle; nothing in this summary, taken alone, meets the threshold for a confirmed, attributable act.
- The **jamming** ties the intelligence problem to friendly operability and to a multi-CCMD seam (SPACECOM / the electromagnetic spectrum) that must be deconflicted with friendly maritime and air operations.
- **Escalation management** is continuous: the same deniability that frustrates attribution also caps the response that competition permits, and the collection-and-signaling approach must deter repetition without crossing the Article 5 / armed-conflict threshold.

## 7. Adversary Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (employed set)

- **Seabed survey and cable interaction.** Hydrographic/research-cover survey of cable corridors; deep-submergence localization; physical interaction (cut, abrasion, tap) at depth, optionally via a submarine-hosted system, leaving a signature that mimics anchor/trawl damage.
- **Anchor-drag mimicry.** Use of merchant anchor-drag — or its appearance — to produce faults that are individually plausible as accidents, defeating single-event attribution.
- **AIS manipulation.** Coordinated AIS gaps (transponder-off) and AIS spoofing (false position/identity) to obscure presence near corridors and degrade the friendly track picture.
- **EW masking and degradation.** GPS/PNT jamming timed to, or coincident with, seabed events — degrading friendly localization/forensics while remaining within a standing, seasonally-plausible EW pattern.
- **Deniable-layer compartmentation.** Routing the act through GUGI-analog and flag-of-convenience merchants so that no overt Fleet tasking links the state to the effect.

## 8. Adversary Center of Gravity (initial)

The adversary's operational center of gravity in this campaign is **deniability itself** — the layered ambiguity (research cover, accident-plausible mechanisms, flag-of-convenience merchants, EW masking, information-domain denial) that lets Russia demonstrate reach over Allied critical undersea infrastructure while denying the Alliance a confirmed, attributable act on which to base a collective response. Critical capabilities flow from the GUGI-analog seabed assets and the Kola EW grouping; critical requirements are research/commercial cover and the genuine baseline rate of accidental faults; the critical vulnerability is the forensic and multi-source fusion picture that, if developed, collapses the deniability and converts a plausible accident into an attributable act. This vulnerability is what the friendly attribution effort is designed to exploit.

## 9. Capabilities vs. Intent (confidence framing)

- **Capability — HIGH confidence.** Russia possesses the platforms (GUGI-analog survey vessels, B-90 *Vologda*), the seabed knowledge, the Kola EW assets, and the demonstrated doctrine to conduct deniable CUI interference and PNT degradation across the Norwegian and Barents Seas. This is not in analytic doubt.
- **Intent — LOW confidence.** Whether the campaign is intended to coerce Norway and the Alliance, condition the environment for future leverage, or probe response thresholds, is unresolved. The deliberate use of deniable means is itself consistent with an intent to stay below the level that compels collective defense.
- **Attribution — LOW confidence.** No single reporting stream resolves whether the damage was state-directed, executed by a deniable proxy/shadow-fleet vessel at state behest, or in any individual case genuinely accidental. The capability-known / intent-and-attribution-uncertain split is the central analytic problem and the driver of the collection priorities.

## 2. Svalbard Archipelago and the Svalbard Treaty Regime

### 2.1 Sovereignty and the Treaty
- **Svalbard** (administrative center **Longyearbyen**, 78.22N, 15.65E) is a high-Arctic archipelago under full **Norwegian sovereignty** by the **Svalbard Treaty (Spitsbergen Treaty, 1920)**.
- The Treaty grants signatory nationals equal rights of access and economic activity (fishing, mining, research) and obliges Norway not to establish or permit the use of the archipelago for **"warlike purposes."** The precise reach of this **demilitarization clause** — what military or military-adjacent activity it permits in and around Svalbard — is contested between Norway and Russia and is a genuine legal seam constraining force posture in the area.
- Russia maintains a long-standing presence at **Barentsburg** and **Pyramiden** under the Treaty's equal-access provisions, giving it a lawful foothold in the archipelago.

### 2.2 Svalbard Satellite Station (SvalSat) and connectivity
- **SvalSat**, on the Platåberget plateau above Longyearbyen, is one of the world's most important polar ground stations: its 78°N latitude lets it command nearly every pass of polar-orbiting satellites, making it a critical node for **civil, scientific, and allied space-ground links** (Earth-observation, weather, and satellite command-and-control downlink/uplink).
- SvalSat's value depends on the data pipe back to the mainland. That pipe is the **Svalbard Link (SVL)** twin-fiber pair (§3). Loss of redundancy on the SVL therefore degrades not only Svalbard's civilian connectivity but the throughput of the satellite ground-station traffic it carries.

## 3. Critical Undersea Infrastructure (Norwegian and Barents Seas)

Four seabed systems define the critical-infrastructure picture. As is typical of modern subsea cables, the telecom systems are shared-carriage: alongside civilian internet and data they transport managed capacity for institutional customers.

- **Svalbard Link — SVL-1 / SVL-2.** A **twin-pair** fiber system connecting **Longyearbyen** to the mainland landing at **Harstad** (68.80N, 16.54E). SVL carries Svalbard and SvalSat connectivity — civil internet and the satellite ground-station data noted at §2.2 — and, as managed transport on the same pair, a portion of **allied military-communications** traffic and **financial-clearing / settlement** data routed northbound. The pair is engineered for mutual redundancy: each fiber can carry the residual load if the other faults. **SVL-2 was severed 07 JAN** (Cut Site A, vicinity 76.5N 014.0E, in the Svalbard approaches); **SVL-1 now carries degraded but not fully severed** Svalbard/SvalSat connectivity, the redundancy gone.
- **Boreal Connect.** A **trans-Atlantic** data cable transiting the **Norwegian Sea** on the **Norway–Iceland–North America** route. Like SVL, Boreal Connect carries civilian internet and data plus managed allied military-communications transport and a share of **financial-clearing** traffic between European and North American nodes. It registered a **partial fault 09 JAN** (Cut Site B, vicinity 67.5N 002.0E).
- **Nordkapp Data Link (NDL).** Coastal fiber running along the **Finnmark** coast, serving northern Norwegian communities and connecting onward to the broader national network. It registered **intermittent faults 11 JAN**, coincident with a stronger episode of GPS/PNT jamming along the coast.
- **Arctic Bridge HVDC.** A high-voltage direct-current **power interconnector** segment in the **Norwegian Sea**, tying northern generation and grids into the wider European market (§1.5). It experienced a **protective trip 12 JAN**, with the operator's preliminary assessment consistent with **suspected anchor-drag damage**.

The systems are mutually independent in routing but concentrate in the same corridors and chokepoints (see `06_Critical_Geography.md`). The loss of redundancy across the telecom pair, and the breadth of traffic each cable carries, mean the consequence of any further cut rises sharply.

## 2. Cable Corridors and Landing Sites

- **Boreal Connect:** Trans-Atlantic data cable routed across the **Norwegian Sea** on the Norway–Iceland–North America line, transiting deep basins toward the GIUK–Bear Gap. Its partial fault, **Cut Site B (~67.5N 002.0E)**, lies in the open Norwegian Sea well seaward of the coast.
- **Nordkapp Data Link (NDL):** A **coastal** fiber following the Finnmark littoral past the North Cape (Nordkapp), largely within or near the Norwegian territorial sea — the one affected system that runs predominantly inside sovereign waters.
- **Arctic Bridge HVDC:** A power-interconnector **segment in the Norwegian Sea**, crossing the same general corridor as the data cables and exposed to the same chokepoint and seabed conditions.

## 5. Maritime-Jurisdiction Geography

The legal geography over the corridors is uneven, and enforcement authority tracks it directly:

- **Norwegian territorial sea (to 12 nm):** Full Norwegian sovereignty. The **NDL** coastal route lies largely here; this is the only corridor where the host nation holds clear enforcement authority over activity above and on the seabed.
- **Norwegian Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ, 12–200 nm) and fisheries-protection zones (incl. around Svalbard):** Norway holds sovereign rights over resources and jurisdiction over installations, but enforcement against a foreign-flagged vessel damaging a submarine cable is constrained; under the UNCLOS submarine-cable regime, jurisdiction over such acts rests substantially with the **flag state**, not the coastal state.
- **High seas (beyond 200 nm and over the deep Norwegian Sea routes):** **Cut Site B** (Boreal Connect) and much of the SVL and Boreal Connect routing lie in or near international waters, where coastal-state enforcement authority is at its weakest and flag-state jurisdiction governs.

The practical consequence is that the two highest-consequence cuts (Cut Site A in the Svalbard approaches; Cut Site B in the open Norwegian Sea) sit where enforcement authority against a foreign-flagged actor is most limited — the binding constraint over much of the corridor is legal/jurisdictional rather than physical reach. Proximity to **Russian territorial waters off the Kola Peninsula** further compresses the maneuver space in the eastern Barents, where operating close to the cut geometry also means operating close to the Russian fleet bastion. The OPLAW treatment of cable jurisdiction, the EEZ regime, and the Svalbard demilitarization nuance is in `11_Annexes.md` Annex A.

## 6. Climatology — Polar Night (mid-January)

- **Light:** Above the Arctic Circle the area is in **polar night** — near-continuous darkness through mid-January, with only brief, dim civil twilight near local noon in the southern Norwegian Sea and none at the Svalbard latitudes. Operations are effectively round-the-clock night.
- **Sea state and weather:** Frequent North Atlantic / Barents storm tracks drive **sea state 4–6** across the open corridors, with higher states in passing systems; usable **weather windows are narrow** and short-fused.
- **Icing and ice edge:** **Superstructure and rotor icing** is a persistent hazard for surface ships and helicopters; the **northern Barents ice edge** advances and shifts through the season, constraining access to the Svalbard approaches and complicating any operation north and east of Bear Island.
- **Temperature/visibility:** Sub-zero air and sea-spray, reduced visibility in snow showers and sea smoke.

## 7. Operational Effects of the Environment

- **Surface operations:** SS 4–6 and icing reduce small-boat, davit, and flight-deck availability and slow the cable-repair ship, lengthening restoration timelines and the windows in which escort and protection must be sustained.
- **Air/ISR operations:** Icing, low ceilings, and darkness constrain MPA and helicopter sorties and degrade some EO/IR collection; high-altitude ISR is less weather-limited but must contend with the PNT environment.
- **Undersea/seabed forensics:** Surface sea state limits the launch, tow, and recovery of seabed-survey and acoustic systems (T-AGOS / ROV operations), narrowing the forensics windows at Cut Sites A and B.
- **PNT-degraded navigation:** The polar-night, high-latitude environment already stresses navigation (poor celestial reference, high-latitude compass error); layered onto the **GPS/PNT jamming and AIS spoofing** over Finnmark and the Svalbard approaches, this makes precise station-keeping over a cut site, corridor patrol, and de-confliction of friendly surface and air tracks materially harder, and elevates the value of alternate-navigation and counter-EW measures. ## Annex A — OPLAW / Legal

**Legal basis for US role.**
- No new self-defense trigger is invoked. US entry and role rest on: (a) the Kingdom of Norway's request for Allied support; (b) NATO Washington Treaty **Article 4** consultations requested by Norway — **Article 5 is deliberately not invoked** (sub-threshold); (c) the US–Norway Supplementary Defense Cooperation Agreement (SDCA 2021) for access and basing; and (d) US Title 10 support-to-ally / forward maritime presence.

**Submarine-cable regime (UNCLOS).**
- UNCLOS **Art. 113** requires each state to make it a punishable offense for a vessel flying its flag, or a person subject to its jurisdiction, to break or injure a submarine cable willfully or by culpable negligence.
- **Art. 114** addresses liability of cable owners to one another; **Art. 115** provides for indemnity of vessels that sacrifice gear to avoid injuring a cable. The regime is principally one of flag-state penalty and inter-owner liability, not third-state enforcement.
- Enforcement therefore runs principally through **flag-state jurisdiction**. There is **no general coastal-state or third-state authority to board, search, seize, or divert a foreign-flagged vessel on the high seas or in the EEZ for cable damage** absent flag-state consent, a treaty basis, or another recognized exception.
- **Consequence.** For the faults at Cut Site A (Svalbard approaches) and Cut Site B (Norwegian Sea high seas), and for activity in the Norwegian EEZ, **US/Allied enforcement authority over cable damage in international waters is limited**. The binding constraint on interdiction is legal/jurisdictional, not military, and it persists even where the responsible vessel is identified.

**Svalbard Treaty regime.**
- The 1920 Svalbard Treaty confirms Norwegian sovereignty over the archipelago while granting treaty-party nationals equal economic access. Art. 9 constrains the use of the archipelago for "warlike purposes" and is the source of the demilitarization nuance that bounds military posture in and around Svalbard.
- Operations near the Svalbard approaches and the Longyearbyen landing — including any escort of repair operations on SVL — must account for this regime and for Russia's standing readiness to characterize Allied activity there as militarization. It is a deliberate legal seam, not a settled permission; coordinate posture with Norway as the sovereign and with the legal-determination track below.

**ROE / RUF summary (SILENT FATHOM ROE Supplement to CJCSI 3121.01B).**
- Inherent right of **unit and individual self-defense** always applies, including defense of accompanied repair and partner units.
- Posture toward Russian state vessels is **defensive**; **no kinetic action against Russian state vessels** and **no entry into Russian territorial waters**.
- **No boarding, seizure, or diversion of vessels in international waters absent a positive legal determination and granted authority.** Shadowing, querying on bridge-to-bridge, overt presence, and persistent ISR are permitted within standing guidance.
- All operations remain **below the threshold of armed conflict.** **Do not invoke or imply NATO Article 5.**
- Coordinate with Norway as host/lead and through the NAC consultative process; comply with UNCLOS and the Svalbard Treaty regime.
- **No public attribution release without NCA and NATO coordination.**

**Open decision item.** The **interdiction-authority determination** — whether, and on what basis, friendly forces may interdict GS Ladoga or a shadow-fleet vessel approaching the Bear Island Gap corridor in international waters / the Norwegian EEZ — is an **unresolved legal question** referred for determination. ## Annex D — Climatology

## 1. Operational Design

### 1.1 The Problem
Over nine days an adversary has degraded four pieces of critical undersea infrastructure (CUI) in the Norwegian and Barents Seas and laid a coincident GPS/PNT-jamming and AIS-spoofing footprint across Finnmark and the Svalbard approaches. The acts are deliberate, plausibly Russian state-directed, and deliberately deniable — executed by survey vessels and shadow-fleet merchants operating in international waters and a contested legal regime, paced and packaged to stay below the armed-conflict threshold. **The central tension is this: we must deter and, where we can, defend against further sabotage and reassure Norway and the Allies, without handing Moscow the escalation it is courting and without ambiguously implying Article 5.** Too forceful a posture invites a confrontation we are not authorized to seek; too little invites repetition. Our task is to find the operating space in between — to deny, attribute, and signal below the threshold.

### 1.2 Operational Approach
Establish a subsurface-inclusive maritime domain awareness picture over the cable corridors; protect the highest-dependency CUI and the cable-repair effort by presence and escort; develop attribution to a confidence sufficient to enable a coordinated Allied political response; and signal resolve through calibrated, reversible presence rather than force. We lead with Norway as host and lead, operate inside the NAC consultative framework, and set conditions to transition CUI security back to Allied and commercial stewardship. We compete in the information environment to deny the adversary an uncontested attribution-denial narrative.

### 1.3 Lines of Effort
- **LOE 1 — Protect CUI.** *Main effort initially.* Surveillance and protective presence over the cable/interconnector corridors; escort and protect cable-repair operations; prioritize by **military-communications dependency**, not by cable length or commercial value.
- **LOE 2 — Attribute.** Fuse vessel tracks, AIS gaps, EW geolocation, and seabed/acoustic forensics — in support of CYBERCOM and FBI — to close the gap between observed activity and state direction.
- **LOE 3 — Deter & Signal.** Calibrated, reversible presence and declaratory measures; shadowing of adversary survey vessels; deterrent signaling options that support, not pre-empt, Article 4 consultations.
- **LOE 4 — Reassure Allies.** Visible commitment to Norwegian sovereignty and Allied unity; integrate UK, SNMG1, and Nordic partners; preserve cohesion across divergent Allied attribution and response thresholds.

### 1.4 Decisive Points
- A subsurface-inclusive MDA picture sufficient to cue protection and forensics in the corridors.
- A legal determination on interdiction authority in international waters and the Norwegian EEZ (gates what we may do to adversary vessels).
- Attribution developed to a confidence that can underwrite a coordinated NATO political response.
- Cable-repair of the highest-dependency segment escorted and completed.
- PNT-resilient maritime operations sustained in the jamming footprint.

### 1.5 Termination / End-State Criteria (initial — deliberately incomplete)
Planners will treat these as a starting point requiring staff development and higher-headquarters clarification:
- Further sabotage **deterred or defeated** — the threshold that distinguishes "deterred" from merely "no incident this week" is **undefined and must be staffed against a defined observation window.**
- Attribution developed to a confidence "sufficient to support a coordinated NATO political response" — **the confidence standard, and who certifies it, is not specified and must be coordinated with the NAC track and the interagency.**
- CUI restoration **enabled and prioritized by military dependency** — restoration is a commercial/Allied function; **the conditions under which the military protection effort transitions off are not yet defined.**
- Allied unity and Norwegian sovereignty reassured **— the measure of "reassured" is left to higher direction.**
- Escalation controlled and kept below the Article 5 / armed-conflict threshold.

The relationship between these military criteria and the **political** end-state of the Article 4 process is intentionally left to higher direction. Identify the gaps; do not assume them closed.

Lead with Norway; operate inside the NAC framework and UNCLOS; obtain the legal determination before we contemplate any action against vessels.
