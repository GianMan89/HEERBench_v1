## 2. National Security Strategy — Indo-Pacific Excerpts (Synthetic, August 2026)

### 2.1 Overarching National Interests

The United States seeks an Indo-Pacific that is **free and open, connected, prosperous, secure, and resilient**. The region accounts for more than half of global GDP, hosts five US treaty allies, and includes the world’s busiest sea lanes. Stability in the Indo-Pacific is a vital national interest because it is the principal arena in which the long-term strategic competition with the People’s Republic of China is being conducted.

The United States holds five mutual defense treaties in the Indo-Pacific: with **the Republic of the Philippines** (1951), **the Commonwealth of Australia** (ANZUS, 1951), **the Republic of Korea** (1953), **Japan** (1960, revised), and **the Kingdom of Thailand** (1954, Manila Pact). The credibility of these treaties is the foundation of American influence in the region and a public good for the rule-based international order.

### 2.2 Strategic Priorities — South China Sea and Adjacent

In the South China Sea, the United States supports the **2016 Permanent Court of Arbitration Award** in *The Republic of the Philippines v. The People’s Republic of China*, which concluded that the PRC’s “nine-dash line” claim has no basis in international law under UNCLOS. The United States rejects unilateral PRC efforts to enforce maritime claims through coercion, including through the use of the People’s Liberation Army Navy (PLAN), the China Coast Guard (CCG), and the People’s Armed Forces Maritime Militia (PAFMM).

The May 2026 PRC fait accompli at Sandy Cay (Operation HIDDEN TIDE) constituted a significant departure from the gray-zone equilibrium that had prevailed since 2012. The subsequent graduated escalation through June–July 2026 “Maritime Police Action” incidents culminated in the August 2026 PRC kinetic action at Pag-asa Island. The deliberate armed attack on an island territory under Philippine jurisdiction in the Pacific, and on the armed forces and a public vessel of the Philippines, satisfies the definitional conditions of Article V of the Mutual Defense Treaty — thereby triggering Article IV obligations to act to meet the common dangers in accordance with constitutional processes.

### 2.3 Strategic Priorities — Northeast Asia (Korea / Japan)

The Korean Peninsula remains a standing strategic concern. As of August 2026, the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea continues to maintain a posture of strategic ambiguity following the 2025 missile test campaign. The Republic of Korea-United States Combined Forces Command (CFC) is at full readiness; ROK forces are not available for redeployment outside the Peninsula at this time.

Japan, under the 2022 National Security Strategy and the 2024 revisions to the US-Japan defense cooperation framework, has indicated willingness to provide direct support to PACIFIC HARRIER through reciprocal access (RAA), maritime patrol, intelligence sharing, and limited force contributions. Japanese force contributions are constrained by domestic political and constitutional factors but are materially significant for ISR and logistics.

### 2.4 Strategic Priorities — Southeast Asia and ASEAN

The United States continues to support an ASEAN-centered regional architecture but recognizes that ASEAN consensus mechanisms have been repeatedly fractured by PRC influence on individual member states. Vietnam, Indonesia, the Philippines, and Singapore are the primary US partners on SCS and SLOC issues; Cambodia and Laos have aligned more closely with PRC positions.

In the present crisis, **Vietnam, Indonesia, and Malaysia have indicated willingness to provide quiet operational support** (intelligence sharing, port access for non-combatants, overflight clearance) without public alignment. The Philippines is the host nation and the principal claimant whose territory is to be restored.

### 2.5 Integration of Instruments of National Power (DIME)

- **Diplomatic.** The Department of State has activated the Article III consultative mechanism with the Philippines and is conducting direct diplomatic engagement with Beijing. UN Security Council action is blocked by the PRC veto; UNGA action is being pursued for narrative purposes.

- **Information.** Strategic communications are coordinated with the Philippines’ AFP/PCG Transparency Initiative. The PRC narrative (“lawful enforcement of sovereign waters”) is being directly contested.

- **Military.** USINDOPACOM has been directed to plan and prepare to conduct multinational operations to restore Philippine territorial integrity. The President has authorized force-flow to position for execution.

- **Economic.** Targeted sanctions on PRC entities involved in the Pag-asa action are being prepared for Treasury / OFAC action. Broader trade actions are not under consideration at this time due to global economic implications.

The 2022 NDS designates the **People’s Republic of China** as the **pacing challenge** for the Department of Defense. The PRC is the only competitor with both the intent to reshape the international order and, increasingly, the economic, diplomatic, military, and technological power to do so. The **Russian Federation** is designated an **acute threat**; the **Democratic People’s Republic of Korea** and the **Islamic Republic of Iran** are designated **persistent threats**. 1.  **First priority of effort:** Defending the homeland (USNORTHCOM, USSPACECOM, USCYBERCOM).

2.  **Second priority:** Deterring strategic attacks against the United States, allies, and partners (USSTRATCOM, regional CCMDs).

3.  **Third priority — and the principal active demand on conventional forces:** Deterring aggression while being prepared to prevail in conflict in the Indo-Pacific, with the Korean Peninsula and the Taiwan Strait as the two principal contingency drivers.

### 5.2 Bilateral Defense Treaties Relevant to PACIFIC HARRIER

**1951 US-Philippines Mutual Defense Treaty (MDT).** Principal articles:

- **Article III.** Parties shall consult together whenever, in the opinion of either of them, the territorial integrity, political independence, or security of either of the Parties is threatened by external armed attack in the Pacific.

- **Article IV.** Each Party recognizes that an armed attack in the Pacific Area on either of the Parties would be dangerous to its own peace and safety, and declares that it would act to meet the common dangers in accordance with its constitutional processes.

- **Article V.** For the purpose of Article IV, an armed attack on either of the Parties is deemed to include an armed attack on the metropolitan territory of either of the Parties, or on the island territories under its jurisdiction in the Pacific, or on its armed forces, public vessels or aircraft in the Pacific.

**Application to the present case.** Pag-asa Island is an island territory under Philippine jurisdiction in the Pacific. The 22 August attack also targeted AFP / PCG personnel (armed forces of the Philippines) and BRP *Teresa Magbanua* (a public vessel of the Philippines). The conditions of Article V are therefore met for three independent reasons. Article IV obligations are accordingly triggered, requiring the United States to “act to meet the common dangers in accordance with its constitutional processes.” The US President’s 23 August 2026 determination so finds; the Philippine President’s joint communiqué of the same date confirms the Philippine reading.

## 1. Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF)

On 22 August 2026, the People’s Republic of China conducted a deliberate amphibious assault on Pag-asa Island (Thitu), Spratly Islands, killing approximately 14 Armed Forces of the Philippines and Philippine Coast Guard personnel, capturing approximately 25 more, and wounding 18; an additional 11 Philippine Coast Guard personnel were killed when an anti-ship missile struck BRP *Teresa Magbanua* at the Pag-asa anchorage. The action followed a graduated 90-day pattern of escalation from the May 2026 Sandy Cay fait accompli (Operation HIDDEN TIDE), through a sequence of three “Maritime Police Action” incidents and a public PRC declaration extending the Marine Scientific Research Protection Zone (MSRPZ) to the Pag-asa cluster. JIOC PACOM assesses with HIGH confidence that the 22 August operation is the opening phase of a campaign to consolidate effective control of the Spratly central group; with MEDIUM confidence that the campaign was authorized for execution within a constrained envelope (specifically: limited to the Spratlys; no kinetic action against US forces unless provoked; off-ramp preserved if the conditions of MDT Article V are met); and with HIGH confidence that the PRC has miscalculated the US response.

The Philippine government has invoked Article III consultations under the 1951 Mutual Defense Treaty; the President of the United States, in a 23 August 2026 determination, has confirmed that the conditions of Article V (definition of “armed attack” against an “island territory under \[Philippine\] jurisdiction in the Pacific” and against “armed forces, public vessels” of the Philippines) are satisfied, thereby triggering Article IV obligations to act to meet the common dangers. The Secretary of Defense has directed USINDOPACOM to plan and prepare to conduct multinational operations to restore Philippine territorial integrity in the Pag-asa cluster.

This summary provides the J2 narrative and background that should inform JFC mission analysis.

## 2. Path to the Crisis (March 2025 → August 2026)

### 2.1 Long-Term Trajectory

The PRC has pursued effective administrative control over the South China Sea for more than a decade. Phase 1 (2014–2018) was the construction and militarization of the “Big Three” Spratly outposts (Mischief, Subi, Fiery Cross). Phase 2 (2018–2025) was the layered gray-zone consolidation through CCG presence, PAFMM saturation, and information / legal warfare. Phase 3 — beginning with the May 2026 HIDDEN TIDE event — appears to be the conversion of consolidation into physical presence at additional features.

The 2024–2025 period marked a significant tightening of US-Philippines alliance posture: EDCA expansion to nine sites including the four-site northern Luzon footprint that materially shortens US response time to the Spratlys; the 2024 PH-Japan Reciprocal Access Agreement; and the Marcos administration’s “Transparency Initiative” that institutionalized public counter-narrative response to PRC harassment.

The May 2026 HIDDEN TIDE event was intended by the PRC as a low-cost demonstration of administrative authority. The combination of US tactical posture (Singapore-vicinity DDG, eastern Taiwan CSG), Philippine restraint (PCG-only response under “document not engage” framing), and ASEAN consensus paralysis allowed PRC to consolidate Sandy Cay without significant operational cost. PRC drew from this experience the conclusion that the US-Philippines alliance had been weakened by the failure to escalate, and that further opportunistic action below the threshold of armed conflict was viable.

### 2.2 May – August 2026 Indicators (Graduated 90-Day Escalation)

The following pattern of activity preceded the 22 August 2026 attack and was generally available to JIOC PACOM analysis. The escalation is **graduated** through five identifiable steps — post-HIDDEN-TIDE consolidation, permanent CCG patrol architecture, three “Maritime Police Action” incidents, a public legal-warfare expansion of the MSRPZ, an unprecedented amphibious rehearsal, and finally the 22 August kinetic event.

**Late May 2026 (post-HIDDEN-TIDE consolidation).** Following the 09 May Sandy Cay fait accompli, PRC executed Phase 2 of HIDDEN TIDE: established 24-hour rotating PRC personnel presence at Sandy Cay (~40 personnel by mid-June, claimed as “scientific researchers”); installed semi-permanent shelters and survey instrumentation; assigned 2 CCG cutters to permanent station within 5 nm. ASEAN consensus statement on attribution stalled (Cambodia/Laos non-aligned). PRC academic and MFA publications began arguing administrative-practice precedent. US/PH protests dismissed.

**Early June 2026 (PRC legal architecture).** PRC State Council established “Sansha Maritime Police Authority” — a new enforcement entity organizationally subordinated to the Sansha “City” administration but operationally networked with CCG South Sea Branch and PLA-STC. Mandate explicitly includes enforcement against “unlawful incursions” into the MSRPZ. JIOC PACOM assessed as legal-warfare predicate for graduated escalation. PRC began issuing “administrative warnings” to AFP / PCG vessels operating within the MSRPZ.

**12 June 2026 — Maritime Police Action \#1 (MPA-1).** Two CCG cutters intercepted a Philippine-flagged fishing vessel operating in Sandy Cay waters; vessel boarded; eight Filipino crew briefly detained for “questioning regarding violation of PRC administrative regulations”; released after approximately 14 hours and returned to Palawan. PCG Transparency Initiative published incident video within 6 hours. US Department of State protested. PRC MFA characterized action as “lawful enforcement.”

**8 July 2026 — Maritime Police Action \#2 (MPA-2).** CCG cutter intercepted AFP-chartered resupply vessel resupplying Pag-asa Island; demanded inspection and withdrawal. AFP refused; standoff for approximately 3 hours; CCG cutter eventually withdrew without escalation when BRP *Antonio Luna* (FF-151) repositioned to support. No casualties. JIOC PACOM assessed PRC as testing PCG / AFP response thresholds.

**22 July 2026 — Maritime Police Action \#3 (MPA-3).** CCG cutter rammed AFP-chartered resupply vessel during second resupply attempt to Pag-asa Island; two AFP personnel wounded (non-fatal); resupply vessel sustained hull damage and withdrew to Puerto Princesa. PCG Transparency Initiative published incident video within 4 hours. Philippine government issued strongest protest to date; US State Department issued public condemnation. PRC MFA characterized action as “defensive maneuver in response to unsafe Philippine behavior.”

**Late July 2026 (PRC legal expansion).** On 30 July, PRC State Council issued “Special Administrative Measures for the Marine Scientific Research Protection Zone,” requiring all vessels to obtain PRC permission to enter the MSRPZ; established 50-nautical-mile administrative buffer zones around designated features (including Sandy Cay); declared enforcement authority. US, PH, VN, JP, AU formally protested. JIOC PACOM assessed as significant predicate expansion enabling subsequent enforcement actions against any non-permitted Philippine military or coast guard activity in the AOR.

**28 July – 06 August 2026 (PLA Joint Sword 2026B).** PLA Southern Theater Command commenced “Joint Sword 2026B” exercise. Exercise included unprecedented amphibious rehearsals at Hainan: PLA Marine Corps Brigade-level vertical envelopment and beach assault on a feature configured to resemble a Spratly island; simulated “scientific personnel emergency evacuation and protection” scenario; integrated PLAAF CAP and CCG / PAFMM coordination training. JIOC PACOM noted the rehearsal was qualitatively different from prior exercises but assessed at the time as deterrent signaling rather than direct rehearsal — an assessment revised in retrospect.

**8 August 2026 (MSRPZ expansion).** PRC State Council announced extension of the MSRPZ to include “all features of the central Spratly group, including those administered by other claimants pending bilateral negotiation.” Effectively included Pag-asa Island, Itu Aba (ROC), Spratly Island (VN), and most Vietnamese, Malaysian, and Bruneian features. PRC MFA characterized the expansion as “consolidation of administrative practice consistent with established PRC sovereign rights.” US, PH, VN, MY, JP issued strongest collective protest to date.

**12 August 2026 (administrative ultimatum).** PRC State Council issued amended “Special Administrative Measures” requiring all vessels in the expanded MSRPZ to obtain prior PRC permission, with explicit enforcement authority including boarding, detention, and use of force. AFP and PCG publicly rejected; AFP increased Pag-asa garrison from 30 to approximately 50 personnel; PCG repositioned BRP *Teresa Magbanua* to Pag-asa anchorage.

**14–18 August 2026 (PLA surge).** PLAN South Sea Fleet conducted surge operations including Type 075 *Hainan* amphibious ready group operations from Yulin to vicinity of the Spratlys and back. PLAAF rotational F-16 / J-11B / J-10C presence at Mischief, Subi, and Fiery Cross was observed in continuous CAP rotation rather than the historical episodic pattern. CCG presence around Pag-asa increased from 2 cutters to 5 cutters. PAFMM presence at Mischief grew from baseline 50 vessels to 110 vessels.

**18–21 August 2026 (cyber preparation).** Multiple cyber probing events against PCG WESCOM, Pag-asa relay, and Palawan / Cebu base infrastructure. Attribution to PRC-aligned actor sets (VOLT TYPHOON, SALT TYPHOON descendants). Limited disruption observed; assessed as pre-positioning rather than effects-delivery.

**21 August 2026, 1800L (operational closure notice).** PRC State Council issued an “operational notice to mariners” closing waters within a 50-nautical-mile zone around Pag-asa Island for “scientific protection operations.” Notice was distributed via PRC official channels but not via the IHO/IMO standard mariners’ notice system. PCG and AFP did not comply.

### 2.3 The Pag-asa Action (22 August 2026)

**220330L — 220400L AUG 26.** Coordinated cyber action disrupted Pag-asa relay station, severing AFP / PCG communications between Pag-asa and Western Command (WESCOM) Puerto Princesa. PCG drone surveillance feeds were degraded by jamming.

**220400L AUG 26 (D-Day, H-Hour).** Multi-axis assault commenced:

- **Maritime axis.** Two CCG cutters (CCG-5901 and CCG-6305) conducted ramming and water-cannon engagement of BRP *Teresa Magbanua* at the Pag-asa anchorage. Within minutes, an anti-ship missile (assessed YJ-12B from a PLA Coastal Defense Cruise Missile launcher emplaced at Subi Reef) struck *Teresa Magbanua* amidships. The strike caused a fire forward of midships and progressive flooding; the captain ordered abandon ship at 220414L. Of the 80-person crew, 11 PCG personnel are confirmed killed (8 in the initial blast, 3 from secondary explosions and burns); 17 wounded; 52 recovered by lifeboat and rescued by AFP small-craft. The vessel sank at approximately 220448L. The attack on *Teresa Magbanua* is the discrete kinetic act against a public vessel of the Philippines that is cited as triggering the conditions of MDT Article V.

- **Air assault axis.** Six Z-20 helicopters (PLA Marine Corps configuration) and four Z-10 attack helicopters launched from a Type 075 LHD (assessed *Hainan* hull 31) and conducted vertical envelopment of the Pag-asa airfield. Z-10 fires suppressed AFP defensive positions; Z-20s landed approximately 80 PLAMC personnel adjacent to the runway.

- **Amphibious axis.** Approximately 120 PLAMC personnel landed in ZBL-09 amphibious vehicles from CCG-disguised landing craft on the southern Pag-asa beach.

- **Information warfare.** Pre-staged content depicting “lawful Chinese authorities responding to Filipino aggression” was published on Weibo, X, and TikTok within 30 minutes of the action.

**220500L – 221200L AUG 26.** AFP / PCG garrison (approximately 50 personnel) put up an organized defense, particularly at the runway approach and the southwestern (civilian-area) defensive perimeter. AFP defensive fires inflicted assessed losses on the PLAMC assault force (PRC has not announced casualties; OSINT estimates 8–15 PLAMC killed and 20–30 wounded). After approximately 4 hours of combat, the AFP commander ordered withdrawal of intact elements to Pag-asa Cay 2 via small-craft and to outlying Philippine-administered features; approximately 13 AFP personnel and 1 PCG embarked personnel completed the withdrawal. Final accounting at 221200L: **14 AFP / PCG personnel killed in action ashore** (combat-related); **18 wounded**; **25 captured** (including some non-combat wounded); **13 successfully withdrew to Pag-asa Cay 2**. Combined with the 11 PCG KIA on *Teresa Magbanua*, the total Philippine treaty-ally service-member loss from the PRC action is **25 KIA, 35 wounded, 25 captured** — a deliberate state action against the armed forces and a public vessel of a US treaty ally.

The PRC announced the airfield closure for “scientific protection operations.” The PCG WESCOM communications restoration was achieved by approximately 220900L. The Philippine President was notified by 220915L. The US Embassy Manila reported the situation to State at 221000L.

**221300L AUG 26.** Philippine President invoked Article III consultations.

**221800Z (230200L) AUG 26.** White House National Security Council convened.

**230600Z (231400L) AUG 26.** Presidential public statement: “The deliberate armed attack at Pag-asa Island, an island territory under Philippine jurisdiction in the Pacific, and on the armed forces and a public vessel of the Republic of the Philippines, satisfies the definition of armed attack in Article V of the 1951 Mutual Defense Treaty. The conditions of Article IV are met. The United States will act with our Philippine treaty ally to meet the common dangers in accordance with our constitutional processes.”

## 3. Assessment of PRC Strategic Intent

### 3.1 What PRC Sought to Achieve

JIOC PACOM assesses with HIGH confidence that PRC strategic objectives at Pag-asa included:

1.  **Conversion of administrative precedent into physical occupation.** The May 2026 Sandy Cay fait accompli established the legal-administrative predicate. Pag-asa converts the precedent into physical control of a Philippine-occupied feature that hosts a 1,300-meter airfield — a militarily significant asset.

2.  **Demonstration of military capability.** The action demonstrates PLAMC vertical envelopment under PLAN amphibious lift, executed under PLAAF and Spratly-outpost air-defense umbrella, with integrated CCG / PAFMM cover. This is a doctrinal milestone for the PLA.

3.  **Test of US alliance resolve.** PRC theory of victory anticipated that the US would invoke Article V but would not commit to a full restoration of Philippine territory; the PRC sought to prove the limits of US treaty commitment in the era of competing demands.

4.  **Pre-positioning for Taiwan.** The action establishes a PLA Joint Operations precedent under contested A2/AD conditions that exercises capabilities and creates strategic-narrative ambiguity prior to any future Taiwan-related contingency.

5.  **Strategic narrative.** Provide a “lawful enforcement” narrative for global-south consumption that frames US response as “external interference.”

### 3.2 What PRC Did NOT Seek

JIOC PACOM assesses with MEDIUM confidence that PRC did **not** seek:

- A general war with the United States.

- Direct kinetic action against US Navy or US Air Force forces (the *Teresa Magbanua* sinking was assessed as risk-acceptable because of its PCG civilian-equivalent status, in PRC framing).

- Loss of US-PH alliance commitment to the Spratlys broadly; PRC accepts that US response to Pag-asa will harden alliance posture but assessed that the strategic gain of physical occupation outweighs that cost.

- Action that would force a Taiwan Strait second front; PRC has actively kept Taiwan Strait posture at baseline.

### 3.3 PRC Miscalculation

JIOC PACOM assesses with HIGH confidence that PRC **miscalculated** in two specific ways:

1.  **Casualty calculation.** PRC anticipated approximately 5–10 PCG / AFP killed, consistent with a calibrated kinetic act. The actual death toll (25 KIA across the assault and the *Teresa Magbanua* strike) is meaningfully above PRC’s planned threshold; the deliberate missile strike against a public vessel of a treaty ally is the discrete act that crosses Article V’s definitional line.

2.  **Article IV/V trigger.** PRC anticipated that the US would invoke Article III consultations and conduct demonstrative force-flow, but would stop short of a public determination that the conditions of Article V were met — relying on the perceived ambiguity of “armed attack on island territories” applied to a contested feature. The 23 August Presidential determination that the deliberate attack on Pag-asa Island (an island territory under Philippine jurisdiction in the Pacific) and on the AFP/PCG (armed forces and a public vessel of the Philippines) satisfies the Article V definition — thereby triggering Article IV obligations — closes that ambiguity and changes the strategic frame.

### 3.4 PRC Likely Response to PACIFIC HARRIER

JIOC PACOM assesses three competing hypotheses on PRC response:

**H1 (LIKELY): Calibrated defense with off-ramp.** PRC will reinforce Pag-asa to maximum sustainable level, employ A2/AD posture to deter US closure, but will not initiate kinetic action against US forces. PRC will pursue diplomatic off-ramp through ASEAN-mediated talks, while preserving the physical presence on Pag-asa as long as possible. PRC will seek to convert PACIFIC HARRIER into a prolonged stand-off that erodes US public will.

**H2 (LESS LIKELY): Escalation to demonstrate resolve.** PRC will conduct a calibrated kinetic action against US ISR or forward forces (e.g., a “warning” missile demonstration against an unmanned target near a US CSG) to signal resolve. Probability assessed LOW–MEDIUM.

**H3 (LEAST LIKELY): Vertical escalation to Taiwan or broader.** PRC opens a second front in the Taiwan Strait or against a different US ally. JIOC PACOM assesses LOW given current Taiwan posture and the absence of mobilization indicators.

The MLCOA for PACIFIC HARRIER planning is H1; the MDCOA combines elements of H1 (defense / off-ramp) with the residual risk of H2 (calibrated kinetic).

## 4. Center of Gravity Analysis (Adversary)

### 4.1 PRC Strategic CoG (Pacific Theater)

**Strategic CoG:** Chinese Communist Party legitimacy and the PLA’s role as guarantor of national rejuvenation. CoG sources: domestic political stability; economic performance; perceived rising international status.

**Critical capabilities at strategic level:** ability to project the appearance of inevitability and domestic strength; ability to enforce administrative claims through layered tools; ability to deter US escalation through capability demonstration.

**Critical requirements:** narrative consistency; ASEAN consensus paralysis; deterrence credibility against US.

**Critical vulnerabilities:** credibility cost of visible PLA defeat; cost of prolonged stand-off; economic-cost of sanctions and trade disruption; loss of access to chokepoints (Malacca, Sunda, Lombok, Bashi).

### 4.2 PRC Operational CoG (PACIFIC HARRIER AOR)

**Operational CoG:** The militarized “Big Three” Spratly outposts (Mischief, Subi, Fiery Cross) that provide forward A2/AD coverage, logistics hub, and ISR-fusion node for the entire Spratly central group. The outposts are the source of PRC operational-level strength in the Spratlys.

**Critical capabilities:** integrated air-defense (HQ-9B SAM); anti-ship missile coverage (YJ-12B ASCM); air sortie generation (3 km runways); deep-water harbor for amphibious lift; ISR fusion.

**Critical requirements:** intact runways; intact air-defense; intact comms / sensor architecture; sustained logistic flow from Hainan; preserved force-protection from PRC air and naval cover.

**Critical vulnerabilities:** - Single-point-of-failure logistics (each outpost is supplied by sea from Hainan; sea-line interdiction degrades sustainment over 30–60 days). - Air defense is finite (HQ-9B magazine depth limits sustained engagement). - Runway / harbor are physically vulnerable to precision fires within their mutual support range. - C2 architecture between the outposts and PLAN South Sea Fleet HQ is dependent on satellite, undersea cable, and HF backup; degrading any tier complicates coordination.

The operational CoG analysis suggests that the JFC’s operational approach should focus on **isolation and degradation of the militarized outposts** as the primary mechanism to make Pag-asa untenable for PRC defense, rather than a direct contested approach to Pag-asa itself.

## 5. Most-Likely and Most-Dangerous Adversary COAs (J2 Initial Assessment)

The full COA development belongs to the JFC J2; this is the J2 initial framing for MA.

### 5.1 MLCOA — PRC Calibrated Defense with Off-Ramp

PRC reinforces Pag-asa to a sustainable level (assessed: one PLAMC battalion equivalent + integrated air defense + coastal defense cruise missiles within 14 days). PRC employs Spratly-outpost A2/AD to impose force-protection cost on US closure. PRC engages diplomatically through ASEAN and bilateral channels seeking face-saving off-ramp that preserves Sandy Cay administration in exchange for partial Pag-asa disengagement. PRC accepts prolonged stand-off if necessary; calculates that US public will erodes faster than PRC capacity to sustain.

**Indicators of MLCOA:** continued PRC reinforcement at Pag-asa; PLA Eastern Theater remains at baseline; PRC diplomatic outreach to ASEAN / Indonesia / Brunei; PRC acceptance of US force-flow without kinetic interdiction; PRC information operations emphasizing US “escalation” framing.

### 5.2 MDCOA — Calibrated Kinetic Demonstration

PRC executes a calibrated kinetic action against US ISR or a forward US naval contact to demonstrate cost. Possible vectors: (a) HQ-9B engagement of an unmanned aerial vehicle in the AOR; (b) YJ-12B “warning shot” near a US CSG; (c) submarine-launched cruise missile against a non-US allied target; (d) targeted disruption of submarine cable. PRC frames the action as defensive; calculates that US public will impose escalation off-ramp.

**Indicators of MDCOA:** PRC fire-control radar emissions transition to engagement-mode; PRC submarine forward deployment outside historical patterns; SSN sortie from Yulin; PRC SLOC threat indicators outside the Spratlys; PRC Eastern Theater Rocket Force readiness changes.

### 5.3 Critical Distinction for the JFC

The MDCOA does **not** entail strategic-level escalation (Taiwan, mainland strikes, nuclear posture changes). The JFC must preserve options against the residual MDCOA risk while planning against MLCOA. The principal tools to address MDCOA risk are: dispersed force posture; integrated air and missile defense; submarine awareness; cable / SLOC protection; clear ROE for force protection.

### 6.2 Doctrine

PRC SCS doctrine emphasizes:

- **Counter-intervention:** capability and posture designed to deter US operational closure to mainland China; the SCS A2/AD architecture is the forward layer.

- **Protection of “core interests”:** sovereignty in SCS, Taiwan, Senkakus, Tibet, Xinjiang treated as non-negotiable.

## 1. Apportionment vs. Allocation — Critical Distinction

For Mission Analysis, the JFC must distinguish:

- **Apportioned forces:** the JSCP-based pool of forces from which the CCDR is told he/she may draw for planning. Apportionment is *for planning purposes*; it does not equate to forces in hand.

- **Allocated forces:** the specific forces the SecDef has directed to the CCDR for *execution* of the named operation. Allocation may be more or less than apportionment, conditioned by competing demands.

- **Assigned / attached:** forces under operational control or tactical control of the CCDR; the actually-available combat power.

The PACOM WARNORD (07_USINDOPACOM_WARNORD.md) reflects forces *apportioned for planning* (per JSCP and SecDef letter). The PACFLT (JTF-PACIFIC HARRIER) WARNORD (08_Component_WARNORD.md) will reflect PACFLT’s *allocation decision* against competing demands — and the deltas matter.

**Specific to PACIFIC HARRIER:** USFK forces are NOT apportioned (Korean Peninsula commitment); USEUCOM and USCENTCOM forces are NOT apportioned. First Marine Expeditionary Force (I MEF) is apportioned but PACFLT planners may judge it not required for the initial sea-control phase, mirroring the Borneo case study reasoning.

## 3. Critical Sea Lanes

### 3.1 Strait of Malacca

- **Location:** Between Malaysian peninsular and Sumatra (Indonesia).

- **Length:** ~500 nm.

- **Narrowest point (Phillips Channel):** ~1.5 nm wide.

- **Depth:** 25 m at narrowest; 90 m main channel.

- **Annual transit:** ~94,000 ships; ~25% of global trade by tonnage.

- **Strategic significance:** Principal Indian Ocean → SCS chokepoint for Northeast Asian energy.

- **Disruption risk:** Vulnerable to terrorism, piracy, deliberate state action; closure would cost USD ~2B/day in delayed trade.

- **For PACIFIC HARRIER:** US/coalition must maintain transit; protection of Singapore approach is critical.

### 3.2 Strait of Singapore

- **Location:** Between Singapore and Indonesian islands.

- **Length:** ~65 nm.

- **Critical role:** transition between Malacca and SCS.

- **Singapore base access:** Sembawang / Changi naval; Paya Lebar / Tengah air.

### 3.3 Sunda Strait

- **Location:** Between Java and Sumatra (Indonesia).

- **Width:** ~14 nm narrowest.

- **Depth:** generally shallow; navigation more difficult than Malacca.

- **Significance:** alternate route to Malacca for very large carriers (VLCC) bound for SCS.

### 3.4 Lombok Strait

- **Location:** Between Bali and Lombok (Indonesia).

- **Width:** ~11 nm narrowest.

- **Depth:** Deep (~250 m); preferred by VLCC.

- **Significance:** principal alternate to Malacca for capesize+ vessels; transits via Makassar Strait northward.

### 3.5 Makassar Strait

- **Location:** Between Borneo (Kalimantan) and Sulawesi (Indonesia).

- **Length:** ~600 nm.

- **Depth:** 100–500 m channel.

- **Significance:** principal route from Lombok Strait northward to Philippines and Pacific.

### 3.6 Bashi Channel / Luzon Strait

- **Location:** Between Taiwan (Yu Shan) and northern Luzon (Philippines).

- **Width:** ~135 nm.

- **Depth:** Deep (~3,000 m); excellent SSN transit.

- **Significance:** Principal Western Pacific → SCS passage; critical for PRC SSN/SSBN transit out to deep water and for US forces transiting in.

- **For PACIFIC HARRIER:** USS *George Washington* CSG transits via Bashi inbound from Yokosuka; USS *Carl Vinson* CSG transits from West Coast via Bashi or alternate routes.

### 3.7 Mindoro Strait / Sibutu Passage / Verde Island Passage

- **Mindoro Strait:** north Philippine archipelagic strait; principal east-west route through Philippine archipelago.

- **Sibutu Passage:** southern Philippine archipelagic route; connects Sulu Sea to SCS.

- **Verde Island Passage:** connects South China Sea to internal Philippine waters.

- **Significance:** Internal Philippine archipelagic waters under Philippine sovereign administration; facilitate east-west force flow without exposing to direct PRC SCS A2/AD.

### 3.8 Taiwan Strait

- **Location:** Between Taiwan and PRC mainland.

- **Width:** 70–110 nm.

- **Depth:** Mostly shallow (~50 m); deeper channels 200 m+.

- **Significance:** Principal Northeast Asia → SCS connector; high-traffic sea lane (~70% of Taiwan-bound and PRC-eastern-coast trade).

- **PACIFIC HARRIER excludes Taiwan Strait operations** (per WARNORD).

## 4. Climatology and Oceanography

### 4.1 Seasonal Pattern

**Late Wet Season (Late August — Operations Window):**

- **Monsoon:** Southwest monsoon (until ~early October).

- **Prevailing winds:** Southwest at 15–25 knots; gusts higher in convective weather; wind shifts during typhoon transit.

- **Sea state:** 3–4 typical; can rise to 5–6 in convective or typhoon conditions.

- **Visibility:** 6–10 nm typical; reduced in afternoon thunderstorms (1–3 nm).

- **Precipitation:** Heavy convective rainfall, particularly afternoon / evening; cumulative monsoon rainfall 200–400 mm/month.

### 4.2 Tropical Cyclones (Typhoons)

- **Peak season:** August through October. Pag-asa cluster is in the typhoon track region.

- **Genesis:** Western North Pacific east of the Philippines; tracks generally west / west-northwest.

- **Frequency:** ~25 named storms per year globally; ~7–9 reach Spratly/Philippine area annually.

- **Speed:** Typically 10–15 knots forward; can intensify rapidly under favorable SST.

- **Implications:** May force pause in amphibious / air operations; force-protection posture changes; SAR demand increases.

- **Forecast horizon:** 5–7 days reliable; PACOM Joint Typhoon Warning Center (JTWC) at Pearl Harbor provides theater forecasts.

### 4.3 Sea-State Considerations

- **Amphibious operations:** Sea state ≤3 desired; ≤2 for LCAC operations.

- **Air operations:** F-35B / MV-22 LHA operations remain feasible to sea state 5; degraded above.

- **Submarine operations:** Generally insensitive to surface conditions.

### 4.4 Ocean Currents and Bathymetry

- **South China Sea Throughflow:** Net flow from northeast to southwest in summer; reverses in winter monsoon.

- **Kuroshio Current:** flows northward east of Taiwan; influences Bashi Channel.

- **Bathymetry:** Spratly central group sits on shallow bank; surrounded by deep water (3,000–5,000 m).

## Slide 6 — Domain Analysis: Land

## Slide 11 — Pag-asa Island OAKOC Analysis

**Avenues of Approach.** Beach gradient is gradual; suitable for LCAC, amphibious vehicle, and helicopter landing. Northern, eastern, and southern beaches are all viable. Western shore has the runway and most existing infrastructure.

**Key Terrain.** The 1,300m runway (Rancudo Airfield) is the principal key terrain — control of the runway determines whether the feature can be contested by air. The civilian-occupied southwestern quarter is the principal humanitarian-key terrain. The eastern shore (high tide) is the principal reverse-slope position.

**Obstacles.** Pre-emplaced PRC defensive obstacles assessed as: barbed wire perimeter; hasty fighting positions; possible IEDs; HJ-10 / HJ-12 ATGM positions overwatching beaches; HQ-17 SHORAD (assessed); YJ-12B mobile launchers (assessed concealed). Civilian buildings impose proportionality constraint.

**Cover and Concealment.** Limited natural cover; civilian buildings provide concealment and complicate engagement. Pioneer vegetation provides marginal concealment. Sea breezes and afternoon convection provide intermittent visual cover.

## Slide 17 — Adversary MLCOA: Calibrated Defense with Off-Ramp

PRC accepts that US/coalition can establish sea control but assesses that US public will erode faster than PRC capacity to sustain.

## Slide 18 — Adversary MDCOA: Calibrated Kinetic Demonstration

The most-dangerous PRC course of action is a calibrated kinetic demonstration designed to test US public will.

**Possible vectors:**

**Vector 1: ASCM warning shot.** YJ-12B from Subi / Fiery Cross against an unmanned aerial target near a US CSG. PRC frame as “defensive measure”; calculates that US public will impose escalation off-ramp.

**Vector 2: Surface engagement.** A PRC submarine engages a US/coalition surface combatant at the edge of the JOA; PRC denies attribution (using older / non-PRC-designated equipment).

**Vector 3: SLOC / cable interdiction.** Targeted disruption of a key submarine cable in the SCS to impose communications cost; PRC denies attribution.

**Vector 4: Allied target.** PRC engages an Australian or Japanese vessel rather than US; calculates that allied response is more constrained.

**Indicators of MDCOA:** - HQ-9B / Type 366 emissions transition from test-mode to engagement-mode. - PRC SSN forward deployment outside historical patterns. - Submarine cable-laying / repair vessel anomalies. - PLA Rocket Force readiness changes outside historical patterns. - PRC Eastern Theater repositioning toward Bashi Channel.

**Implications.** MDCOA does NOT entail strategic-level escalation (Taiwan, mainland strikes, nuclear). The JFC must hedge through dispersed force posture, IAMD, submarine awareness, cable / SLOC protection, and clear ROE for force protection.

## 1. Purpose

This Commander’s Initial Planning Guidance (CIPG) provides my initial guidance to the Joint Task Force – PACIFIC HARRIER (JTF-PACIFIC HARRIER) and the USINDOPACOM J5 Planning Team for the conduct of Mission Analysis and subsequent Joint Planning Process (JPP) activities. I expect the JTF Planning Team to refine and challenge this guidance as Mission Analysis develops. Where this CIPG and the WARNORD diverge, the WARNORD governs as the formal planning directive; this CIPG is my framing.

## 2. Situation

The People’s Republic of China has conducted a deliberate armed attack on the Republic of the Philippines — on the island of Pag-asa (an island territory under Philippine jurisdiction in the Pacific) and on Armed Forces of the Philippines / Philippine Coast Guard personnel and on a public vessel of the Philippines (BRP *Teresa Magbanua*) at the Pag-asa anchorage. The conditions of MDT Article V are satisfied. The President has so determined; the SecDef has directed USINDOPACOM to plan and prepare to conduct multinational operations to restore Philippine territorial integrity.

The full situation summary is at Reference (f). The graduated escalation through May–August 2026 (post-HIDDEN-TIDE consolidation, three Maritime Police Actions, MSRPZ expansion, PLA Joint Sword 2026B rehearsal, surge operations) is the proximate context.

I assess the PRC strategic objective is to consolidate effective control of the Spratly central group while preserving an off-ramp consistent with PRC strategic interests. The most likely PRC course of action is calibrated defense with diplomatic off-ramp; the most dangerous PRC course of action is a calibrated kinetic demonstration against US or allied forces, not strategic escalation.

## 3. Operational Design

### 3.1 Problem Statement

**How does the United States and its coalition partners restore Philippine territorial integrity over the Pag-asa cluster of the Spratly Islands, deter further People’s Republic of China armed action in the South China Sea, preserve the credibility of US Indo-Pacific alliances and the rule-based international order, and create conditions for diplomatic termination — while operating against a sophisticated PRC anti-access / area-denial architecture in close proximity to PRC mainland sanctuary, in coalition with treaty allies and partner states whose force contributions are conditioned on national political authorizations, in concurrent management of competing strategic demands on the Korean Peninsula and standing Taiwan Strait deterrent posture, and within explicit strategic restraints prohibiting offensive operations against PRC mainland territory and infrastructure absent Presidential release?**

### 3.2 Operational Approach

I direct the planning team to consider an operational approach organized around four lines of effort (LOE) executed concurrently and sequenced through five phases:

**Line of Effort 1 — Sea Control and Air Superiority in the JOA.** Establish maritime and air-domain advantage within the JOA sufficient to permit coalition force flow, force protection, and amphibious operations. This LOE is the prerequisite for the others.

**Line of Effort 2 — Isolation and Degradation of the PRC Militarized Spratly Outposts.** Make the PRC’s forward A2/AD architecture (Mischief, Subi, Fiery Cross) sustained logistic flow untenable; degrade outpost air defense and anti-ship capability to a level that permits coalition operations within their nominal engagement envelope. Decisive points are: (a) interdiction of sea-line resupply from Hainan, (b) attrition of HQ-9B / YJ-12B magazine depth, (c) degradation of outpost C2 architecture.

**Line of Effort 3 — Restoration of Philippine Sovereignty at Pag-asa.** Conduct controlled amphibious restoration of Pag-asa Island with minimum Philippine civilian casualties, preservation of Philippine sovereignty, and rapid transition to Philippine-led administration. This LOE is the operation’s defining objective and the political center of gravity.

**Line of Effort 4 — Diplomatic Conditions for Termination.** Through information operations, allied coordination, strategic communications, and operational tempo, create the political conditions for PRC acceptance of an off-ramp consistent with restored Philippine sovereignty and US strategic interests.

The five phases are described in detail in Reference (a) Phase III (Shaping, Sea Control, Restoration, Stabilization, Transition).

### 3.3 Centers of Gravity (Initial Assessment — to be refined by J2/J5)

- **Adversary strategic CoG.** CCP regime legitimacy and PLA role as guarantor of national rejuvenation. Critical vulnerabilities: credibility cost of visible defeat; sanctions and trade-disruption costs; loss of access to Indo-Pacific chokepoints.

- **Adversary operational CoG.** Militarized Spratly outposts (Mischief, Subi, Fiery Cross). Critical vulnerabilities: single-point-of-failure logistics; finite air-defense magazine; physical vulnerability of runways and harbors; multi-tier C2 architecture.

- **Friendly strategic CoG.** Alliance credibility; coalition cohesion; rule-based international order; sustained US public commitment.

- **Friendly operational CoG.** Coalition sea-control and air-superiority capability. Critical vulnerabilities: tanker / SPOD / APOD throughput; weather window; force-protection cost in A2/AD; competing demands (Korea, Taiwan).

### 3.4 Decisive Points

I direct the planning team to consider the following candidate decisive points for inclusion in the operational approach:

1.  **Pag-asa Cay 2 (Philippine-held).** Stable forward foothold for amphibious approach and civilian evacuation; established intelligence-fusion node.

2.  **Sea-line interdiction zone (Hainan – Spratly).** Choke point for sustaining PRC Spratly outposts; principal high-payoff submarine operating area.

3.  **Bashi Channel / Luzon Strait.** Forward CSG presence point and interdiction node for PRC SSN / SAG ingress / egress.

4.  **Antonio Bautista AB (Palawan EDCA site).** Forward F-22 / F-35 / tanker operating base; enables continuous CAP over the JOA.

5.  **Subi Reef HQ-9B / YJ-12B emplacements.** Adversary operational CoG component; physical degradation makes Pag-asa militarily defensible from outside.

The planning team should identify additional decisive points and develop them in the COA matrix.

### 3.5 Termination Criteria / Military End State

**Military End State (initial framing — to be refined by JTF):**

1.  PRC armed presence eliminated from Pag-asa Island and the Pag-asa cluster.

2.  Philippine sovereign administration restored at Pag-asa and reinforced across the Kalayaan Island Group.

3.  PRC forces returned to status quo ante May 2026 positions, or as negotiated by State.

4.  Coalition forces postured for sustained Philippine partnership; long-term EDCA posture preserved.

5.  Detained AFP / PCG personnel repatriated.

6.  US and allied citizens accounted for.

7.  Coalition force posture sufficient to deter near-term PRC re-escalation; preserved INDOPACOM capacity for Korean Peninsula and Taiwan Strait standing missions.

8.  Information / diplomatic conditions favorable to durable rule-of-law SCS narrative.

**Termination Criteria (the observable conditions that, if met, indicate End State achievement):**

1.  Zero PRC armed personnel verifiable on Pag-asa Island or within Pag-asa Cluster for ≥7 consecutive days.

2.  PCG / AFP routine presence operations resumed at Pag-asa airfield (one C-130 cycle / week minimum).

3.  Philippine President and SecState public statement of restored sovereignty.

4.  All detained AFP / PCG personnel released via ICRC.

5.  Zero kinetic incidents against US, allied, or Philippine forces for ≥14 consecutive days.

6.  Diplomatic agreement (whether ASEAN-mediated or bilateral) establishing PRC withdrawal commitment.

The planning team should propose specific measurable termination criteria for each Military End State element and incorporate them into the COA evaluation matrix.

5. (E) MISSION. EFFECTIVE ON RECEIPT OF THIS ORDER, USINDOPACOM (SUPPORTED) EXECUTES OPERATION PACIFIC HARRIER UNDER COA-2 (SHAPING-AND-NEGOTIATE) TO IMPOSE COST ON PRC AGGRESSION THROUGH CONTINUOUS ISR, FREEDOM-OF-NAVIGATION OPERATIONS, COALITION MARITIME AND AIR PRESENCE, AND ECONOMIC PRESSURE; TO DEMONSTRATE CREDIBLE COALITION CAPABILITY SUFFICIENT TO COMPEL PRC ACCEPTANCE OF A DIPLOMATIC OFF-RAMP RESTORING PHILIPPINE TERRITORIAL INTEGRITY; AND TO PRESERVE THE FORWARD POSTURE, OPTIONS, AND READINESS NECESSARY TO TRANSITION TO COA-1 (ISOLATE-AND-RESTORE) UPON DECISION BY THE PRESIDENT IF NEGOTIATION FAILS — IN ORDER TO RESTORE PHILIPPINE SOVEREIGNTY OVER THE PAG-ASA CLUSTER, DEFEND THE PHILIPPINES FROM ADDITIONAL ARMED ATTACK, DETER FURTHER PRC AGGRESSION IN THE SOUTH CHINA SEA, AND REINFORCE THE CREDIBILITY OF US INDO-PACIFIC ALLIANCES, CONSISTENT WITH ARTICLE IV OBLIGATIONS UNDER THE 1951 US-PHILIPPINES MUTUAL DEFENSE TREATY.\

6. (E) COMMANDER'S INTENT (CHAIRMAN). MY INTENT IS THAT WE PROSECUTE THIS OPERATION AS A DELIBERATE, DISCIPLINED COALITION CAMPAIGN OF PRESSURE — NOT A WAR OF ANNIHILATION — DESIGNED TO PRODUCE A NEGOTIATED RESTORATION OF PHILIPPINE SOVEREIGNTY ON FAVORABLE TERMS WHILE PRESERVING OUR CAPACITY TO ESCALATE IF NEGOTIATION FAILS. EVERY DEPLOYMENT, EVERY FONOP, EVERY ISR LINE, EVERY COALITION PORT CALL IS AN INSTRUMENT OF THE DIPLOMATIC EFFORT, NOT A SUBSTITUTE FOR IT. WE WILL OPERATE AT A TEMPO THAT IS UNMISTAKABLY SERIOUS BUT THAT LEAVES THE PRC A FACE-SAVING EXIT. WE WILL NOT EXPAND THE CONFLICT BEYOND THE JOINT OPERATIONS AREA. WE WILL NOT TARGET THE PRC MAINLAND. WE WILL NOT BE THE FIRST TO ESCALATE INTO ATTRITION. WE WILL HOLD THE AMPHIBIOUS RESTORATION OPTION VISIBLE, READY, AND CREDIBLE — AND WE WILL EXECUTE IT IF AND ONLY IF DIRECTED BY THE PRESIDENT. THE END STATE IS RESTORED PHILIPPINE TERRITORIAL INTEGRITY OVER THE PAG-ASA CLUSTER, REINFORCED ALLIANCE CREDIBILITY, PRESERVED INDOPACOM POSTURE FOR CONTINUED KOREAN PENINSULA AND TAIWAN STRAIT DETERRENCE, AND A REGIONAL ORDER IN WHICH PRC RECOGNIZES THAT KINETIC SEIZURE OF DISPUTED FEATURES IS NOT A SUSTAINABLE INSTRUMENT OF POLICY.\
\
7. (E) CONCEPT OF OPERATIONS.\
\
A. (E) APPROVED COA. COA-2 (SHAPING-AND-NEGOTIATE) IS APPROVED AS THE PRIMARY COURSE OF ACTION. COA-1 (ISOLATE-AND-RESTORE) IS APPROVED AS THE ON-ORDER BRANCH UPON FAILURE OF NEGOTIATION. USINDOPACOM WILL MAINTAIN COA-1 AT A LEVEL OF READINESS SUCH THAT TRANSITION FROM COA-2 TO COA-1 IS EXECUTABLE WITHIN 96 HOURS OF DIRECTION.\

CONTINUED USFK PENINSULAR DETERRENCE AND TAIWAN STRAIT STANDING DETERRENT POSTURE REMAIN INDOPACOM PRIORITY-OF-EFFORT TASKS NOT TO BE DEGRADED IN SUPPORT OF PACIFIC HARRIER.\

\
(1) (E) PHASE I — SHAPING AND POSTURE (ONGOING; CULMINATES D-DAY +14). RAPID FORCE CLOSURE; ESTABLISHMENT OF COALITION C2; ACTIVATION OF PHILIPPINE EDCA SITES; ESTABLISHMENT OF AIR BRIDGE TO ANTONIO BAUTISTA AB AND CLARK AB; ESTABLISHMENT OF MARITIME AND AIR PRESENCE PATTERN; INITIATION OF FONOP CADENCE.\

\
(3) (E) PHASE III — DECISION (D+60 TO D+90). ASSESSMENT OF DIPLOMATIC PROGRESS AGAINST TERMINATION CRITERIA; CCDR-LED RECOMMENDATION TO SECDEF AND CJCS NLT D+75 ON ONE OF: (A) CONTINUE COA-2; (B) TRANSITION TO COA-1; (C) INITIATE PHASE IV. AMPLIFICATION OF PRESSURE INSTRUMENTS AS DIRECTED.\

(4) (E) PHASE IV — STABILIZATION (D+90 ONWARD, AS DIRECTED). UPON ACHIEVEMENT OF NEGOTIATED OR COMPELLED PRC WITHDRAWAL, COALITION SUPPORT TO PHILIPPINE RE-OCCUPATION OF PAG-ASA; AFP/PCG REPATRIATION VIA ICRC; UNCLASSIFIED VERIFICATION REGIME; SUSTAINED COALITION DETERRENT PRESENCE.\

(5) (E) PHASE V — TRANSITION (CONDITIONS-BASED). REDEPLOYMENT OF COALITION SURGE FORCES; RESTORATION OF STANDING INDOPACOM POSTURE; PRESERVATION OF LONG-TERM EDCA POSTURE; TRANSITION TO STEADY-STATE COMPETITION.\

(1) (E) LOE 1 — SEA CONTROL AND AIR PRESENCE IN THE JOA. LIMITED TO ESTABLISHMENT OF SEA AND AIR CONTROL SUFFICIENT TO ENABLE PRESENCE AND PROTECT COALITION FORCES; KINETIC DEGRADATION OF PRC FORCES NOT AUTHORIZED EXCEPT IN SELF-DEFENSE OR AGAINST DECLARED HOSTILE FORCES ON PAG-ASA.\
